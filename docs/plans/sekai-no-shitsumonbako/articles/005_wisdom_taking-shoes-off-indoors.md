# Q. What do people get wrong about taking shoes off indoors in Japan?｜Japanese Everyday Wisdom #5

- Shelf: `wisdom` ／ URL: `/en/wisdom/taking-shoes-off-indoors` ／ Status: **Draft — sources unverified**

Almost every visitor knows shoes come off at the door. Fewer notice that the habit shapes how the whole home is built, sized and cleaned.

## A. The common misunderstanding is that this is only about politeness. It is mostly about where dirt is allowed to stop — and it makes more sense once you know how large the average home is.

The entrance is a boundary. Everything beyond it is a surface people sit, sleep and put food on.

## The number that explains it｜90.86 m²

Japan's 2023 Housing and Land Survey puts the **average floor area of a dwelling at 90.86 m²**, with **4.26 rooms** and **14.65 tatami-mat equivalents of living space per person**. In a home that size, the floor is not just walked on. It is where a futon goes at night and where a low table sits at mealtimes.

**When the floor is furniture, the floor has to stay clean.** The shoe rule follows from that.

## Misunderstanding 1｜It is a rule about respect alone

Removing shoes keeps street dirt at the door. **It is a hygiene system before it is a courtesy.**

## Misunderstanding 2｜The entrance is just a hallway

The *genkan* is usually a step lower than the rest of the home. That step is the line: shoes below it, socks above it. **The height difference is doing the work, not a sign on the wall.** Even modern flats with no tatami keep some version of it.

## Misunderstanding 3｜It is a recent custom

Picture scrolls from the Heian period (794–1185) already show footwear being removed before entering residences. **The habit predates most of the architecture around it.**

## Misunderstanding 4｜Any indoor footwear is fine anywhere

Many homes keep separate slippers for the toilet. Wearing them back into the living space defeats the purpose. **If a different pair is waiting at a door, it is waiting there for a reason.**

## Misunderstanding 5｜It only applies to homes

Some clinics, schools, traditional restaurants, temples and fitting rooms follow the same rule. **Watch what the people ahead of you do at the entrance.**
