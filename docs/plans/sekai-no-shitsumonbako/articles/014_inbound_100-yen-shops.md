# Q. What do people get wrong about 100-yen shops in Japan?｜Japan Questions #14

- Shelf: `inbound` ／ URL: `/en/questions/100-yen-shops` ／ Status: **Draft — sources unverified**

These shops appear on almost every visitor's list, usually described as somewhere cheap. The numbers say something more interesting.

## A. The common misunderstanding is that the point is the price. This is a market worth over ¥1.1 trillion that grew by moving *away* from ¥100.

## The market, in numbers

Teikoku Databank's industry survey estimates the domestic 100-yen shop market at **about ¥1.11 trillion for fiscal 2025 — the third consecutive year above ¥1 trillion.** The four largest chains operate roughly **9,400 stores**, up more than 200 on the year and **1.4 times the count of a decade ago.**

The driver, according to the same report, is the **¥150–¥500 price tier**: DIY tools, outdoor goods, functional housework items, beauty tools, stationery and craft supplies. **Growth came from specificity and quality, not from staying at ¥100.**

> **[Verify]** Figures are from Teikoku Databank's fiscal-2025 outlook (published April 2026). Confirm before publication.

## Misunderstanding 1｜Everything costs ¥100

Most shops now carry ¥300 and ¥500 lines alongside the standard range. **Check the tag rather than assuming.**

## Misunderstanding 2｜The price on the shelf is what you pay

Since April 2021 shelf tags must show the tax-inclusive price, so a '¥100' item is labelled ¥110 (10% tax; 8% on food). **The '100' in the name is a nominal price - you pay ¥110 or more at the till.**

## Misunderstanding 3｜It is the same product everywhere

Ranges differ by chain and branch, and lines rotate quickly. **An item you saw once may not be there next time.**

## Misunderstanding 4｜Buy first, work out the use later

The items worth carrying home solve one specific annoyance you already have. **Ask whether it solves something at home, not whether it is cheap.**

## Misunderstanding 5｜Anything can go in your luggage

Knives and blades cannot travel in hand luggage (small round-tipped scissors with blades under 6 cm are usually allowed), and liquids are restricted. **Check your airline's rules, and your own country's import rules, before buying.**
