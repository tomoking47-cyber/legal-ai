# Q. What are the common misunderstandings about storing vegetables?｜Japan Questions #53

- Shelf: `trend` ／ URL: `/en/trend/storing-vegetables` ／ Status: **Draft — English edition, sources unverified**

Putting every vegetable straight into the fridge is a habit almost everywhere. But some vegetables are damaged at the temperature of the main fridge compartment (2–5°C). Japanese fridges usually have a separate vegetable drawer (野菜室, yasai-shitsu) for exactly this reason, and it helps to know which vegetables belong there — or outside the fridge altogether.

## A. The biggest misunderstanding is "all vegetables go in the fridge". Vegetables that originate in warm regions suffer "chilling injury" — damage to their cells caused by low temperature.

## Vegetables prone to chilling injury

| Vegetable | Threshold | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| **Sweet potato (さつまいも, satsumaimo)** | below **10°C** | Cells inside break down; rot or internal browning |
| **Cucumber** | below **5°C** | Pitting (small sunken spots) on the surface. Ideal range is 10–15°C |
| **Aubergine / eggplant (なす, nasu)** | sensitive to cold | Can be kept at room temperature. **Vulnerable to drying out** — wrap each one individually |
| **Tomato** | if over-chilled | Aroma compounds are lost and the flavour declines |

**A temperature close to the one the vegetable grew in** is the guide for storage. This is why the vegetable drawer in a Japanese fridge is set warmer than the main compartment (about 3–8°C).

> **[Verify]** The temperature guides are based on agriculture and food information sites. **Check them against materials from the Ministry of Agriculture, Forestry and Fisheries (MAFF) or NARO before publication.**

## Misunderstanding 1｜The colder, the longer it keeps

As above, **some vegetables are damaged by cold.**

## Misunderstanding 2｜Leaving them in the bag is fine

Condensation left on the surface speeds up spoiling. **Dry off any moisture and wrap them before storing.**

## Misunderstanding 3｜How you place them makes no difference

It is sometimes described that vegetables grown underground keep better lying down, and vegetables grown upright keep better standing up. **Some change simply from being stood upright.**

## Misunderstanding 4｜Everything can go together in one place

Some fruit, such as apples, is known to give off a gas (ethylene) that speeds up the ripening of vegetables around it. **Store them separately.**

## Misunderstanding 5｜Cutting before storing saves time

Spoiling starts from the cut surface. **Cutting just before use keeps them longer in the end.**
