# Q. What do people get wrong about dashi in Japan?｜Japanese Everyday Wisdom #55

- Shelf: `wisdom` ／ URL: `/en/wisdom/dashi` ／ Status: **Draft — sources unverified**

Dashi is often described abroad as a stock. The word fits, and it also misleads — because the point of dashi is a chemical effect, not a long simmer.

## A. The common misunderstanding is that dashi is simmered like a stock. Its core is combining two kinds of umami, which together taste roughly seven to eight times stronger than either alone.

## The synergy

Kelp (kombu) contributes **glutamate**. Dried bonito flakes (katsuobushi) contribute **inosinate**. Combined, the perceived umami is not additive — it is reported as **about 7–8 times** the strength of either alone. This is known as **umami synergy**, and classic first dashi (*ichiban dashi*) is built on it.

A Western stock is cooked for hours. **Dashi is often a matter of minutes, because the goal is extraction of two compounds, not reduction.**

> **[Verify]** The 7–8× figure is cited by the Umami Information Center and related bodies. Confirm before publication.

## Misunderstanding 1｜Longer cooking means more flavour

Kelp held at a rolling boil turns slippery and bitter; bonito flakes lose aroma if simmered. **Each ingredient has its own handling, and neither wants a long boil.**

## Misunderstanding 2｜It requires equipment

Kelp left in cold water is a recognised method (*mizudashi*). **No heat, no special tools.**

## Misunderstanding 3｜Kelp alone or bonito alone is the same

Either makes a dashi. **The synergy only happens when both are present.** That is the reason for combining them.

## Misunderstanding 4｜Instant dashi is a shortcut people hide

Powdered and liquid dashi are ordinary in Japanese kitchens, and umami seasonings use the same glutamate. **Knowing what is in what you use matters more than which you use.**

## Misunderstanding 5｜It only belongs in Japanese food

Savoury depth is not cuisine-specific. **Glutamate and inosinate work in any pot.**
