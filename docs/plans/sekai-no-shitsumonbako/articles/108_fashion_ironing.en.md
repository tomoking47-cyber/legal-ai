# Q. What are the common misunderstandings about ironing?｜Japan Questions #108

- Shelf: `fashion` ／ URL: `/en/fashion/ironing` ／ Status: **Draft — English edition, sources unverified**

Iron temperature settings prompt the same question everywhere: what do the dots on the label mean? Japan's care labels were aligned with the international standard in 2016, so the number of dots shows the maximum temperature, just as it does in Europe. Irons sold in Japan, however, may still carry the old "low / medium / high" (低・中・高) wording on the dial.

## A. The biggest misunderstanding is "hotter is faster and gives a better finish". The maximum temperature is fixed by the label, and going above it melts or glazes the fabric. What decides the finish is moisture and how you apply pressure, more than heat.

## Reading the iron symbol (JIS L0001)

| Symbol | Maximum soleplate temperature | Typical fabrics |
|---|---|---|
| Iron with one dot | **110°C** | Acrylic, nylon, polyurethane |
| Iron with two dots | **150°C** | Polyester, wool, silk |
| Iron with three dots | **200°C** | Cotton, linen |
| Iron crossed out | Do not iron | — |

Under the old symbols (JIS L0217), the setting was shown in words as "low / medium / high" (低・中・高), and the maximum temperatures differ slightly from the current ones. **For garments made from December 2016 onwards, read the dots.**

> **[Verify]** Check the temperature classes in the table on the Consumer Affairs Agency's "Care labelling" page.

## Misunderstanding 1｜Steam is fine on anything

**Silk and rayon can be left with water marks.** Even if the label does not say "no steam", test on a hidden spot first.

## Misunderstanding 2｜Shine is from over-ironing and cannot be undone

Shine on black or navy wool and polyester is **flattened fibres reflecting light**. If it is mild, **holding the iron above the fabric with steam and then brushing the fibres upright** can make it less noticeable. Severe shine will not come back.

## Misunderstanding 3｜Iron only when the garment is dry

Creases in cotton and linen **come out more easily when the fabric is damp.** Ironing while still slightly damp, or using a spray bottle, is the method widely shared among people living in Japan.

## Misunderstanding 4｜Iron straight over prints and embroidery

**Heat-transfer prints melt.** Iron from the reverse side, or use a pressing cloth (当て布, atenuno).

## Misunderstanding 5｜Put the iron away as soon as you finish

**Most burns and fires from irons involve an iron left unattended while still hot.** The National Institute of Technology and Evaluation (NITE) warns of accidents in the period when the soleplate is still hot after the power is switched off. Let it cool before storing.
