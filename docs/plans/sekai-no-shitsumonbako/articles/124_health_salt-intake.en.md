# Q. What are the common misunderstandings about salt intake?｜Japan Questions #124

- Shelf: `health` ／ URL: `/en/health/salt-intake` ／ Status: **Draft — English edition, sources unverified**

Cutting salt is a challenge everywhere, but Japan is one of the higher-salt countries by international standards. Soy sauce, miso and pickles are central to the food culture, so people here have built up a stock of ideas about where to cut first. If you have moved to Japan and started eating Japanese food daily, this article is for you.

## A. The biggest misunderstanding is that not adding salt at the table means a low-salt diet. Most of the salt in a Japanese diet comes from seasonings and processed food. Table salt is only a small part. The first step is learning to read "salt equivalent" (食塩相当量, shokuen sōtō-ryō) on food labels.

## In numbers

| Item | Figure | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Average daily salt intake in Japan (age 20 and over) | **About 9.8 g** (men 10.7 g, women 9.1 g) | National Health and Nutrition Survey (2023) |
| Target in the Dietary Reference Intakes for Japanese (2025 edition) | **Under 7.5 g for men, under 6.5 g for women** | MHLW |
| Health Japan 21 (third term) target | **Under 7 g** | MHLW |
| WHO recommendation | **Under 5 g** | WHO |

Under the **Food Labelling Act** (食品表示法), processed food must show **"salt equivalent"** (食塩相当量) on the nutrition label (fully in force since April 2020). The conversion is **sodium (mg) × 2.54 ÷ 1000 = salt equivalent (g)**.

| Common seasoning or food | Approximate salt equivalent |
|---|---|
| Soy sauce, 1 tablespoon (18 g) | About 2.6 g |
| Miso, 1 tablespoon (18 g) | About 2.2 g |
| Umeboshi (pickled plum), 1 piece (10 g) | About 2.0 g |
| Instant cup noodles, 1 serving | About 5–6 g |
| Sliced white bread (shokupan), 1 slice from a 6-slice loaf | About 0.7 g |

> **[Verify]** Check intake against the latest year of the National Health and Nutrition Survey, and the targets against the "Dietary Reference Intakes for Japanese (2025 edition)". The food figures are rough estimates based on the Standard Tables of Food Composition in Japan (8th revised edition).

## Misunderstanding 1｜Japanese food is healthy, so salt is not a problem

Salt is precisely the weak point of Japanese food. It is concentrated in **soups, pickles, dried fish and fish-paste products (kamaboko, chikuwa etc.)**. Limiting soup to one bowl a day and keeping pickles to a small portion are the standard salt-cutting habits among people living in Japan.

## Misunderstanding 2｜With reduced-salt soy sauce you can use as much as you like

Reduced-salt soy sauce (減塩しょうゆ, gen'en shōyu) still contains **roughly half the salt** of the regular kind. Use twice as much and you are back where you started.

## Misunderstanding 3｜If you sweat, load up on salt

Heavy sweating does call for replacement. But **in ordinary daily life and light exercise, food usually provides enough**, according to the general guidance. **Drinking sports drinks as a habit also needs care because of the sugar.**

## Misunderstanding 4｜Using dashi stock automatically cuts salt

The **umami in dashi supplements saltiness and makes it easier to use less soy sauce and miso.** But **many granulated dashi products contain salt**, so check the label.

## Misunderstanding 5｜"Sodium" and "salt equivalent" on a label are the same number

**400 mg of sodium is roughly 1 g of salt equivalent.** Mix up the units and the figure looks a quarter of its real size. Labels in Japan now show salt equivalent as the rule.
