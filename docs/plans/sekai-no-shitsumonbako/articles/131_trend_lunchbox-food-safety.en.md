# Q. What are the common misunderstandings about keeping a packed lunch (bento) safe?｜Japan Questions #131

- Shelf: `trend` ／ URL: `/en/trend/lunchbox-food-safety` ／ Status: **Draft — English edition, sources unverified**

Packed lunches exist in every culture, but the Japanese bento is made to be carried for several hours at room temperature, and a body of hygiene know-how has grown up around it. That know-how largely matches the food-poisoning prevention principles of the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare (MHLW). If you pack lunches for school or work through Japan's hot, humid summer, this is what matters.

## A. The biggest misunderstanding is "if I make it in the morning, it is safe until lunchtime". Bacteria multiply between 10 and 60°C, and fastest at 30–40°C. A classroom or a parked car in summer reaches those temperatures within a few hours. The basic approach is to apply Japan's three food-hygiene principles — "don't contaminate, don't let it multiply, kill it" (tsukenai, fuyasanai, yattsukeru) — to the lunchbox.

## By the numbers

- The MHLW sums up food-poisoning prevention in three principles: **"don't contaminate, don't let it multiply, kill it"**.
- Most bacteria **multiply slowly below 10°C and stop below −15°C**, and are generally described as killed by **heating to a core temperature of 75°C for at least 1 minute** (norovirus: 85–90°C for at least 90 seconds).
- In 2023 (Reiwa 5) Japan recorded **1,021 food-poisoning incidents with 11,803 patients** (MHLW food-poisoning statistics). Restaurants are the most common source, but **homes account for around 100 incidents every year**.

| Lunchbox practice | Principle |
|---|---|
| Wash your hands; fill the box with clean chopsticks (do not shape food with bare hands) | Don't contaminate |
| Cool everything completely before closing the lid | Don't let it multiply (steam turns to water, which feeds bacteria) |
| Ice pack (保冷剤, horeizai) and insulated bag; keep it in the shade | Don't let it multiply |
| Heat side dishes right through to the centre; reheat yesterday's leftovers | Kill it |
| Drain off liquids; use dividers | Don't contaminate / don't let it multiply |
| Umeboshi (salted plum), vinegared rice, ginger, wasabi | Support only (**they do not protect the lunch on their own**) |

## Misunderstanding 1｜An umeboshi in the rice stops it going off

An umeboshi (salted pickled plum) may have a slight inhibiting effect on the rice right next to it, but **it cannot protect the whole lunchbox.** It survives as a piece of everyday Japanese wisdom, but the official view is that **it is no substitute for cooling and thorough heating**.

## Misunderstanding 2｜Onigiri (rice balls) should be shaped with bare hands

Hands can carry **Staphylococcus aureus**, and the toxin this bacterium produces is generally described as hard to destroy even by heating. **Shaping the rice ball through plastic wrap** is now the standard method.

## Misunderstanding 3｜Raw vegetables add colour

They add moisture too. **Remove the stalks from cherry tomatoes and wash them; drain lettuce completely** — the aim is to leave no free water. In summer many people switch to **cooked vegetables** instead.

## Misunderstanding 4｜Packing yesterday's leftovers is sensible

Only if you **reheat them until hot in the centre, then cool them** before packing. Do not put them into the box straight from the fridge.

## Misunderstanding 5｜With an ice pack, direct sunlight is fine

An ice pack works for **a few hours**. **Inside a car or a locker in direct sun the temperature can pass 50°C**, and no ice pack keeps up. **Where you leave the lunchbox** is part of the plan.
