# Q. What are the common misunderstandings about Japanese pickles (tsukemono)?｜Japan Questions #137

- Shelf: `trend` ／ URL: `/en/trend/pickles` ／ Status: **Draft — English edition, sources unverified**

Pickles are a preserved food in every culture, but Japan has an unusually wide range, and the hygiene thinking behind lightly salted "asazuke" and fully cured "honzuke" pickles is completely different. A fatal outbreak of food poisoning from asazuke in 2012 changed the rules. Here is what a newcomer should know before buying or making them.

## A. The biggest misunderstanding is "pickles are preserved in salt, so they are safe". Asazuke (quick pickles) are low in salt and not fermented, so they need the same hygiene as raw vegetables. In 2012 an outbreak from asazuke killed several people; the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare (MHLW) revised its hygiene code for pickles, and from 2021 pickle manufacturing became a licensed business.

## Rules and numbers

- **In August 2012 in Hokkaido, an outbreak of enterohaemorrhagic E. coli O157 was caused by asazuke of Chinese cabbage (hakusai)**: **169 patients and 8 deaths** (MHLW material).
- In response, the MHLW **revised its "Hygiene Code for Pickles"** (October 2012). It specifies **washing and sanitising the raw ingredients for asazuke (sodium hypochlorite etc.) and storage at 10°C or below**.
- **When the amended Food Sanitation Act took effect in June 2021**, pickle manufacturing became **a business requiring a licence** (previously only notification or prefectural ordinance). This has affected small farmers' direct sales too.

| Type | Typical salt | Storage |
|---|---|---|
| Asazuke / overnight pickles (ichiyazuke) | **2–3%** | **Fridge, within 2–3 days** |
| Nukazuke (rice-bran pickles) | Bran bed (nukadoko) **around 10%** | Fridge or a cool place; stir every day |
| Takuan, umeboshi (fully cured) | **10% or more** (lower in reduced-salt products) | Follow the label. Reduced-salt products need refrigeration |
| Kimchi | **2–3%**, lactic fermentation | Fridge |

## Misunderstanding 1｜Home-made asazuke is safer than shop-bought

**Poorly washed ingredients, leaving it at room temperature, an unclean container** — at home the risk can be higher. The basic rule: **wash the vegetables well, pickle them in the fridge, and eat them within 2–3 days.**

## Misunderstanding 2｜"Reduced-salt" umeboshi keeps like the traditional kind

Traditional umeboshi (**18–20% salt**) keeps for a long time at room temperature. **Reduced-salt products (5–10% salt) mostly need refrigeration and have a short shelf life.** Read the label.

## Misunderstanding 3｜Pickles are salty, so it is better not to eat them

Pickles are eaten **in a set amount**. Two slices of takuan (20 g) contain **about 0.7 g of salt equivalent**. Japan's salt-intake problem lies mainly in **seasonings, soups and processed food as a whole**.

## Misunderstanding 4｜A bran bed goes bad unless it is stirred every day

The shared guide is **every day in summer, every few days in winter or in the fridge**. A white surface layer is **film-forming yeast (産膜酵母, sanmaku kōbo)**, generally described as fine once stirred back in. **Pink, black or blue mould is a different matter.**

## Misunderstanding 5｜Pickles are fermented, so they are all good for you

Asazuke is **not fermented.** Only some pickles — nukazuke, suguki, kimchi — are lactic-fermented. **Under the Health Promotion Act and the Act against Unjustifiable Premiums and Misleading Representations, definitive health claims cannot be made either.**
