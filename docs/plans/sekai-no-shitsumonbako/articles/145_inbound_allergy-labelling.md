# Q. What do people get wrong about allergy labelling on Japanese food?｜Japan Questions #145

For travellers with food allergies, Japan is both reassuring and confusing: the labelling law is strict, but the list of covered allergens differs from the EU and US, and restaurants are not covered at all.

## A. The common misunderstanding is that Japanese labels follow the same allergen list as home. Japan requires labelling of 8 "specified ingredients" (including walnut since 2023) and recommends 20 more. Sesame, celery and mustard — mandatory in the EU — are only "recommended" here, and restaurant menus have no legal labelling duty.

## The Japanese list (Food Labelling Standards)

| Status | Allergens |
|---|---|
| **Mandatory (8)** | Egg, milk, wheat, buckwheat (soba), peanut, shrimp, crab, **walnut** (added 2023; full enforcement from 1 April 2025) |
| **Recommended (20)** | Almond, abalone, squid, salmon roe, orange, cashew, kiwi, beef, sesame, salmon, mackerel, soybean, chicken, banana, pork, macadamia (added 2024, replacing matsutake), peach, yam, apple, gelatin |

- Labelling applies to **pre-packaged processed foods** sold in shops. **Restaurants, bakeries and takeaway counters are exempt**; many post voluntary allergen charts, especially chains.
- Japanese labels list allergens in the **ingredient list**, often in brackets, e.g. しょうゆ（小麦・大豆を含む）— "soy sauce (contains wheat and soy)". The **"一部に…を含む"** phrase at the end summarises the allergens present.
- **Fish sauces and dashi** are common hidden sources; buckwheat (soba) flour can appear in noodles that look like wheat noodles.

> **[Verify]** The walnut enforcement date and the macadamia addition with the Consumer Affairs Agency's allergen labelling page before publication.

## Misunderstanding 1｜"No allergens listed" means allergen-free

It means **none of the 8 mandatory ones** are declared. Sesame, soy or mustard may still be present and unlabelled if the manufacturer chose not to declare.

## Misunderstanding 2｜Restaurant menus are legally checked

They are not. **Ask staff**; an allergy card in Japanese (available from the Japan National Tourism Organization and hospital associations) is the most reliable tool.

## Misunderstanding 3｜"May contain" warnings are standard

Precautionary "may contain" labels are **discouraged** under Japanese rules and used less than in the US. Absence of such a warning does not mean the factory is dedicated.

## Misunderstanding 4｜Soba is a wheat noodle

Soba is **buckwheat**, a mandatory allergen in Japan because reactions can be severe. Soba is also served in the same water as udon in many shops.

## Misunderstanding 5｜Epinephrine auto-injectors are easy to replace

Adrenaline auto-injectors are prescription-only. **Bring your own** within the personal-import limits and carry the prescription (see the article on bringing medicine).
