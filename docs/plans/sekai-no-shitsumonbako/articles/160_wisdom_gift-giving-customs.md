# Q. What do people get wrong about Japanese gift-giving customs?｜Japanese Everyday Wisdom #160

- Shelf: `wisdom` ／ URL: `/en/wisdom/gift-giving-customs` ／ Status: **Draft — sources unverified**

Visitors notice the wrapping and the bowing and conclude that Japanese gift-giving is about ceremony. Households experience it as a calendar of obligations with fairly precise budgets — and a set of rules about what not to give that are practical, not mystical.

## A. The common misunderstanding is that any thoughtful gift will do. Japanese gift-giving runs on occasion, reciprocity and proportion: a mid-year (ochūgen) and year-end (oseibo) gift to people you are indebted to, a return gift (okaeshi) at roughly half the value of what you received, and a preference for consumables that leave no burden. The taboos are few and specific.

## The calendar and the amounts

| Occasion | When | Typical amount | To whom |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Ochūgen** (mid-year) | Early to mid-July (August in some regions) | **¥3,000–5,000** | Bosses, in-laws, mentors, doctors |
| **Oseibo** (year-end) | Early to 20 December | **¥3,000–5,000**, slightly higher than ochūgen | same |
| **Wedding gift (cash, goshūgi)** | At the reception | **¥30,000** (friend), **¥50,000–100,000** (relative) | Odd numbers, new notes |
| **Condolence money (kōden)** | At the funeral | **¥5,000–10,000** (acquaintance), more for relatives | Odd or set amounts, **used notes** |
| **Okaeshi** (return gift) | Within about a month | **about half (hangaeshi)** of what was received | To the giver |
| **Temiyage** (visiting gift) | When visiting a home | **¥1,000–3,000**, consumable | Host |

- Surveys by department stores and gift associations put ochūgen participation at **around 30–40% of households** and falling, while temiyage and okaeshi remain near-universal.
- **Noshi** paper and **mizuhiki** cords: a **red-and-white bow that can be re-tied** for repeatable happy events (birth, seasonal gifts); a **tight knot (musubikiri)** for once-only events (wedding, recovery from illness, funerals in black-and-white or yellow-and-white).

> **[Verify]** Amounts are ranges reported in etiquette surveys (e.g. by the All Japan Gift Association and department stores) and shift with prices; participation rates with the latest surveys.

## Misunderstanding 1｜Bigger is better

An oversized gift **creates a debt** the receiver must repay at half value. Proportion is the courtesy. For a first visit, a **¥1,000–2,000 box of local sweets** is exactly right.

## Misunderstanding 2｜The wrapping is decoration

The paper and cord **encode the occasion**. A re-tieable bow on a wedding gift reads as "may this happen again". Shops wrap correctly if you tell them the purpose — **ask for the occasion-appropriate noshi**.

## Misunderstanding 3｜Cash is impersonal

For weddings, funerals, births and children's New Year (otoshidama), **cash in the correct envelope is the norm**, in the correct condition: **new notes** for celebrations, **used notes** for funerals (new ones imply you were prepared for the death).

## Misunderstanding 4｜Anything from home is welcome

Avoid **sets of four or nine** (homophones for death and suffering), **knives and scissors** (cutting ties), **combs** (ku-shi, another death homophone) and, for hospital visits, **potted plants** ("taking root" in the illness) and camellias (heads drop). **Consumables — sweets, tea, coffee, fruit, sake — are the safe default.**

## Misunderstanding 5｜Refusing twice means no

A gift may be **politely declined once or twice** before acceptance; likewise the recipient may not open it in front of you. **Neither is rejection.** Present it with both hands, mention modestly that it is a small thing ("tsumaranai mono desu ga" is old-fashioned; "kokoro bakari desu ga" is current).
