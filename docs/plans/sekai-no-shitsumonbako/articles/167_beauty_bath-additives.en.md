# Q. What are the common misunderstandings about bath additives?｜Japan Questions #167

- Shelf: `beauty` ／ URL: `/en/beauty/bath-additives` ／ Status: **Draft — English edition, sources unverified**

In Japan, where most people soak in a bath every day, the range of bath additives (入浴剤, nyūyokuzai) is enormous, and they are a popular souvenir to take home. Whether they "do anything" or are "just fragrance" depends on which legal category the product falls into. This article also covers a point specific to Japanese bathrooms: whether the additive is safe for a bath that reheats its own water.

## A. The biggest misunderstanding is "all bath additives are the same." In Japan they are split into quasi-drugs (medicated bath salts; a quasi-drug is a Japanese category between cosmetics and medicines) and cosmetics (bath cosmetics), and the effects each may claim are different. Whether a product suits your bath's reheating system or the material of the tub is something to check on the label.

## Categories and labelling

| Category | Label | Examples of claims allowed | Typical ingredients |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Quasi-drug (bath preparation)** | "医薬部外品" (quasi-drug) | Approved effects such as **recovery from fatigue, poor circulation (冷え症), stiff shoulders, lower back pain, neuralgia, heat rash, eczema** | Inorganic salts (sodium sulphate etc.), carbonated (fizzing) types, herbal extracts |
| **Cosmetic (bath cosmetic)** | No category label ／ "化粧品" (cosmetic) | Within the 56 items, such as **cleanses the skin, moisturises, refreshes with fragrance** | Moisturising ingredients, fragrance, colouring |
| General goods (雑貨, zakka) | — | No effects may be claimed | Bath salts (table salt), bath bombs etc. |

- With **carbonated (fizzing) types**, the guide is to get in **after it has dissolved and before the carbon dioxide has gone (about 2 hours).**
- In a bath with a **reheating or circulating heater (追い焚き, oidaki — Japanese baths often reheat the same water by passing it through the boiler)**, products containing **sulphur, salt or cloudy (milky) ingredients** may **not be usable.** Check **both the bath heater's manual and the additive's label.**
- **Milky (cloudy) types** carry warnings about discolouring **certain tub materials (marble, wood, enamel).**

> **[Verify]** Check the range of quasi-drug claims against the MHLW "Scope of efficacy claims for quasi-drugs", and the duration of carbonation against Japan Bath Additive Industry Association materials.

## Misunderstanding 1｜Add a bath additive and it is the same as a hot spring

It is a different thing from a "hot spring" as defined by the Hot Spring Act. **"Contains hot-spring ingredients" is not the same as a hot spring, and the claims allowed are decided by the product's category.**

## Misunderstanding 2｜Add more and it works better

**Exceeding the stated amount does not increase the effect; it increases the strain on the tub and heater and the irritation to the skin.** Keep to the amount on the label.

## Misunderstanding 3｜The leftover bath water can be used for laundry

Japanese washing machines can draw leftover bath water (残り湯, nokoriyu), but **cloudy types, strongly coloured types and heavily moisturising types** can leave colour or residue on clothes. **Check whether the label says it can be used for laundry**, and **rinse with tap water.**

## Misunderstanding 4｜The same additive is fine for babies and the elderly

For **infants, people with sensitive skin and the elderly**, check **the age range and warnings on the label.** The rule is to **stop if the skin reacts.**

## Misunderstanding 5｜A long soak in a hot bath with additives is healthy

Most bathing accidents involve **hot water and long soaks** (see our article on bath temperature). Aim for **41°C (106°F) or below, about 10 minutes**, and even if the additive makes you feel it is "working", **do not linger** — that is the safe side.
