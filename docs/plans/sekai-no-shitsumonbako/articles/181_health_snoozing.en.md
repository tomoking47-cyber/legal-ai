# Q. What are the common misunderstandings about snoozing (going back to sleep after the alarm)?｜Japan Questions #181

- Shelf: `health` ／ URL: `/en/health/snoozing` ／ Status: **Draft — English edition, sources unverified**

Turning the alarm off and dozing off again is a habit found everywhere. In Japanese it has a name, *nidone* (二度寝, literally "second sleep"), and the question "is it bad for you, or actually good?" comes up again and again. Here we sort it out along the lines of the sleep guide published by the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare (MHLW).

## A. The biggest misunderstanding is "snoozing tops up your sleep." The broken-up sleep you get after the alarm is shallow, and it pushes your wake-up time later and unsettles your body clock. Feeling sleepy is a sign that you are not getting enough sleep. The answer is either to go to bed earlier or to fix your wake-up time.

## What to base your judgement on

- The MHLW **Sleep Guide for Health Promotion 2023** [健康づくりのための睡眠ガイド2023] lists **keeping a fixed wake-up time** and **getting morning light** as the basics for keeping the body clock in order.
- Sleep after being woken by an alarm is **mostly light sleep**. Even **several snatches of 5–10 minutes** are generally described as giving little of the recovery that deep sleep provides.
- The groggy state right after waking (**sleep inertia**) is noted to **last longer** when you snooze repeatedly.

| Situation | How to think about it |
|---|---|
| You snooze every morning | You may **simply not be getting enough sleep**. Move bedtime 15–30 minutes earlier |
| Only on days off | Keep the gap from your weekday wake-up time **within 2 hours** (social jet lag) |
| Still sleepy after getting up | Use **morning light** (open the curtains, go outside) to move the body clock forward |
| Snoring, or strong daytime sleepiness | **Not a question of how often you snooze — see a doctor** (possible sleep apnoea, etc.) |

> **[Verify]** Check the relevant passages of the Sleep Guide 2023. The description of sleep inertia is the general account given in sleep medicine.

## Misunderstanding 1｜Snoozing adds to your sleep

It is **repeated light sleep**, generally described as giving little of the recovery that deep sleep gives. **Adding the same amount of time to the night before** is more efficient.

## Misunderstanding 2｜Several alarms 5 minutes apart make it easier to get up

**You drift back to sleep each time**, so sleep inertia drags on. **One alarm, get up, get some light** — that is the approach in line with the guide.

## Misunderstanding 3｜Snoozing feels good because your body needs it

The pleasant feeling is **the sensation of light sleep and dozing**; it has nothing to do with how much sleep you need. **If you feel very sleepy, that is a sign to look again at your night-time sleep.**

## Misunderstanding 4｜A lie-in at the weekend makes up for a tiring week

**If you wake more than 2 hours later than usual, the body clock shifts later and Monday morning gets harder.** Keep your wake-up time. **If you are sleepy, take a nap of 30 minutes or less in the early afternoon** (see our article on "banking" sleep).

## Misunderstanding 5｜Snoozing is a sign of weak will

**Not enough sleep, an irregular wake-up time, a dark bedroom** — environmental factors are generally described as having a large influence. Tricks shared among people living in Japan include **sleeping with the curtains slightly open, or using a light-based (dawn-simulation) alarm clock**.
