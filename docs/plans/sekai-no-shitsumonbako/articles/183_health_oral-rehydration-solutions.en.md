# Q. What are the common misunderstandings about oral rehydration solutions and sports drinks?｜Japan Questions #183

- Shelf: `health` ／ URL: `/en/health/oral-rehydration-solutions` ／ Status: **Draft — English edition, sources unverified**

In many countries, "a sports drink for heatstroke" is the standard idea. In Japan, oral rehydration solutions (ORS; 経口補水液, *keikō hosuieki*, sold under names such as OS-1) are on the shelves of every pharmacy and convenience store, and official guidance sets out when to use which. Here is the difference.

## A. The biggest misunderstanding is "an oral rehydration solution and a sports drink are the same thing." An ORS is a "drink for dehydration": it is designed low in sugar and high in salt so that water and electrolytes are absorbed quickly, and it contains too much salt for everyday hydration. A sports drink is high in sugar and does not contain enough salt to correct dehydration.

## How the contents differ (approximate, per 100 mL)

| Item | Oral rehydration solution | Sports drink | Water / tea |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sodium | **approx. 115 mg** (approx. 0.3 g salt equivalent) | **approx. 40–50 mg** | almost 0 |
| Carbohydrate (sugar) | **approx. 1.8–2.5 g** | **approx. 4–6 g** | 0 |
| Role | **Replacing water and electrolytes when dehydrated** (Food for Special Dietary Uses — for the sick, etc.) | Fluid and energy during exercise | Everyday hydration |
| Drinking it every day | **Not suitable** (too much salt) | Watch the sugar | The default |

- ORS products are approved by the Consumer Affairs Agency as **"Food for Special Dietary Uses (food for the sick)"** [特別用途食品（病者用食品）]. In May 2023 a **standards-based category for "oral rehydration solution"** was created (per 100 mL: sodium 92–138 mg, glucose 1.00–2.60 g, osmolality 300 mOsm/L or below, and so on), and **from 1 June 2025 labelling a product as an "oral rehydration solution" without approval breaches the Health Promotion Act.** Products approved through the older **individual evaluation** route also remain on sale.
- The basis is the WHO's approach to oral rehydration salts: **a solution containing sodium and glucose in a set ratio speeds up absorption from the gut**.
- MHLW heatstroke guidance says: **after heavy sweating, a 0.1–0.2% salt solution (1–2 g of salt in 1 L of water)**; **if there is dizziness or nausea, an oral rehydration solution**; **if the person is confused or cannot drink, call an ambulance (dial 119 in Japan)**.

> **[Verify]** The nutritional figures are approximate, based on the labels of the major products. Check the Food for Special Dietary Uses category and labelling with the Consumer Affairs Agency, and the response to heatstroke in the MHLW leaflet "Preventing heatstroke" [熱中症予防のために].

## Misunderstanding 1｜On hot days it is good to drink ORS all the time

**It contains too much salt and is not suitable for everyday hydration.** The division of roles: normally **water or tea**; after heavy sweating, **a weak salt solution or a sports drink**; **when you have symptoms of dehydration**, an oral rehydration solution.

## Misunderstanding 2｜Diluting a sports drink turns it into an ORS

Diluting **reduces both the sugar and the salt**, so you cannot reproduce the **higher salt content** of an ORS. **If you make your own, the guide figure given by the MHLW and others is 40 g of sugar and 3 g of salt in 1 L of water**, but **for hygiene reasons a commercial product is the surer choice**.

## Misunderstanding 3｜ORS tastes bad, so children will not drink it

The explanation given in clinical settings is that it tastes bad **because you are not dehydrated**. **When dehydrated, people often find it easy to drink.** Jelly versions are also sold.

## Misunderstanding 4｜Older people do not drink water, so give them ORS every day

**People with kidney or heart disease are on salt restrictions**, and regular use needs a doctor's advice. The basics: **frequent water or tea day to day**, and **an ORS during fever, diarrhoea or heavy sweating**.

## Misunderstanding 5｜Once they have had a drink, the heatstroke is dealt with

Its role is **correcting mild dehydration**. If the person is **drowsy or confused, cannot drink unaided, or has a high body temperature**, **do not make them drink — call an ambulance and cool them down** first.
