# Q. What are the common misunderstandings about choosing food storage containers?｜Japan Questions #191

- Shelf: `trend` ／ URL: `/en/trend/food-storage-containers` ／ Status: **Draft — English edition, sources unverified**

With the spread of batch cooking (tsukurioki, 作り置き), storage containers have become a fixture of the Japanese kitchen. "Is plastic dangerous?" and "Can glass take anything?" are questions asked in every country. Here we set out how Japan regulates food utensils and containers, and what each material is good and bad at.

## A. The biggest misunderstanding is "some materials are safer than others." Food containers made of **synthetic resin (plastic)** sold in Japan have been covered by the positive-list system since 2020 (glass, ceramics and metals are outside it and come under separate standards), so if you use them as labelled, a safety gap between materials is not the starting point. Where they do differ is heat tolerance, transfer of smells and colours, weight, and whether they can go in the microwave or dishwasher.

## The rules, and how the materials compare

- In **June 2020**, an amendment to the Food Sanitation Act introduced a **positive-list system for synthetic-resin utensils and food packaging**. **The state defines which substances may be used, and nothing else is allowed** (**the transition period ended at the end of May 2025**).
- Containers carry labels for **heat tolerance, cold tolerance, microwave use and dishwasher use** (some fall under the Household Goods Quality Labelling Act). The basic rule is **do not exceed the temperature on the label**.

| Material | Good at | Bad at | Guide |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Polypropylene (PP)** | Light, does not break, cheap | Picks up grease, colours and smells; scratches | Heat tolerance **about 120–140°C**; usually labelled microwave-safe |
| **Heat-resistant glass** | No smell or colour transfer, oven-safe, you can see the contents | Heavy, breaks, sensitive to sudden cooling or heating | Tolerates a temperature difference of **about 120°C** |
| **Enamel (hōrō, ホーロー)** | Stands up to acid and salt, can go on a direct flame, cools quickly | **Not for the microwave**; rusts if chipped | — |
| **Stainless steel** | Tough, cools quickly, no smell transfer | **Not for the microwave**; you cannot see the contents | — |
| **Silicone** | Folds flat, freezer- and microwave-safe | Holds on to smells | Heat tolerance **about 200°C** |

> **[Verify]** Check the start date of the positive-list system and the end of the transition period with the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare and the Consumer Affairs Agency; check heat tolerances on each product's label.

## Misunderstanding 1｜Plastic containers are dangerous in the microwave

The starting point is **a container labelled "microwave-safe," used within the temperature on the label**. **Oily foods can exceed 140°C**, which can warp even a PP container. **Removing or loosening the lid** prevents steam from warping or bursting it.

## Misunderstanding 2｜Glass never breaks, whatever you do

Even heat-resistant glass can break from **sudden cooling or heating (freezer straight into the oven; a hot container set on a wet worktop)**. The basic sequence is **freezer → let it come partly back to room temperature → heat**.

## Misunderstanding 3｜The lid has the same heat tolerance as the container

**Lids are often polyethylene or another material with a lower heat tolerance.** For most products the body is microwave-safe but **the lid is not**. Check the two labels separately.

## Misunderstanding 4｜A scratched container is fine once washed

**Fine scratches are where dirt and bacteria linger.** If **colours or smells will not come out, scratches are obvious, or the lid no longer closes**, it is time to replace it.

## Misunderstanding 5｜If it is sealed, it keeps for any number of days

A seal only **prevents drying out and smell transfer**; **what stops food going off is temperature**. The usual guide for batch-cooked dishes is **2–3 days in the fridge, 2–3 weeks in the freezer** (see the batch-cooking article). **Writing the date on the container** is standard practice among people living in Japan.
