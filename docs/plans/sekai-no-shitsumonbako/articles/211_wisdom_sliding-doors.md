# Q. What do people get wrong about Japanese sliding doors (shoji and fusuma)?｜Japanese Everyday Wisdom #211

- Shelf: `wisdom` ／ URL: `/en/wisdom/sliding-doors` ／ Status: **Draft — sources unverified**

Paper-covered sliding doors are the image of a Japanese room. Abroad they read as fragile decoration; in the houses that still have them, they are a way of making a small building behave like a larger one.

## A. The common misunderstanding is that sliding doors are a style choice. They are a space strategy: a swinging door needs a clear arc of floor, a sliding door needs none, and a wall of sliding panels lets two small rooms become one large one for guests or a funeral and go back to two for sleeping. The paper is replaceable by design.

## Two kinds

| | Shoji (障子) | Fusuma (襖) |
|---|---|---|
| Construction | Wooden lattice with **translucent paper** on one side | Wooden frame with **opaque paper or cloth** on both sides |
| Where | Between a room and a corridor or veranda (**light comes through**) | Between rooms, or as closet doors |
| Function | Diffuse daylight, privacy without darkness | Divide or join rooms; hide storage |
| Maintenance | **Re-paper every few years** (paper sold in rolls; glue is starch-based) | Re-paper less often; patch tears |
| Weak point | Tears, children's fingers, pets | Humidity warping |

- A swinging door needs a clear quarter-circle of floor — roughly **0.4–0.5 m²** for a typical 75–80 cm leaf; a sliding door consumes **none**. In a **6-tatami room (about 9.3 m² at Tokyo tatami size, about 11 m² at Kyoto size)** that difference is significant.
- Traditional Japanese rooms had **no fixed function**: the same room was living room by day and bedroom by night (futon). Removable partitions made that possible.
- Shoji paper transmits roughly **40–50% of light** while blocking the view — the original privacy glass.

> **[Verify]** Light-transmission figures vary by paper type; check with paper manufacturers. Tatami dimensions vary by region (Kyoto 1.91 × 0.955 m; Tokyo 1.76 × 0.88 m).

## Misunderstanding 1｜They are flimsy

The frame is joinery and lasts generations. **The paper is the consumable** — cheap, replaceable in an afternoon, and the annual re-papering was traditionally done before New Year.

## Misunderstanding 2｜They offer no privacy

**Visual privacy, yes; acoustic, no.** Japanese homes evolved a culture of not hearing what one hears. Modern homes use sliding doors of wood or glass for the same space reasons with better sound.

## Misunderstanding 3｜Modern homes have abandoned them

**Sliding doors are standard in new Japanese homes** — not paper, but the same mechanism, for closets, bathrooms and to join living and dining rooms. Barrier-free design also prefers them (no threshold, no swing).

## Misunderstanding 4｜Shoji block the cold

Single-layer paper is a poor insulator. Old houses were cold; the solution was to heat the person (kotatsu, hot-water bottle — see those articles), not the room. Modern "shoji" often have a glass pane behind the paper.

## Misunderstanding 5｜You slide them any way you like

Open with the hand in the **recessed pull**, slide slowly (the track is wood), and **kneel to open a fusuma in a formal room** — a custom, not a rule, but still seen at ryokan.
