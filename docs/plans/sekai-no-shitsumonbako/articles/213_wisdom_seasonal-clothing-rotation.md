# Q. What do people get wrong about koromogae (the seasonal wardrobe change)?｜Japanese Everyday Wisdom #213

- Shelf: `wisdom` ／ URL: `/en/wisdom/seasonal-clothing-rotation` ／ Status: **Draft — sources unverified**

Twice a year, Japanese households swap the contents of their wardrobes — winter clothes into storage, summer clothes out, and back again in autumn. Schools and offices used to switch uniforms on fixed dates. It looks like tradition; it is closet management for a small home.

## A. The common misunderstanding is that koromogae is a ceremony with fixed dates. The dates (1 June and 1 October) are a school and office convention; at home it is a practical cycle: wash everything before storing it, store it with protection from moths and humidity, and use the changeover to discard what was not worn. It exists because closets are too small to hold both seasons.

## How it is done

| Step | Detail | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Pick the day | **Dry, sunny day**, when temperatures have settled (late May/June; late September/October) | Store clothes dry |
| 2. Wash or clean everything going away | Even if it looks clean — **sweat and skin oil are what moths eat and what yellows** | Moth and stain prevention |
| 3. Dry completely | Air a full day; ironing is optional | Humidity is the enemy in storage |
| 4. Sort | **Not worn this season → out** (sell, give, recycle) | The main decluttering moment of the year |
| 5. Store | Breathable boxes or drawer units; **moth repellent on top**; **desiccant at the bottom** | Repellent vapour sinks; moisture sinks |
| 6. Rotate the closet | Off-season to the top shelf or under the bed; current season at eye level | Space |

- The school convention: **summer uniform from 1 June, winter from 1 October**, with a transition period either side; many companies followed for office wear, and **Cool Biz** loosened it — though since 2021 the government has set **no nationwide Cool Biz period**, leaving each workplace to judge by temperature (the Ministry of the Environment's own offices run **1 May–30 September**).
- Storage in the **oshiire** (bedding closet) or under-bed boxes is the norm; **vacuum bags** are used for bulky items but damage down and wool if left compressed for long.
- Repellent types must not be mixed (see the moth-repellent article): **paradichlorobenzene, naphthalene and camphor** can melt together and stain.

> **[Verify]** Cool Biz dates with the Ministry of the Environment; storage practices are widely shared conventions.

## Misunderstanding 1｜It happens on 1 June and 1 October

Those are **institutional dates**. Households go by the weather, and many now do it in **three stages** as autumns lengthen.

## Misunderstanding 2｜Clean-looking clothes can go straight into storage

**Invisible sweat and oil** are what moths target and what turns yellow by autumn. Everything gets washed or cleaned first.

## Misunderstanding 3｜Vacuum bags are the ideal

Good for **cotton and synthetic bedding for a season**; **bad for down, wool and anything with loft**, which lose resilience when compressed for months.

## Misunderstanding 4｜Moth repellent goes at the bottom of the box

Repellent vapour is **heavier than air** — place it **on top**. Desiccant, which fights moisture that sinks, goes **at the bottom**.

## Misunderstanding 5｜It is a Japanese quirk

It is a **response to closet size**. Anyone living in a small flat does a version of it; Japan just named it and built the storage products around it.
