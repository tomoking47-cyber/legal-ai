# Q. What do people get wrong about Japanese New Year (Oshōgatsu)?｜Japanese Everyday Wisdom #218

- Shelf: `wisdom` ／ URL: `/en/wisdom/the-new-year` ／ Status: **Draft — sources unverified**

New Year is Japan's most important holiday — quieter than Christmas abroad, more consequential than the calendar suggests. Visitors expecting fireworks and parties find closed shops and families at home, and miss what is actually happening.

## A. The common misunderstanding is that Japanese New Year is a party. It is a family homecoming with a set of foods, visits and objects, most of which carry a practical logic: osechi is preserved food so nobody cooks for three days, the shrine visit is the year's first act, and the closing of shops (now mostly ended) was the one holiday shopkeepers had.

## The calendar

| Date | What happens |
|---|---|
| **Late December** | Year-end clean (ōsōji), nengajō (New Year cards) posted to arrive on 1 January, decorations up (**kadomatsu** pines at the door, **kagami-mochi** rice cakes, **shimekazari** rope) |
| **31 December (Ōmisoka)** | **Toshikoshi soba** (buckwheat noodles: long life, and easily cut ties with the old year); temple bells rung **108 times** at midnight (joya no kane) |
| **1–3 January (Sanganichi)** | **Hatsumōde** (first shrine/temple visit; Meiji Shrine receives about **3 million** visitors); **osechi** and **ozōni** (mochi soup); **otoshidama** (money gifts to children); many businesses closed |
| **7 January** | Nanakusa-gayu (seven-herb porridge) to rest the stomach; decorations come down (some regions 15 January) |
| **11 January** | Kagami-biraki: the display mochi is broken and eaten |

- **Nengajō volume** peaked at about **4.46 billion cards for the 2004 issue (printed in 2003)** and has fallen every year since: the initial print run for the **2026 issue was about 750 million**, down **30%** on the previous year and the fifteenth consecutive annual fall. Japan Post holds back cards posted from **15 December** and delivers them on 1 January (post by about 25 December to be sure).
- **Osechi** is a lacquered box of preserved dishes each with a meaning (black beans for health, herring roe for fertility, sweet chestnuts for wealth). It is preserved food because **cooking on the first three days was traditionally avoided** — the hearth deity rested, and so did the cook.
- **Mochi (rice cake) choking** is a real seasonal hazard: Tokyo Fire Department reports **dozens of ambulance calls** each New Year, mostly among the elderly; cut mochi small and chew thoroughly.

> **[Verify]** Nengajō figures with Japan Post; hatsumōde attendance with shrine announcements; mochi incident figures with the Tokyo Fire Department's annual warning.

## Misunderstanding 1｜It is like Western New Year's Eve

The eve is quiet: soba, television, a temple bell. **The day itself** is what matters, and it belongs to family and the shrine.

## Misunderstanding 2｜Everything is closed for a week

Department stores and chains now open **on 1 or 2 January** for the New Year sales and **fukubukuro** (lucky bags). Small shops, banks and government offices close **roughly 29 December to 3 January**. Convenience stores never close.

## Misunderstanding 3｜Osechi is a feast to be eaten at once

It is **three days of cold food**, eaten a little at a time. Modern households buy it from department stores (**¥10,000–50,000** a box) and supplement with hot dishes.

## Misunderstanding 4｜Anyone can send a New Year card

**Nengajō are not sent to a household in mourning** (they send a mochū notice in advance). Cards arriving after 7 January are sent as **kanchū-omimai** (midwinter greetings) instead.

## Misunderstanding 5｜Mochi is just a snack

Mochi is soft, sticky and dense, and **the leading cause of New Year choking deaths among the elderly**. The public guidance: **cut it small, eat it slowly, and never leave an older person eating mochi alone**.
