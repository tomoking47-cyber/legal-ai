# Q. What do people get wrong about how Japanese people layer clothes for winter?｜Japanese Everyday Wisdom #219

- Shelf: `wisdom` ／ URL: `/en/wisdom/layering-for-winter` ／ Status: **Draft — sources unverified**

Japanese winters are not the coldest, but Japanese homes and stations are colder than visitors expect, and the layering strategy — thin heat-retaining underwear, a mid layer, and a coat that comes off indoors — differs from the heavy-sweater approach of colder countries.

## A. The common misunderstanding is that a thick coat is the answer. Japanese winter dressing assumes moving between cold outdoors, cold homes and hot trains: a thin functional base layer, a light mid layer, and a coat you can carry. The base layer is where the innovation went, and where the country's "Warm Biz" campaign put its 20°C office target.

## The system

| Layer | What | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Base** | Thin synthetic heat-retaining underwear (the type popularised by Japanese fast-fashion chains from 2003) or merino | Warmth without bulk; wicks sweat on a hot train |
| **Mid** | Light knit or fleece; **vest/gilet** popular | Removable in a heated room |
| **Outer** | Down or wool coat; **taken off indoors** | Indoor spaces vary from 15°C (old homes) to 25°C (trains) |
| **Extremities** | **Neck** (scarf/snood), **wrists**, **ankles** — the "three necks" (san-kubi) rule | Blood vessels near the surface; warming them warms the body |
| **Disposable heat pads (kairo)** | Stuck to the lower back or held in pocket; **12–20 hours** of heat | Cheap targeted warmth; avoid direct skin contact (low-temperature burns) |
| **Feet** | Wool or heat-retaining socks; slippers indoors (floors are cold) | Homes lack central heating |

- The Ministry of the Environment's **Warm Biz** sets a **20°C** reference for heated offices, and promotes layering over turning up the heat.
- Japanese homes are **poorly insulated by rich-country standards** (see the kotatsu article); indoor temperatures in winter bedrooms below **18°C** are common, and the WHO recommends **18°C minimum** for health.
- **Kairo** (disposable heat pads) sell about **1.75 billion units a year** (Japan Kairo Industry Association: **1,753 million in FY2025**, 1,777 million in FY2024); the low-temperature burn warnings apply (see the hot-water-bottle article).

> **[Verify]** Warm Biz temperature with the Ministry of the Environment; kairo sales with industry association data; WHO housing temperature guidance.

## Misunderstanding 1｜One warm coat solves it

Trains and shops are heated hard; **you will be hot within minutes**. The coat must be **easy to remove and carry**, so thinner is often better.

## Misunderstanding 2｜Thick base layers are warmest

Bulk traps air but also sweat. **Thin heat-retaining fabrics** warm a little from moisture and dry fast — the reason they dominate here. Layer two thin ones rather than one thick.

## Misunderstanding 3｜Cover the torso, forget the neck

The **"three necks"** — neck, wrists, ankles — are where Japanese layering focuses: a scarf and warm socks do more than another sweater.

## Misunderstanding 4｜Heat pads go against the skin

**Never directly on skin** — they hold **40–60°C for hours**, the low-temperature burn range. Stick them on the outside of the base layer, and do not sleep with them.

## Misunderstanding 5｜Indoors you can strip down

**Homes are cold.** Residents wear the base layer, socks and often a fleece indoors, and heat the room only where they sit. Bring warm socks; the floor is where the cold is.
