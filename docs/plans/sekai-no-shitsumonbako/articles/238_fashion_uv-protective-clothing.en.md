# Q. What do people get wrong about UV-protective clothing and parasols?｜Japan Questions #238

- Shelf: `fashion` ／ URL: `/en/fashion/uv-protective-clothing` ／ Status: **Draft — English edition, sources unverified**

Visitors to Japan in summer often ask why so many people carry a parasol (日傘, higasa) and wear long-sleeved UV-cut clothing in the heat. Blocking UV with fabric has measurable effectiveness, and Japan's Ministry of the Environment has set out how clothing, parasols and sunscreen fit together.

## A. The biggest misunderstanding is "if you put on sunscreen you don't need clothing or a parasol". The Ministry of the Environment's manual describes basic UV protection as a combination: use shade, a hat, clothing, a parasol, sunglasses and sunscreen. Clothing and a parasol are the reliable part, because they need no reapplying. A fabric's UV-shielding rate is printed on the label.

## In numbers

| Measure | Detail |
|---|---|
| **UV-shielding rate (紫外線遮蔽率)** | The percentage of UV a fabric blocks. Most products sold as "UV-cut" claim **90% or more**. Measured under **JIS L1925** |
| UPF (the measure used abroad) | UPF 15–50+. **UPF 50 = 1/50 of UV (2%) gets through** |
| Parasols | **Dark colours such as black with a light-blocking rate of 99% or more** stop almost all UV reaching the head. **They cannot stop reflected UV** |
| A plain white T-shirt | UV-shielding rate **about 80%**. **Lower when wet** |
| Hats | **A brim of 7 cm or more** cuts UV to the face by **about 60%** (Ministry of the Environment manual) |

- The Ministry of the Environment's "UV Environmental Health Manual" gives these guides: **take precautions from UV Index 3**, and **avoid going out in the middle of the day from UV Index 8**. Japanese summers reach **UV Index 8–10**.
- **Darker colours, tighter weaves and thicker fabrics** shield more. **Polyester is generally described as letting less UV through than cotton.**
- **Some "UV-cut finish" products lose their effect gradually in the wash**; **others have the agent built into the fibre and do not.** Check the label.
- **A parasol's light-blocking rate and its heat-blocking are different things**: **even at 99% light-blocking the air under it is not cooler**, but **keeping direct sun off you lowers the perceived temperature** (the Ministry also recommends parasols as a heatstroke measure).

> **[Verify]** Check the measurement method against JIS L1925 and the UV Index guides against the Ministry of the Environment manual (2020). The figures are general guides.

## Misunderstanding 1｜Long sleeves are just hot and pointless

**A UV-cut long sleeve blocks reliably without reapplying sunscreen.** In a thin, breathable fabric, because direct sun never reaches the skin, **many people find it feels cooler than short sleeves in full sun**.

## Misunderstanding 2｜White clothes are cooler and block UV too

**White reflects heat, but tends to let more UV through than dark colours.** **"Cool" and "shielding" are separate**; look for a UV-cut finish.

## Misunderstanding 3｜Parasols are for women

The Ministry of the Environment **recommends parasols for men as a heatstroke measure** (public campaign from 2019). The **perceived temperature under a parasol is generally described as a few degrees Celsius lower**.

## Misunderstanding 4｜A UV-cut finish never washes out

**Surface-applied finishes decline gradually with washing.** Some labels state "up to ○ washes". **Finishes built into the fibre** are far more durable.

## Misunderstanding 5｜With a parasol you don't need sunscreen

**A parasol cannot stop UV reflected from the ground or walls** (**10–25%** is reflected by sand, **10–20%** by water, **80% or more** by snow). **Sunscreen on exposed skin** alongside the parasol is the approach in the Ministry's manual.
