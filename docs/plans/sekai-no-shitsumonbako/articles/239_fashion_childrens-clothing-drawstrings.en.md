# Q. What do people get wrong about drawstrings on children's clothes?｜Japan Questions #239

- Shelf: `fashion` ／ URL: `/en/fashion/childrens-clothing-drawstrings` ／ Status: **Draft — English edition, sources unverified**

Hood cords catching on playground equipment have caused accidents in many countries. Japan adopted a safety standard for cords on children's clothing (a JIS, Japanese Industrial Standard) in 2015. This article sets out what it says and how it compares with the standards you may know from home.

## A. The biggest misunderstanding is "if the design is cute, safety is not something to think about". In 2015 Japan adopted JIS L4129 (the number reads yoi fuku, "good clothes"), which for children's clothing under age 13 bans cords around the head and neck and sets maximum lengths for cords at the waist and hem. But a JIS is a voluntary standard, and imported garments and second-hand clothes may not have been made to it.

## What the standard says (JIS L4129, adopted December 2015)

| Part of the garment | Rule (applies to **under-13s**) |
|---|---|
| **Head and neck** | **No cords** (**under 7**). Ages 7 to under 13: **limits on the length of hanging cords and on drawstrings** |
| Waist and hem | Hanging cord **14 cm or less**; no cord may hang below the hem (must not extend past the bottom edge of the garment) |
| Cuffs | Cord protruding from the cuff: **75 mm (7.5 cm) or less for under-7s**, **140 mm (14 cm) or less for ages 7 to under 13** |
| Back | **No cords** |

- Background to the standard: **a Tokyo Metropolitan Government survey (2006) found that about 80% of parents had experienced an accident or near-miss involving cords on children's clothing**, followed by **requests from the Consumer Affairs Agency and Tokyo from 2013 onward**.
- A JIS is **voluntary**. **Products labelled "JIS L4129 compliant" or similar** can be checked for compliance. **Imported goods and second-hand clothes may not comply**, so **checking the cords yourself when you buy** is the practical approach.
- Standards abroad: the **EU standard (EN 14682)** was the model for the Japanese one and is almost identical in content. The **US (CPSC)** likewise bans neck cords.

> **[Verify]** Confirm the year and month of adoption, the age bands and the year of the Tokyo survey against published material from the Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry (METI) and the Tokyo Metropolitan Government.

## Misunderstanding 1｜Neck cords are safe if you tie them

**The knot or loop catching on playground equipment, a door or a bus door** is the typical accident. **Removing the cord** is the thinking behind the standard. **Pull it out or cut it off** is the practical step.

## Misunderstanding 2｜Anything sold as children's clothing in Japan meets the standard

**A JIS is voluntary, and non-compliant products are on the market.** The big makers have largely adopted it, but **imports, small brands and second-hand clothes** need checking.

## Misunderstanding 3｜Once they are at primary school it stops mattering

**The standard covers children under 13.** Cords catch on **playground equipment, bicycles, escalators and train doors** whatever the child's size.

## Misunderstanding 4｜There are more important things to worry about than cords

Clothing accidents reported to the Consumer Affairs Agency include **cords catching, hoods catching, buttons swallowed and ribbons tangling**. **Cords are the easiest of these to prevent.**

## Misunderstanding 5｜Children's clothes bought abroad follow the same rules

**EU and US products meet equivalent or stricter standards**, but **there are countries with no standard at all**. Judge by looking at **cords at the neck and back, and cords hanging below the hem**.
