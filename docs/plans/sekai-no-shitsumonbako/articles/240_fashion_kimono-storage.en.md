# Q. What do people get wrong about storing a kimono?｜Japan Questions #240

- Shelf: `fashion` ／ URL: `/en/fashion/kimono-storage` ／ Status: **Draft — English edition, sources unverified**

A kimono is often described as something handed down through generations, but that depends on storing it properly. More visitors now buy a yukata or a kimono in Japan, and "how do I put it away?" has become a question asked from abroad. Here is the traditional storage know-how, developed for Japan's humid climate.

## A. The biggest misunderstanding is "put a kimono in the chest and it keeps for decades". Silk kimono are vulnerable to damp, insects and light. They need airing (虫干し, mushiboshi) once or twice a year, a fresh paper wrapper (たとう紙, tatōshi) every few years, and careful use of moth repellent. After wearing, hang it on a kimono hanger for a few hours or overnight to let the moisture out before folding it away.

## Storage basics

| Point | What to do | Why |
|---|---|---|
| After wearing | **Hang on a kimono hanger overnight (out of direct sun)** to let sweat and moisture out. **Brush off dust with a brush or soft cloth** | Moisture causes mould and discolouration |
| Folding | **Hondatami** (the standard fold, along the seams) | The folds fall on the seams, so no creases show |
| Wrapping | Wrap in **tatōshi** (a washi paper wrapper). **Replace it every 1–3 years** (or as soon as it yellows) | Breathable and moisture-resistant. Old tatōshi holds damp |
| Where | **A paulownia (桐, kiri) chest or a breathable garment box**. **In a plastic storage box, add a desiccant**. Kimono-specific desiccants and moth repellents must **not touch the fabric** | Paulownia regulates humidity well |
| **Airing (mushiboshi)** | **Once or twice a year** (after a run of clear days in October–November, or in January–February), **half a day to a day in a well-ventilated room** | Lets the damp out; time to check for insects and mould |
| Moth repellent | **One type only**, placed **on top of the garments**. **Paradichlorobenzene can discolour gold and silver thread and foil** | Mixing types makes them liquefy and stain |

- Silk is **animal protein**: it yellows with **sweat (which oxidises and turns alkaline), skin oil and damp**, and is eaten by **clothes moths and carpet beetles**.
- **A kimono worn on a sweaty day needs asenuki** (sweat removal, a specialist kimono cleaner's process). **Whole-garment dry cleaning (丸洗い, maruarai) alone is generally described as leaving the sweat components behind.**
- **The top drawers of a chest stay dry; the bottom ones collect damp.** The usual arrangement is **silk at the top, cotton and linen at the bottom**.

> **[Verify]** The timing and frequency of airing and the interval for replacing tatōshi are general guides based on advice from the kimono trade and kimono-related associations.

## Misunderstanding 1｜Once wrapped in tatōshi it is protected for good

**Tatōshi absorbs damp, yellows and reaches the end of its life in a few years.** **Replace it when it yellows or stains.** **Never a plastic bag** (it traps moisture).

## Misunderstanding 2｜Fold it and put it away as soon as you take it off

**Folding it with sweat and moisture still in it leads to mould and yellowing.** **Hang it overnight to dry** is the basic rule.

## Misunderstanding 3｜More moth repellent, and several kinds, is safer

**Mixed types liquefy and stain**, and **paradichlorobenzene types can discolour gold thread and foil**. **One type, the stated amount, placed on top of the garments.**

## Misunderstanding 4｜Airing is a chore and can be skipped

**Airing once or twice a year is what keeps a silk kimono for decades.** **Simply opening the chest drawers and running a fan** is generally described as having some effect.

## Misunderstanding 5｜A cotton yukata is handled the same way as a kimono

**A cotton yukata can be washed at home** (check the label). **Wash, dry completely, fold and store.** It is a different matter from a silk kimono: the concern is **damp and yellowing rather than insects**.
