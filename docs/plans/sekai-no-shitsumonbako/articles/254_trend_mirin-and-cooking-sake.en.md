# Q. What are the common misunderstandings about mirin and cooking sake?｜Japan Questions #254

- Shelf: `trend` ／ URL: `/en/trend/mirin-and-cooking-sake` ／ Status: **Draft — English edition, sources unverified**

Mirin (sweet rice seasoning) and cooking sake are basic seasonings in Japanese cooking, but they are hard to find and tell apart outside Japan. Even in Japan, many people do not know the difference between "hon-mirin" and "mirin-style seasoning". What separates them is how the Liquor Tax Act classifies them, and that decides where they are sold, how they are stored and how much salt they add.

## A. The biggest misunderstanding is "all mirin is the same." Three products sit side by side on the shelf: hon-mirin (本みりん), an alcoholic drink of about 14% ABV taxed as liquor; mirin-style seasoning (みりん風調味料), a food with under 1% alcohol; and fermented seasoning "mirin type" (発酵調味料), which contains alcohol but has salt added so it cannot be drunk. Cooking sake also comes in two forms, "with salt" and "without salt (plain sake)", and the two are used differently.

## Three kinds of mirin

| Type | Alcohol | Category | Features | Once opened |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Hon-mirin (本みりん)** | **About 14%** | **Liquor** (Liquor Tax Act) | Made from glutinous rice, rice kōji and shōchū spirit. Gives **gloss, depth and stops food falling apart while simmering**. Sometimes needs **nikiri** (boiling off the alcohol) | **Room temperature is fine (away from direct sunlight)**, about 3 months |
| **Mirin-style seasoning (みりん風調味料, mirin-fū chōmiryō)** | **Under 1%** | Food | Sugars, seasonings and acidulants imitate the flavour. **No nikiri needed**. **Almost no salt** | **Refrigerate** (high in sugar and spoils easily); use soon after opening |
| **Fermented seasoning, mirin type (発酵調味料 みりんタイプ)** | **About 8–14%** | Food (**salt added so it cannot be drunk**; no liquor tax) | Close to hon-mirin but **about 2% salt**. **Reduce the salt in the recipe** | Room temperature is fine |

## Cooking sake

| Type | Salt | How to use |
|---|---|---|
| **Cooking sake (料理酒, ryōri-shu; salted)** | **About 2–3% salt** added so it cannot be drunk (no liquor tax, cheap) | **Reduce the salt in the recipe**. A recipe that says "sake" normally assumes **unsalted** |
| **Sake (清酒, seishu / nihonshu)** | None | Follow the recipe. Sold as liquor |

- Under the **Liquor Tax Act**, any drink of 1% ABV or more is "liquor". **Hon-mirin is liquor, so it cannot be sold to anyone under 20 and is sold only by shops with a liquor sales licence**.
- Hon-mirin is **about 40–45% sugar**; the **gloss** is generally attributed to this sugar, and **keeping food intact while simmering** to the combined action of the alcohol and sugar.

> **[Verify]** Check the alcohol strengths and categories against the Liquor Tax Act and the National Mirin Association's materials, and the salt figures against product labels.

## Misunderstanding 1｜Mirin-style seasoning can replace hon-mirin one for one

**Without alcohol, it does less to stop food falling apart or to mask odours, and its sugar profile differs.** It works as a substitute for gloss and sweetness. **Add "mirin-style" at the end of cooking.**

## Misunderstanding 2｜Using hon-mirin will make you tipsy

**Most of the alcohol is driven off by cooking**, but **for dishes that are not heated (dressed salads, dressings) the standard step is nikiri** (boil it briefly in a small pan to drive off the alcohol). **Take care for pregnant women, children and anyone about to drive.**

## Misunderstanding 3｜Cooking sake is the same as Japanese sake

**Salted cooking sake is about 2–3% salt.** **Replacing "1 tablespoon of sake" (about 15 g) in a recipe with cooking sake adds roughly 0.3–0.5 g of salt (about 1/16 teaspoon)**, so you need to **cut the salt** elsewhere.

## Misunderstanding 4｜Store mirin in the fridge

**Hon-mirin keeps at room temperature thanks to its alcohol and sugar** (avoid direct sunlight and heat). **Refrigeration can make the sugar crystallise.** **Mirin-style seasoning, on the other hand, goes in the fridge.**

## Misunderstanding 5｜You cannot get mirin outside Japan

**Exported hon-mirin is handled as liquor because of its alcohol content, so it can be hard to find in some countries.** **Mirin-style seasoning is classed as a food and travels more easily.** A known substitute is **sugar plus sake (or water)** to supply the gloss and sweetness.
