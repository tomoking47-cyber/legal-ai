# Q. What are the common misunderstandings about which vinegar to use?｜Japan Questions #255

- Shelf: `trend` ／ URL: `/en/trend/types-of-vinegar` ／ Status: **Draft — English edition, sources unverified**

Every kitchen in the world has vinegar, and the choice is wide: Japan's rice vinegar, grain vinegar and black vinegar, the West's wine vinegar and balsamic, China's Zhenjiang vinegar. Two questions come up again and again: "which one should I use?" and "is vinegar good for you?" Here we answer both from Japan's standards and labelling rules.

## A. The biggest misunderstanding is "all vinegar is the same sour liquid." Japan's JAS (Japanese Agricultural Standards) divides table vinegar into "brewed vinegar" (grain, rice, fruit, etc.) and "synthetic vinegar", and the raw material decides the aroma and sweetness. As for health effects, no claim may be made for ordinary vinegar; the exception is products notified as Foods with Function Claims, a regulated Japanese category.

## Types and uses

| Type | Raw material (JAS standard) | Character | Suits |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Grain vinegar (穀物酢, kokumotsu-su)** | Grains (wheat, rice, corn, etc.), **40 g or more per litre** | Clean, sharp acidity; cheap | Vinegared dishes (sunomono), pickles, also cleaning |
| **Rice vinegar (米酢, kome-zu)** | **Rice, 40 g or more per litre** | Mellow, with umami | Sushi rice, vinegared dishes |
| **Pure rice vinegar (純米酢, junmai-su)** | **Rice only** | Sweetness and aroma of rice | Same |
| **Black vinegar (黒酢, kuro-zu; rice black vinegar)** | Rice (brown rice), **180 g or more per litre**; brown from long fermentation | Depth and aroma | Stir-fries, simmered dishes, drinking vinegar |
| **Apple vinegar and other fruit vinegars** | Fruit juice, **300 g or more per litre** | Fruit aroma | Dressings, drinking |
| Wine vinegar, balsamic | Grapes | Western dishes | Dressings, sauces |
| **Synthetic vinegar (合成酢, gōsei-su)** | Glacial acetic acid diluted with water and seasoned | Flat acidity | Mostly commercial use |

- **Most table vinegar is 4–5% acidity.** **"Sushi vinegar" (すし酢) and "ponzu" (ポン酢) are seasoned processed products** and are classified separately from table vinegar.
- Claims such as **"drinking vinegar makes you slim or lowers blood pressure"** are **allowed only on products notified as Foods with Function Claims**; for ordinary vinegar they are prohibited under the **Health Promotion Act and the Act against Unjustifiable Premiums and Misleading Representations**.
- **Drinking undiluted vinegar irritates the teeth and the oesophagus**; makers advise **diluting it before drinking**.

> **[Verify]** Check the JAS raw-material standards against the Ministry of Agriculture, Forestry and Fisheries' "Japanese Agricultural Standard for Table Vinegar".

## Misunderstanding 1｜Vinegar kills germs, so it prevents food poisoning

**Vinegar does have some antibacterial action**, but **the concentration and time needed to kill food-poisoning bacteria reliably are not realistic in home cooking**; the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare's basics are **heating, chilling and cleanliness**. **Sushi rice and vinegared dishes do not "keep longer".**

## Misunderstanding 2｜It is fine to say black vinegar has health benefits

**No health claim may be made for black vinegar sold as an ordinary food.** Products notified as Foods with Function Claims may use **only the wording covered by the notification**.

## Misunderstanding 3｜Rice vinegar and grain vinegar are the same

**The raw material and aroma differ**; the usual practice is **rice vinegar for sushi rice** and **grain vinegar for light vinegared dishes**. **They can stand in for each other.**

## Misunderstanding 4｜Vinegar never goes off, so it keeps for years at room temperature

**Unopened, it keeps for a long time at room temperature**, but **once opened, oxidation and loss of aroma set in**; use it up within **6 months to 1 year**. **Many fruit vinegars carry a "refrigerate" instruction.**

## Misunderstanding 5｜The same vinegar does for cleaning and cooking

**For cleaning, use cheap grain vinegar** (or citric acid). **Never mix it with chlorine-based cleaners** (toxic gas); the same rule applies to citric acid (see the article on baking soda and citric acid).
