# Q. What do people get wrong about vegetarian, vegan and halal food in Japan?｜Japan Questions #268

- Shelf: `inbound` ／ URL: `/en/questions/vegetarian-and-halal-options` ／ Status: **Draft — sources unverified**

Japan looks vegetable-friendly — tofu, rice, seaweed — and then serves fish stock in almost everything. Travellers with dietary restrictions find the country harder than expected, and the reasons are structural, not hostile.

## A. The common misunderstanding is that Japanese food is largely vegetarian. Dashi made from bonito (fish) flakes underlies most soups, sauces and simmered dishes, and "vegetarian" is not a widely understood category. Options exist — shōjin ryōri temple cuisine, a growing number of certified vegan and halal restaurants, and convenience-store items with clear labels — but they must be sought out and asked for precisely.

## Where the traps are

| Looks vegetarian | Usually contains |
|---|---|
| Miso soup | **Bonito dashi** (fish) |
| Noodle broth (soba, udon) | Bonito and/or **pork/chicken** stock |
| Vegetable tempura at a set-meal restaurant | Tempura dipping sauce (**dashi**) |
| Tofu dishes (agedashi, hiyayakko) | **Dashi**-based sauce, **bonito flakes** on top |
| Okonomiyaki, takoyaki | **Fish flakes, seafood, pork**, egg |
| Onigiri "kombu" or "ume" | Often fine; **check for bonito (おかか) and dashi seasoning** |
| Curry | **Beef or pork fat/stock**, sometimes lard in the roux |

- The Japan Tourism Agency estimated vegetarian/vegan visitors at **about 1.5–2 million a year** pre-2020 and has published a **guide for restaurants** on serving them (2020).
- **Halal**: Japan has **no national halal certification body**; certification is by **private organisations** (e.g. Japan Halal Association, Nippon Asia Halal Association), so standards vary. **Muslim-friendly** signs mean some accommodation, not full certification.
- **Shōjin ryōri** (Buddhist temple cuisine) is **fully vegan by tradition** — Kyoto and Mount Kōya are the centres (¥3,000–8,000 a meal).
- **Alcohol in seasonings**: mirin and sake are used in many sauces; **soy sauce is typically brewed with wheat and contains trace alcohol** from fermentation.

> **[Verify]** JTA visitor estimates and restaurant guide with the Japan Tourism Agency; halal certification bodies with the organisations listed.

## Misunderstanding 1｜"Vegetarian" is understood

The word is known; the **category boundaries** are not. **Fish is often not considered meat** in casual usage. Say what you **cannot eat**: "sakana dame" (no fish), "dashi mo dame" (no fish stock too), "niku dame" (no meat). Carry a **card in Japanese** (JNTO and several apps provide them).

## Misunderstanding 2｜Ask for the dish without meat

Removing the topping does not remove the **stock**. Ask whether **the soup or sauce** contains dashi or meat — "dashi wa nani desu ka?" (what is the stock?).

## Misunderstanding 3｜Convenience stores are hopeless

They are **easier than restaurants** because ingredients are labelled. Look for **allergen lines** (see the labelling article) and simple items: **rice with salt, kombu onigiri, edamame, fruit, salads without dressing, soy milk**. Some chains label **vegan items**.

## Misunderstanding 4｜Halal means the restaurant is Muslim-run

Certification is **private and varied**. Check the certificate on display or the certifying body's list; **"Muslim-friendly"** may mean no pork but alcohol served.

## Misunderstanding 5｜You will go hungry

**Shōjin ryōri, Indian and Nepalese restaurants (widespread), certified vegan cafés in Tokyo, Kyoto and Osaka, and supermarket cooking** cover most needs. **Book ahead** for temple meals; walk-in vegan options are still thin outside big cities.
