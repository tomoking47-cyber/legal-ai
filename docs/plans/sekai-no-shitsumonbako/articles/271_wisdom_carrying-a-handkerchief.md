# Q. What do people get wrong about the Japanese habit of carrying a handkerchief?｜Japanese Everyday Wisdom #271

- Shelf: `wisdom` ／ URL: `/en/wisdom/carrying-a-handkerchief` ／ Status: **Draft — sources unverified**

Almost every adult in Japan carries a small towel or handkerchief, and the reason becomes obvious in the first public toilet: there are often no paper towels and no dryer. What looks like an old-fashioned nicety is a piece of infrastructure people carry on themselves.

## A. The common misunderstanding is that the handkerchief is a nostalgic accessory. It replaces paper towels — many public toilets deliberately have none — and doubles as a sweat towel in summer, a lap cloth at lunch, and a wrap for a wet umbrella. The modern version is a small terry "hand towel" about 25 cm square, sold everywhere, changed daily and washed with the laundry.

## Why it exists

| Fact | Detail |
|---|---|
| Public toilets | **Paper towels are uncommon**; many hand dryers were switched off from May 2020, and although the industry guideline dropped that restriction in April 2021, some were never switched back on |
| The item | **Hand towel (ハンドタオル)**, terry, **about 25 × 25 cm**, ¥300–1,000; or a cotton handkerchief for the pocket |
| Frequency | **One clean towel per day**; it goes in the wash with clothes |
| Other uses | Wiping sweat (summer), drying a wet seat, a lap cloth, wrapping a wet folding umbrella, a coaster for a cold drink |
| Children | Kindergartens and schools **require a handkerchief and tissues** in the pocket daily — the habit is taught at 3 |

- The paper-towel absence is partly **cost and waste**: a busy station toilet would use tens of thousands of towels a day. Building operators assume the visitor carries their own.
- Japan sells an estimated **hundreds of millions of hand towels and handkerchiefs a year**; the imabari region (Ehime) is the towel-making centre, and a **hand towel is the most common small gift** (see the gift article).
- **Pocket tissues** are the companion item — Japanese public toilets do provide paper, but tissues are for the street, and advertising tissues are handed out free at stations.

> **[Verify]** Towel production figures with the Japan Towel Industry Association; school requirements are near-universal conventions.

## Misunderstanding 1｜It is old-fashioned

It is **taught in kindergarten and carried by teenagers**. The item has modernised (terry hand towels, character prints); the habit has not faded.

## Misunderstanding 2｜Public toilets are poorly equipped

They are usually **clean and well-stocked with paper** — they simply expect you to dry your own hands. Shaking hands dry and wiping on trousers marks the visitor.

## Misunderstanding 3｜One handkerchief lasts a week

**Daily change** is the norm; a damp cloth in a pocket smells by evening. Buy several.

## Misunderstanding 4｜It is only for hands

In summer it is a **sweat towel** (see the summer heat article); at lunch a **lap cloth**; on a rainy day it **wraps a wet umbrella** before it goes in the bag. That versatility is why people bother.

## Misunderstanding 5｜Paper tissues do the same job

Tissues **disintegrate on wet hands** and create litter where bins are scarce. The towel is reusable and goes home in the bag.
