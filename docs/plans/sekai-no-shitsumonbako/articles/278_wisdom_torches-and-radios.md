# Q. What do people get wrong about torches, radios and power in a Japanese disaster kit?｜Japanese Everyday Wisdom #278

- Shelf: `wisdom` ／ URL: `/en/wisdom/torches-and-radios` ／ Status: **Draft — sources unverified**

Every Japanese household disaster checklist starts with a torch and a radio, and newcomers wonder why a radio in the age of smartphones. The 2011 and 2024 earthquakes answered that: the mobile network fails or is throttled, and the phone battery is the most precious thing you own.

## A. The common misunderstanding is that a smartphone replaces the torch and radio. In the first hours after a major earthquake, mobile networks are congested or down (carriers restrict traffic at 70–95%), and power may be out for days. A hand-crank or battery radio receives NHK's emergency broadcast regardless; a headlamp leaves hands free; and the real planning question is how to keep the phone charged for a week.

## The kit and the reasoning

| Item | Why | Detail |
|---|---|---|
| **Torch — headlamp preferred** | Hands free to carry children or move debris; falling glass at night | LED, **AA or AAA batteries** (the same as the radio), spare set stored separately |
| **Radio** | **Mobile networks were restricted for days after 2011**; radio works on batteries; **NHK Radio 1 and local FM** carry evacuation and supply information | Hand-crank models also charge a phone slowly; **AM/FM**; check it works every 1 September |
| **Power bank(s)** | The phone is torch, map, family contact and information — if charged | **10,000–20,000 mAh**, kept charged; rotate use so it is never empty |
| **Batteries** | Torch and radio | **Standardise on one size**; store 10+ with dates; **rotate every 2–3 years** |
| **Car** | A charging point and a radio | Keep the tank at least half full in typhoon and winter seasons |
| Solar / hand-crank | Slow, but indefinite | Backup, not primary |

- After the 2011 Tōhoku earthquake, carriers **restricted voice traffic by up to 70–95%**; texts and data worked intermittently. **Disaster message boards** (171 voice service and web171) exist for this reason and are open for practice on the **1st and 15th of every month**, plus 1–3 January, Disaster Prevention Week (30 Aug–5 Sep) and Disaster Prevention and Volunteer Week (15–21 Jan).
- **Public Wi-Fi "00000JAPAN"** is opened free by all carriers in a declared disaster.
- **Battery-free LED lanterns that run on salt water** and **candle-free lighting** are sold because candles caused fires after earthquakes; **do not use candles** after a quake (gas leaks).

> **[Verify]** Traffic-restriction figures with MIC's 2011 communications reports; 171 and 00000JAPAN details with carriers and MIC.

## Misunderstanding 1｜The phone is enough

Its battery is **the constraint**. The radio and torch exist so the phone's charge goes to **contact and information**, not to lighting a room.

## Misunderstanding 2｜Radio is obsolete

**Radio worked when networks did not** in 2011 and 2024. NHK is legally required to broadcast disaster information; local FM stations relay municipal announcements.

## Misunderstanding 3｜A big torch is best

A **headlamp** is the choice of people who have done it: both hands free, light where you look. A lantern for the room; a headlamp per person.

## Misunderstanding 4｜Buy batteries when the typhoon is forecast

They **sell out the same afternoon**. Store them, **one size for all devices**, and rotate on Disaster Prevention Day (1 September).

## Misunderstanding 5｜Candles are a fine backup

**Post-earthquake fires from candles and gas leaks are a documented cause of secondary disaster.** LED only; check for gas smell before any flame.
