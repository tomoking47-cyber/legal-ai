# Q. What do people get wrong about warranties and repair in Japan?｜Japanese Everyday Wisdom #280

- Shelf: `wisdom` ／ URL: `/en/wisdom/warranty-and-repair-culture` ／ Status: **Draft — sources unverified**

Japan repairs things: knives, umbrellas, shoes, cast-iron pans, a 30-year-old rice cooker. Visitors see this as thrift or sentiment. It rests on a warranty and parts culture with specific rules — and on an industry-code repair-parts retention period most people do not know exists.

## A. The common misunderstanding is that Japanese appliances simply last longer. Manufacturers are expected — under an industry standard, not just goodwill — to hold repair parts for 5 to 10 years after a model is discontinued, warranties are one year as standard with extended cover sold by retailers, and a network of repair shops and municipal repair events keeps everyday objects going. The habit of repairing is supported by a system that makes repair possible.

## The system

| Element | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Standard warranty (保証書)** | **1 year** on most appliances; the **warranty card must be stamped with the purchase date by the shop** — keep it with the receipt |
| **Extended warranty** | Sold by electronics retailers for **5–10% of the price**, covering 3–5 years; some include accidental damage |
| **Repair-parts retention** | A fair-competition code (家庭電気製品製造業における表示に関する公正競争規約), run by the EFTC and approved by the CAA and the JFTC, under which manufacturers **hold functional parts for a set period after discontinuation**: e.g. **refrigerators 9 years, washing machines 6 years, colour TVs 8 years, rice cookers 6 years, air conditioners 9 years** |
| **Repair channels** | Manufacturer service centres; retailer counters; **independent repair shops** for shoes, bags, watches, knives; **municipal "repair cafés" and events** |
| **Long-term-use warnings** | Since April 2009, five product types — **electric fans, ventilation fans, air conditioners, washing machines and CRT televisions** — carry a **"design standard use period"** label (e.g. 10 years) and manufacturers must warn users to inspect after it |
| Consumer protection | The **Consumer Contract Act** and **Civil Code (contract non-conformity liability, 2020 revision)** give remedies for defects beyond the warranty card |

- The **knife-sharpening stall at the department store, the umbrella repair counter, the shoe-repair kiosk at the station** are commercial services that survive because customers use them.
- **Design standard use period (設計上の標準使用期間)** labels appeared after a series of fires in aged appliances; the label is a reminder to **inspect or replace**, not a guarantee of function.
- Repair is also a **waste-policy** matter: large-item disposal costs money in Japan (**¥300–3,000 per item via the municipality**, and the **Home Appliance Recycling Law fee** — about ¥550 for an air conditioner since February 2026 (down from ¥990), ¥1,320–2,970 for a TV, ¥2,530 for a washer and ¥3,740–4,730 for a fridge, plus a separate collection-and-transport charge), which tips the calculation toward repair.

> **[Verify]** Parts retention periods with the Association for Electric Home Appliances (AEHA) guidelines; long-term-use labelling with METI; recycling fees with the AEHA recycling ticket system.

## Misunderstanding 1｜Japanese products never break

They break. **They get fixed** because parts exist and repair is routine — and because throwing a large item away costs money and paperwork.

## Misunderstanding 2｜The warranty card is a formality

**Unstamped cards may be refused.** Have the shop stamp the date at purchase, keep it with the receipt; for tax-free purchases, note that **international warranty coverage varies by brand**.

## Misunderstanding 3｜After a year, you are on your own

**Parts are held for years after discontinuation**, and the Civil Code's non-conformity rules apply to defects present at sale. Out-of-warranty repair at the manufacturer is routine and quoted before work.

## Misunderstanding 4｜The "10 years" label means the product will last 10 years

The **design standard use period** is a **safety reminder**: after that, inspect or replace because of fire risk. Many units run longer; the label says to check.

## Misunderstanding 5｜Repairing shoes and umbrellas is quaint

It is **cheaper than replacement** for anything decent, and the infrastructure is there: station shoe kiosks re-heel in **10 minutes for ¥1,000–2,500**; umbrella ribs are replaced for a few hundred yen; knives are sharpened while you shop.
