# Q. What do people get wrong about underarm sweat marks and yellowing?｜Japan Questions #298

- Shelf: `fashion` ／ URL: `/en/fashion/underarm-sweat-stains` ／ Status: **Draft — English edition, sources unverified**

Yellow underarm stains on shirts are a worldwide complaint, and "is it the antiperspirant or the sweat?" comes up again and again on forums abroad. Here we sort out the causes and how to remove the stains, with Japan's sweaty summers and its antiperspirant habits in mind.

## A. The biggest misunderstanding is "the yellow is the colour of sweat". Sweat is almost colourless; the main causes of yellowing are oxidised skin oil, and a reaction in the fibre between the aluminium compounds in antiperspirant, skin oil and sweat. Prevention is "wash straight after wearing" and "let the antiperspirant dry before you dress". Removal is "soak in, or paste with, oxygen bleach (酸素系漂白剤, sanso-kei hyōhakuzai)" — and avoid chlorine bleach.

## Causes and responses

| Cause | Mechanism | Response |
|---|---|---|
| **Oxidised skin oil** | Skin oil left after washing yellows over time | **Wash straight after wearing**; **pre-wash the underarms (apply neutral detergent directly and rub gently)** |
| **Antiperspirant ingredients** | Aluminium salts + sweat + skin oil bind to the fibre, causing **yellowing and stiffening** | **Let the antiperspirant dry before dressing**, **use less**, **apply at night (only a thin layer in the morning)** |
| **Apocrine sweat** | Sweat from the underarm apocrine glands contains protein and lipids and colours as it oxidises | **Sweat pads (the stick-on type for clothing, 汗取りパッド)**, **an undershirt** |
| **Fibre** | Cotton yellows readily; polyester holds odour | **Bleach white shirts regularly with oxygen bleach** |

### Removal

| Stage | Method |
|---|---|
| Light yellowing | **Apply liquid oxygen bleach directly, leave 30 minutes → normal wash** |
| Set-in yellowing | **Dissolve powdered oxygen bleach in water at 40–50°C (104–122°F) and soak for 30 minutes to 2 hours** (**check the temperature and bleach symbol on the care label**) |
| Hardened antiperspirant build-up | **Mix powdered oxygen bleach + baking soda (重曹) with a little water into a paste → apply, leave 30 minutes → brush gently → wash** |
| Won't come out | **Take it to a dry-cleaner and say it is "yellowing" (黄ばみ, kibami).** **Do not use chlorine bleach** (it strips colour from patterned items; even on whites it can discolour the fabric and buttons) |

> **[Verify]** Check the bleach-symbol categories against the Consumer Affairs Agency's "Care labelling" page, and antiperspirant ingredients against the product label. The removal methods are the general guidance of detergent makers.

## Misunderstanding 1｜It's the colour of sweat, so stop the sweat

**Sweat is almost colourless. The main cause is the reaction between skin oil and antiperspirant.** Many people report **less yellowing after cutting down on antiperspirant**.

## Misunderstanding 2｜Chlorine bleach will definitely shift it

**Chlorine bleach can react with the aluminium in antiperspirant and cause more yellowing and fabric damage.** **Oxygen bleach** is the rule.

## Misunderstanding 3｜More antiperspirant means fewer sweat marks

**Too much means more build-up in the fibre, and more yellowing and stiffening.** **The right amount, and let it dry before you dress.** **Applying at night** works while you sweat less and lets you use less in the morning (see the antiperspirant article).

## Misunderstanding 4｜Once it's yellow, bin it

**An oxygen-bleach soak fades most of it.** **The sooner, the easier.** **A batch soak before the seasonal wardrobe change (衣替え, koromogae)** is a standard habit among people living in Japan.

## Misunderstanding 5｜Sweat pads are over the top

**Stick-on sweat pads are the most reliable prevention for extending a shirt's life**, and in Japan they are an everyday item. **Undershirts with built-in underarm pads** work the same way.
