# Q. What are the common misunderstandings about when to take a bath before bed?｜Japan Questions #301

- Shelf: `health` ／ URL: `/en/health/bath-timing-before-bed` ／ Status: **Draft — English edition, sources unverified**

In Japan most people soak in a hot bath (ofuro) every evening, so "should I get in right before bed, or leave a gap?" is an everyday question. Japan's national sleep guide touches on the link between bathing and sleep, and the answer comes from how body temperature changes.

## A. The biggest misunderstanding is "warm up right before bed and get straight under the covers." People become sleepy as their core body temperature (the temperature deep inside the body) falls. A bath raises it, and it starts to fall again 1–2 hours later; going to bed at that point is in line with the thinking in the national sleep guide. A hot bath immediately before bed leaves your temperature high, and falling asleep is harder.

## In numbers

| Item | Detail | Source |
|---|---|---|
| When to bathe | **1–2 hours before bed** | Sleep Guide 2023, e-Healthnet |
| Water temperature | **Around 40°C (104°F)**. **41°C (106°F) or below, about 10 minutes** is the guide for preventing bathing accidents | Consumer Affairs Agency |
| Mechanism | A bath **raises core body temperature by 0.5–1°C**, and sleepiness strengthens as it **falls again through heat loss** | General explanation in sleep medicine |
| If you bathe right before bed | **A lukewarm 38–39°C (100–102°F) bath**, or **a shower or foot bath** | Same as above |
| Morning shower | For waking up. **Slightly hotter** is fine | — |

- The Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare (MHLW) *Sleep Guide for Health Promotion 2023* lists **a (lukewarm) bath before bed** as one of the daily habits that help you fall asleep.
- **Hot water (42°C / 108°F or above) is generally described as stimulating the sympathetic nervous system and pushing the body towards wakefulness**, so it **does not suit the moment before bed**. It does suit **waking up in the morning**.
- **Bathing accidents (very hot water, long soaks)** are most common among older people and are concentrated in **winter** (see our article on bath temperature).

## Misunderstanding 1｜A hot bath right before bed helps you sleep

**While body temperature is still high, falling asleep is generally described as harder.** Bathe **1–2 hours before**, and **go to bed as your temperature starts to fall**.

## Misunderstanding 2｜A shower alone has nothing to do with sleep

**A bath raises body temperature more easily**, but **even a lukewarm shower opens the blood vessels in the hands and feet and speeds up heat loss**, so an effect on falling asleep is discussed. **Right before bed, a shower may in fact be the better choice.**

## Misunderstanding 3｜With a half-body bath (hanshin-yoku), you can soak for hours and sleep better

**Long soaks lead to dehydration and fatigue**, which disturb sleep instead. **10–15 minutes** is the guide (see our article on half-body bathing).

## Misunderstanding 4｜In winter, the healthy thing is a hot bath and straight to bed

**Very hot baths in winter are the main cause of bathing accidents**, and they **work against falling asleep too**. **Around 40°C, 1–2 hours before bed.** **Warming the changing room and the bathroom** is the safer way to deal with the cold.

## Misunderstanding 5｜Changing when you bathe makes no difference

**A bath 1–2 hours before bed is one of the few concrete daily habits the sleep guide names.** Together with **light, caffeine and a fixed wake-up time**, it is generally described as one of the first measures worth trying.
