# Q. What do people get wrong about Japanese toilets (washlets)?｜Japan Questions #321

- Shelf: `inbound` ／ URL: `/en/questions/japanese-toilets` ／ Status: **Draft — sources unverified**

The heated seat with a control panel is the first piece of Japan most visitors touch. It is also the source of a specific set of mistakes — the wrong button, the flush that is not where you expect, the slippers — that are easy to avoid once the panel is decoded.

## A. The common misunderstanding is that the panel is complicated. Four buttons matter: stop, rear wash, front wash, and flush (which may be a lever on the tank, a button on the wall, or a sensor). Over 80% of Japanese homes have a bidet toilet, and public ones follow the same logic. The remaining surprises are the separate toilet slippers, the fake flushing sound, and the squat toilets that still exist in older stations.

## The panel, decoded

| Symbol / word | Meaning | Note |
|---|---|---|
| **止** (stop) | **Stop** — the button to find first | Usually orange or red |
| **おしり** (oshiri) | Rear wash | Adjustable position and pressure |
| **ビデ** (bide) | Front wash | — |
| **やわらか** (yawaraka) | Soft rear wash | — |
| **乾燥** (kansō) | Warm-air dry | Not on all models |
| **水勢 / 位置** | Pressure / nozzle position | −/+ arrows |
| **流す 大 / 小** (nagasu dai / shō) | **Flush** full / half | May be a wall panel, a lever on the tank, a sensor, or **automatic on standing up** |
| **音姫 / 擬音** (otohime) | **Flushing sound** to mask noise | Does not flush |
| **便座** (benza) | Seat heater on/off | — |

- **Household penetration**: about **82% of two-or-more-person households** have a bidet toilet (Cabinet Office Consumer Confidence Survey, March 2025); public toilets in stations, department stores and convenience stores mostly do too.
- **Nozzles self-clean** before and after use; the water is **warmed** and the seat **heated**. The mechanism is the same across brands.
- **Toilet slippers**: in homes, ryokan and some restaurants, a separate pair sits inside the toilet doorway — **change into them at the door and back out again**. Walking away in them is the classic error.
- **Squat toilets (和式)** survive in some older stations and parks: face the **hood**, feet either side. Many public toilets now label stalls **洋式 (Western) / 和式 (Japanese)**.
- **Paper goes in the toilet**; a bin is for sanitary items only. Public toilets **provide paper but rarely hand towels** (see the handkerchief article).

> **[Verify]** Household penetration with the Cabinet Office's Consumer Confidence Survey (durable goods); button labels vary by manufacturer.

## Misunderstanding 1｜The buttons are dangerous

Nothing sprays until you press a wash button while seated (a **seat sensor** prevents spraying an empty bowl). **Find 止 (stop) first**, then experiment.

## Misunderstanding 2｜The flush is on the panel

Often it is a **lever on the tank, a button on the wall behind you, or a sensor**. If you cannot find it, look for **流す** or wave a hand at a sensor plate; some flush **automatically when you stand**.

## Misunderstanding 3｜The sound button flushes

**音姫 plays a flushing sound** for privacy. Pressing it repeatedly flushes nothing; look for 流す.

## Misunderstanding 4｜Slippers are optional

In a home or ryokan, **toilet slippers are mandatory inside and forbidden outside** the toilet room. Leaving them on into the corridor is noticed by everyone.

## Misunderstanding 5｜Squat toilets are gone

They remain in **older stations, parks and schools**; hold the **handrail**, face the hood, and note that many now have a **Western stall next door** marked 洋式.
