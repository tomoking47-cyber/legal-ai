# Q. What do people get wrong about earthquakes as a visitor to Japan?｜Japan Questions #324

- Shelf: `inbound` ／ URL: `/en/questions/earthquakes-for-visitors` ／ Status: **Draft — sources unverified**

Most visitors will feel a tremor during a two-week stay and almost none will be harmed. The country records about 2,000 felt earthquakes a year. What matters is knowing the two or three things to do in the first seconds and where information comes from, because the rules differ from what other countries teach.

## A. The common misunderstanding is that you should run outside. Japanese guidance is the opposite for buildings built to the 1981 standard: stay inside, protect your head, get under or beside sturdy furniture, and move away from windows. The phone alarm (Earthquake Early Warning) gives a few seconds; the real risks are falling objects, and — near the coast — tsunami, for which the rule is to move to high ground immediately without waiting for confirmation.

## What to do

| Moment | Action | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Alarm sounds** (phone blares, TV chime) | **Protect your head; get under a table or beside a solid wall; away from windows and shelves** | You have **seconds to tens of seconds** |
| During shaking | **Stay put.** Do not run outside; do not use elevators | Falling glass and signage outside are the main injury cause |
| In a hotel | **Open the door slightly** once shaking stops (frames can jam); shoes on (glass); torch or phone light | — |
| On a train | Trains **auto-stop**; stay seated, follow staff | — |
| **Near the coast** | **Tsunami: move to high ground or the 3rd floor and above of a concrete building immediately**, before any warning | Tsunami can arrive within minutes |
| After | **Check the JMA / NHK World app**; expect aftershocks; do not phone (networks jam) — text | — |

- Japan records about **1,500–2,000 felt earthquakes a year**; the Japan Meteorological Agency's **Earthquake Early Warning** is pushed to **all phones (including roaming ones)** for shaking of intensity 4+ or 5-lower+ depending on setting.
- **Buildings**: the **1981 New Earthquake Resistance Standard** is the dividing line; hotels and stations built since are designed not to collapse in a major quake. The **Shinkansen has never had a passenger fatality from an earthquake**, thanks to automatic braking.
- The **JMA seismic intensity scale (震度 0–7)** describes shaking at your location; **magnitude** describes the quake's energy. A magnitude 6 far away may be shindo 2 where you stand.
- **NHK World** (app, English) carries emergency information; **Safety tips** (Japan Tourism Agency app) pushes alerts in English.

> **[Verify]** Alert thresholds and intensity definitions with the JMA; tsunami guidance with the Cabinet Office; the 1981 standard with MLIT.

## Misunderstanding 1｜Run outside

**Inside a modern building is safer** than a street full of falling glass and tiles. Get under or beside something solid and cover your head.

## Misunderstanding 2｜Stand in a doorway

Old advice. Japanese guidance is **under a sturdy table or beside an interior wall**, away from anything that can fall.

## Misunderstanding 3｜A small tremor means nothing will follow

Usually true, but **aftershocks** follow large quakes for days, and rarely a first quake is a foreshock. **Keep shoes and a bag by the bed** for a few nights after a big one.

## Misunderstanding 4｜Wait for the tsunami warning before leaving the beach

**If you feel a strong or long quake near the coast, go to high ground immediately.** Warnings follow within minutes; the wave may too. Look for the **tsunami evacuation signs** (green with a wave and running figure).

## Misunderstanding 5｜Nothing can be done in advance

Five minutes on arrival: **install NHK World and Safety tips**, note the **evacuation area** sign near your hotel, keep **passport, cash, charger and shoes together** at night. That is what residents do (see the emergency-bag article).
