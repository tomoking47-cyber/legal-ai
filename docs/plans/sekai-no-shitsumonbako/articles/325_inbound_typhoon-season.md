# Q. What do people get wrong about typhoon season for travellers?｜Japan Questions #325

- Shelf: `inbound` ／ URL: `/en/questions/typhoon-season` ／ Status: **Draft — sources unverified**

Typhoons reach Japan mostly from July to October, peaking in September, and each year some visitors are stranded by cancelled trains and flights. The storms themselves are well forecast; the misunderstanding is about how transport reacts.

## A. The common misunderstanding is that a typhoon is a day of rain to sit out. Japanese rail operators now announce planned suspensions (keikaku unkyū) a day or two ahead, airlines cancel domestic flights en masse, and the Shinkansen stops. The storm passes in a day; the disruption can last two or three. Watch the JMA track from five days out, expect transport to stop before the wind arrives, and build slack into any travel in September.

## The pattern

| Item | Figure |
|---|---|
| Typhoons per year (NW Pacific) | **about 25**; **about 3 make landfall in Japan** on average; **about 11 approach** |
| Peak | **August–September** (September has the most landfalls) |
| Most affected | **Okinawa, Kyushu, the Pacific coast**; Hokkaido least |
| Duration at one place | **Roughly 6–24 hours** of strong wind and heavy rain |
| Transport | **Planned suspensions announced 1–2 days ahead** (JR since 2019); **Shinkansen stops** when wind exceeds set limits; **domestic flights cancelled** in the hundreds; **expressways closed** |
| Warnings | JMA **advisory (注意報) → warning (警報) → emergency warning (特別警報)**; **"evacuation" levels 1–5** issued by municipalities |

- **Planned suspension (計画運休)** became standard after the 2018 and 2019 typhoons: operators publish **the stop time and expected restart** so people stay home. **Restart is often the following morning**, after track inspections.
- **Typhoon Hagibis (October 2019)** cancelled **more than 1,600 flights** and stopped most rail in the Kanto region for a day; the Rugby World Cup lost matches.
- **Travel insurance** typically covers delays; airlines **rebook without fees** for weather cancellations, but seats on the next day are scarce.

> **[Verify]** Landfall statistics with the JMA's typhoon database; planned-suspension practice with JR East/West announcements.

## Misunderstanding 1｜A typhoon means a wet day; carry on

**Transport stops before the storm and restarts after inspection**; the wind itself makes walking dangerous (**flying signage, umbrellas are useless**). Plan to stay put, with food bought the day before (**convenience stores empty out of bread and onigiri**).

## Misunderstanding 2｜Trains will run until the weather is actually bad

**Planned suspensions start hours before landfall**, sometimes from the previous evening. Check the operator's site the day before; **move to your next city early or stay an extra night**.

## Misunderstanding 3｜Flights are the reliable option

**Domestic flights are cancelled en masse** and international ones delayed. Airlines rebook free, but the **next day is oversubscribed**. Give yourself a **buffer day before an international departure** in September.

## Misunderstanding 4｜The hotel will be dangerous

Modern hotels are **safe places to wait out a typhoon**; stay away from windows on the windward side, and expect the restaurant to close. **Ryokan in mountain or coastal areas may evacuate** — follow staff.

## Misunderstanding 5｜Once the rain stops it is over

**Rivers peak hours after the rain**; landslide and flood warnings continue. Trains restart after **track inspection, often the next morning**. Check **JMA and the operator**, not the sky.
