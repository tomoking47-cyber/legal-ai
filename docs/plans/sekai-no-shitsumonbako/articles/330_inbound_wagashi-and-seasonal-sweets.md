# Q. What do people get wrong about wagashi (Japanese sweets)?｜Japan Questions #330

- Shelf: `inbound` ／ URL: `/en/questions/wagashi-and-seasonal-sweets` ／ Status: **Draft — sources unverified**

Wagashi are the sweets sold in the depachika and offered with tea — beans, rice, sugar and seasonal shapes. Visitors find them beautiful and confusing: what is inside, why they change every month, how long they keep, and whether they are the right gift.

## A. The common misunderstanding is that wagashi are one kind of thing. They divide by moisture into fresh (namagashi, eaten the same day), half-dry (han-namagashi, a few days) and dry (higashi, weeks), and by season — shapes and ingredients follow the calendar (sakura in April, hydrangea in June, chestnut in autumn). The main ingredient is sweet bean paste, and the sugar level is often lower than Western pastry.

## The categories

| Type | Moisture | Keeps | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Namagashi (生菓子)** | High (30%+) | **Same day, 1–2 days** | Nerikiri (shaped bean paste), daifuku, dorayaki, sakura-mochi, kusa-mochi, warabi-mochi |
| **Han-namagashi (半生菓子)** | Medium | **3–10 days** | Yōkan (bean jelly), monaka (wafer with paste), most boxed gift sweets |
| **Higashi (干菓子)** | Low | **Weeks to months** | Rakugan (pressed sugar), senbei, konpeitō, aruheitō |

## The calendar (a sample)

| Month | Sweet | Note |
|---|---|---|
| January | Hanabira-mochi | New Year, tea ceremony |
| March–April | **Sakura-mochi**, hanami-dango | Cherry blossom |
| May | **Kashiwa-mochi**, chimaki | Children's Day |
| June | Minazuki, hydrangea nerikiri | Kyoto eats minazuki on 30 June |
| Summer | **Mizu-yōkan**, kuzu-kiri, warabi-mochi | Chilled |
| Autumn | **Kuri (chestnut) kinton**, imo (sweet potato) sweets, tsukimi dango | Harvest, moon-viewing |
| Winter | Yuzu sweets, zenzai (red bean soup) | — |

- **Anko (sweet bean paste)** is the core: **azuki** beans, sugar, salt — **koshi-an (smooth)** or **tsubu-an (chunky)**. **White bean (shiro-an)** for shaped sweets.
- Allergens: **wheat** (in some dorayaki, monaka wafers, senbei), **soy**, **egg** (castella, some dorayaki), **sesame**; **gluten-free** items are common but check the label (see the allergen article). Wagashi are almost always **vegan**, except egg-containing baked types and gelatin in some jellies.
- **Gift culture**: boxed han-namagashi from a known shop is the **default omiyage** (see the gift article); shops wrap with noshi for occasions. **Namagashi cannot travel** — eat them where you buy them.
- Sugar: a piece of yōkan (50 g) carries roughly **25–30 g of sugar**; a nerikiri **10–15 g** — comparable to or lower than Western cake by weight.

## Misunderstanding 1｜They all keep for a week

**Fresh sweets are same-day**; the beautiful nerikiri in the display case will not survive a flight. **Boxed yōkan and monaka** are the travelling kind.

## Misunderstanding 2｜"Red bean" is one flavour

**Koshi-an and tsubu-an** are different textures; **shiro-an** is a different bean; **kuri-an** adds chestnut; salt and sugar ratios vary by shop. Trying three shops' dorayaki is a real comparison.

## Misunderstanding 3｜Wagashi are very sweet

Most are **less sweet by weight than Western pastry** and designed to pair with **bitter tea** (matcha or sencha). Eaten with coffee they can seem bland; that is the pairing, not the sweet.

## Misunderstanding 4｜Any wagashi is a fine gift

**Fresh sweets are not** (they spoil); **boxed sweets from a shop with a name** are. Sets of **four** are avoided; **odd numbers or six/eight/ten** are standard.

## Misunderstanding 5｜The shapes are decoration

They are **the season**: a shop that sells sakura-mochi in September is signalling that it does not know its trade. Asking "kisetsu no wagashi wa?" (what is seasonal?) gets the right answer.
