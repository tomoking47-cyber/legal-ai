# Q. What do people get wrong about the oshiire (built-in closet) and under-bed storage?｜Japanese Everyday Wisdom #331

- Shelf: `wisdom` ／ URL: `/en/wisdom/the-oshiire-and-under-bed-storage` ／ Status: **Draft — sources unverified**

The oshiire — a deep built-in closet with a single shelf across the middle — is in almost every Japanese home built before 2000, and visitors mistake it for a wardrobe. It was designed for futon, and using it well is the main storage skill in an older Japanese flat.

## A. The common misunderstanding is that the oshiire is a badly designed wardrobe: too deep, no rail. It is a bedding closet, sized so a folded futon fits front to back on each of two levels. The way to use it is with drawer units and rolling cases sized to it, and the same logic applies to the space under a Western bed, which Japanese households treat as a drawer.

## The dimensions and the system

| Item | Standard | Use |
|---|---|---|
| **Oshiire depth** | **about 75–90 cm** (a folded futon is ~70 cm) | Deep enough for two rows of boxes |
| **Middle shelf (nakadana)** | At about **80–90 cm** height | Futon above; drawers below |
| **Top cupboard (tenbukuro)** | Above the doors, **30–40 cm** high | Seasonal items, suitcases, emergency stock |
| **Drawer units** | Sold in widths **39 / 44 / 54 cm** and depths **50–74 cm** to fit half or a third of a bay | Clothes by category, standing (see the vertical-storage article) |
| **Rolling cases** | With wheels, so the back row can be pulled forward | Seasonal clothes, bedding |
| Under-bed | Clearance **15–30 cm**; **flat rolling cases** or fitted drawers | Off-season bedding, shoes, suitcases |

- **Humidity** is the enemy: the oshiire's back wall is often an exterior wall, cold and damp. Households use **sunoko (slatted boards)** on the floor, **desiccant**, and **open the doors on dry days**; futon stored there are aired first (see the futon article).
- The **tatami-room oshiire** is being replaced in new homes by **walk-in closets with rails**, but the **modular drawer market** created for it — clear plastic units in fixed widths — now fits Western closets and under beds too.
- **Under-bed storage** works because Japanese beds are often **low platform types with 20–30 cm clearance**; lidded flat cases on wheels slide in and out and keep dust off.

> **[Verify]** Dimensions vary by builder; product widths are common retail standards.

## Misunderstanding 1｜It is a wardrobe without a rail

It is a **bedding cupboard**. Adding a tension rail works for short items, but the depth is for **boxes in two rows**, and drawers use it far better.

## Misunderstanding 2｜Fill it front to back and forget the back row

The back row becomes invisible and damp. **Rolling cases** or **two rows where the back holds off-season items** — and pull them forward at each seasonal change.

## Misunderstanding 3｜Any plastic box will do

**Measure the bay** and buy units in the standard widths that fill it exactly; leftover gaps collect clutter. Japanese organisers say "measure first, buy second".

## Misunderstanding 4｜Closed doors keep it dry

**Closed doors keep it humid.** Slatted boards, desiccant, and doors open on dry days; check the back wall for mould at the seasonal change.

## Misunderstanding 5｜Under the bed is for things you never use

Treat it as a **drawer**: flat cases on wheels for **the current off-season** (winter duvet in summer), not for things you have forgotten. Vacuum under there when you rotate.
