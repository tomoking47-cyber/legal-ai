# Q. What do people get wrong about the household first-aid box (kyūkyūbako)?｜Japanese Everyday Wisdom #332

- Shelf: `wisdom` ／ URL: `/en/wisdom/the-household-first-aid-box` ／ Status: **Draft — sources unverified**

Most Japanese homes keep a first-aid box, historically restocked by a visiting salesman (the "okigusuri" system that still exists in some regions). What goes in it, and what does not, reflects a country where a clinic is usually nearby and self-medication is regulated.

## A. The common misunderstanding is that a first-aid box is a small pharmacy. The Japanese household box is mostly for wounds, fever and stomach trouble: dressings, disinfectant, thermometer, oral rehydration, a few OTC medicines with their boxes and dates, plus the health-insurance card copies and the clinic's phone number. Anything more is what the clinic down the road is for. The box is checked twice a year and stored away from heat and children.

## A typical box

| Category | Contents | Note |
|---|---|---|
| **Wounds** | Adhesive dressings (several sizes), sterile gauze, non-stick pads, tape, elastic bandage, **hydrocolloid dressings** (widely used), disinfectant or saline, tweezers, small scissors | Wash wounds under running water first — the current guidance |
| **Fever / pain** | Thermometer, **acetaminophen** (adults and a children's formulation), cooling gel sheets | Aspirin not given to children |
| **Stomach** | Oral rehydration solution (sachets or bottles), antidiarrhoeal only as directed, digestive aid | ORS matters for children and elderly |
| **Other** | Antihistamine (OTC), eye wash, motion-sickness tablets, hot/cold packs, disposable gloves, mask | — |
| **Paper** | **Copies of health-insurance cards**, clinic and pharmacy numbers, **#7119 / #8000 / 119** written down, medication list | For anyone in the house — or a babysitter |
| **Check** | **Every 1 March and 1 September** (with the emergency kit); discard expired items | Dates on OTC boxes |

- **OTC medicines in Japan are classed 1–3** (see the OTC article); keep them **in their boxes** with the package insert — dosage and age limits are printed there.
- **Children's medicine** is dosed by **age and weight**; keep a children's acetaminophen product rather than splitting adult tablets.
- **#7119** (emergency consultation), **#8000** (children, nights and weekends) and **119** (ambulance) are the three numbers; **poison information: Japan Poison Information Center 072-727-2499 (Osaka) / 029-852-9999 (Tsukuba)**.
- The **okigusuri** (placed medicine) system — a box left in the home and restocked by a visiting vendor, paid for on use — dates from the Edo period in Toyama and still serves rural households.

> **[Verify]** Phone numbers with the Fire and Disaster Management Agency and the Japan Poison Information Center; wound-care guidance with the Japanese Society for Burn Injuries / MHLW first-aid materials.

## Misunderstanding 1｜Stock it like a pharmacy

**A clinic is usually within walking distance** and open six days a week. The box handles the **first hour**; it is not for treating illness at home over days.

## Misunderstanding 2｜Disinfectant first on every cut

Current guidance is **running water first**, then a clean dressing; strong disinfectants on healthy tissue are used less than a generation ago. **Deep, dirty or animal-bite wounds go to a clinic.**

## Misunderstanding 3｜Adult medicine, half dose, for children

**Children's formulations exist for a reason** (dose by weight, safe ingredients). Keep one, with its insert, and check the age limit.

## Misunderstanding 4｜The box is set up once

**Dressings dry out, tablets expire, children grow into new dose bands.** Twice a year, with the emergency kit (1 March, 1 September), is the household rhythm.

## Misunderstanding 5｜Everyone knows where it is

Put it **where a guest or babysitter could find it** — a labelled box in the kitchen or living room, **out of children's reach**, not in a bedroom drawer. Tape the three emergency numbers to the lid.
