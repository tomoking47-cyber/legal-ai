# Q. What do people get wrong about reusing bath water (nokoriyu)?｜Japanese Everyday Wisdom #333

- Shelf: `wisdom` ／ URL: `/en/wisdom/reusing-bath-water` ／ Status: **Draft — sources unverified**

A Japanese bath holds about 200 litres, and since the family washes before getting in, the water is clean enough to reuse. Most washing machines sold in Japan have a hose to draw from the bath. To visitors this sounds unhygienic; the numbers and the rules explain it.

## A. The common misunderstanding is that reusing bath water is dirty. Because everyone washes outside the tub first, the water after bathing is far cleaner than in a Western bath, and it is warm — which improves washing. Households use it for the wash cycle only (not the rinse), the same evening or next morning, and never for underwear on days when someone was ill. It saves about 50–100 litres per load and a little energy.

## How it is done

| Item | Detail |
|---|---|
| Bath volume | **about 180–220 L** for a standard tub |
| Washing machine intake | **50–100 L per load** for a top-loader; machines have a **bath-water pump hose (furomizu pump)** |
| Which cycle | **Wash cycle only**; **rinse with tap water** — this is the manufacturers' and government advice |
| When | **Same evening or next morning**; bacteria increase in warm standing water over 24 hours |
| Not for | **Baby clothes, underwear if someone is ill, towels you want odour-free** |
| Bath additives | **Check the additive's label** — some (with sulphur or milky agents) say "not for laundry"; many say "OK for wash cycle, not rinse" |
| Other uses | **Toilet flushing (bucket), floor washing, watering plants (if no additives), emergency reserve** (see the water-storage article) |
| Savings | **50–100 L per load; about ¥15–30 per load** in water and sewer charges at Tokyo rates |

- The **Tokyo Waterworks Bureau** lists bath-water reuse among household water-saving measures and gives the wash-only rule.
- **Bacterial counts** in bath water rise **roughly tenfold overnight**; the wash-only rule and the warm-water benefit make this acceptable for outer clothing, and the tap-water rinse leaves no residue.
- **Warm water (about 30–35°C the next morning)** dissolves detergent and lifts grease better than cold; Japanese washing machines otherwise use **cold water by default**.
- Households keep the bath **covered overnight** to retain warmth and keep dust out; the tub is the household's **emergency water** in a quake (200 L for flushing).

> **[Verify]** Water-saving figures with the Tokyo Waterworks Bureau; bacterial data with consumer-testing reports (e.g. Kokusen); additive guidance on product labels.

## Misunderstanding 1｜It is dirty water

**The family washes before entering**; the water is warm, soap-free and lightly used. It is used for the **wash**, not the rinse, and the rinse is tap water.

## Misunderstanding 2｜Use it for everything, including the rinse

**The rinse is tap water** so no residue or odour remains. Wash-only is the rule from manufacturers, waterworks and consumer bodies.

## Misunderstanding 3｜Leave the water for a couple of days

**Bacteria multiply in warm standing water**; use it **the same evening or the next morning**, then drain and clean the tub.

## Misunderstanding 4｜Bath additives make no difference

**Some additives stain fabric or damage the pump.** Read the additive's label for a laundry statement; many list it explicitly.

## Misunderstanding 5｜The saving is trivial

**50–100 L per load, every day, for a family** is 20–35 m³ a year — a meaningful share of household water. And the warm wash cleans better.
