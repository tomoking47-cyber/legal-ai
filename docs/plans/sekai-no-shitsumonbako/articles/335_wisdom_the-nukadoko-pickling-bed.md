# Q. What do people get wrong about the nukadoko (rice-bran pickling bed)?｜Japanese Everyday Wisdom #335

- Shelf: `wisdom` ／ URL: `/en/wisdom/the-nukadoko-pickling-bed` ／ Status: **Draft — sources unverified**

A tub of fermenting rice bran in the kitchen, stirred daily by hand, turning cucumbers and aubergines into pickles overnight — the nukadoko is one of the oldest pieces of Japanese household fermentation still in daily use, and one of the most misunderstood abroad.

## A. The common misunderstanding is that a nukadoko is a difficult, smelly project for enthusiasts. It is rice bran, salt and water kept alive by lactic-acid bacteria, and it asks for one thing: a daily stir to keep the surface aerated. Vegetables go in for hours to a day. Kept in the fridge it needs stirring only a few times a week, and a bed can last for decades.

## How it works

| Element | Detail |
|---|---|
| The bed | **Rice bran (nuka) + salt (about 13% of the bran weight) + water** to a miso-like consistency; kombu, chilli, and a piece of bread or vegetable scraps to start |
| Microbes | **Lactic-acid bacteria and yeasts** from the bran and air; the bed is ready in **1–2 weeks** of daily stirring with "starter" vegetables |
| Daily care | **Stir by hand once or twice a day** from the bottom — aerates the bed and keeps surface yeasts (which cause off-smells) in check |
| In the fridge | **Stir every 2–3 days**; fermentation slows; a fridge-kept bed is how most working households manage it |
| Pickling times | Cucumber **4–12 h**, aubergine **12–24 h** (with a nail or alum for colour), carrot **1–2 days**, daikon **1–2 days** |
| Salt | Add a little salt and bran as the bed dilutes with vegetable water |
| Signs | **White surface film = yeast (stir in or remove)**; **pink, green or black spots = mould (remove with margin, or discard)**; **alcohol smell = over-fermented (add salt, stir, rest)** |

- Nuka-zuke are **lacto-fermented**; unlike shallow pickles, they carry live bacteria, and the bran adds **B vitamins** to the vegetable surface.
- **Salt** in the finished pickle is about **2–3%**; a few slices are a side dish, not a salad.
- The bed's **taste changes with the house** — the household's hands, kitchen temperature and vegetables — which is why old beds are treated as heirlooms. Some sold beds claim origins of **100+ years**.
- **Regulation**: home pickling is unregulated; **commercial pickle production requires a licence since 2021** (see the pickles article).

> **[Verify]** Salt ratios and times are traditional ranges; food-safety guidance with MHLW's pickle hygiene standards.

## Misunderstanding 1｜It smells terrible

A healthy bed smells **sour and nutty**. Bad smells mean **neglect**: surface yeast or over-fermentation, fixed by stirring, salt and a day's rest.

## Misunderstanding 2｜It has to be stirred daily or it dies

**At room temperature, yes**, in summer even twice. **In the fridge, every 2–3 days** is enough; for a holiday, cover the surface with a thick layer of salt and refrigerate.

## Misunderstanding 3｜Any mould means throwing it out

**White film is yeast**, not mould — stir it in or scrape it off. **Coloured mould** is a problem: remove generously; if it recurs, start again.

## Misunderstanding 4｜It is only for cucumbers

Aubergine, daikon, carrot, celery, turnip, okra, **boiled eggs, tofu, cheese, avocado** — anything firm. Meat and fish are pickled in a separate small batch and the bran discarded.

## Misunderstanding 5｜It is a rural tradition

The fridge made it urban. **Ready-made beds in resealable bags** are sold in supermarkets; the daily stir takes 30 seconds and a bed the size of a lunchbox serves a couple.
