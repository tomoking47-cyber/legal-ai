# Q. What do people get wrong about heavy bags and the strain on the body?｜Japan Questions #359

- Shelf: `fashion` ／ URL: `/en/fashion/bag-straps-and-shoulder-load` ／ Status: **Draft — English edition, sources unverified**

Carrying a heavy bag on one shoulder every day is discussed worldwide as a cause of stiff shoulders and poor posture. In Japan, commuters and students carry heavy loads, and the weight of children's *randoseru* (the rigid school backpack used by primary pupils) became a public issue. This article sets out the official guidance and what people do about it.

## A. The biggest misunderstanding is "bag weight is something you get used to". A load carried on one side puts continuous strain on the shoulder and neck muscles and is generally described as a cause of the body tilting. Loads above 10–15% of body weight are generally described as a heavy burden. The fixes are: swap sides; spread the load with a backpack or a cross-body strap; carry less; and shorten the strap so the bag sits close to the body.

## Numbers and guidelines

| Item | Detail | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Guide weight for a bag | **10–15% of body weight or less** (for children **10% or less** is given as an international guideline) | American Academy of Pediatrics; orthopaedic guidance in various countries |
| Randoseru | Average **about 4–6 kg** (textbooks and tablet included). Can exceed **20% of a first-year pupil's body weight**; in 2018 the Ministry of Education (MEXT) issued a notice allowing **"oki-ben" (置き勉, leaving textbooks at school)** | MEXT (the notice); the weights come from private surveys |
| Commuter bag | Often **3–5 kg** with laptop, water bottle and papers | — |
| Effect of one-shoulder carrying | **The shoulder rises, the neck and shoulder muscles tense**, **the trunk tilts**. One factor in **stiff shoulders and headaches** | Standard orthopaedic explanation |
| Fixes | **Alternate sides**, **backpack (both shoulders)**, **cross-body strap held close to the body**, **shorten the strap (bag above the hips)** | As above |

- **In September 2018 MEXT notified schools nationwide that pupils may leave textbooks and other items at school ("oki-ben")**. The background was **heavier randoseru and children complaining of back and shoulder pain**.
- **A backpack spreads the load over both shoulders**, but **if the straps are long and the bag hangs below the hips, the strain on the lower back increases**. **A chest strap** adds stability.
- **On a crowded train, wear your backpack on your front** (see the article on train etiquette).

> **[Verify]** Check the MEXT notice (September 2018, "On consideration for items carried by pupils and students") and the source for the weight guidelines.

## Misunderstanding 1｜You get used to the weight

**Muscles may adapt, but a continuous load on one side is generally described as affecting posture and neck-shoulder tension**. **Simply swapping sides** makes a difference.

## Misunderstanding 2｜With a backpack, any weight is fine

**Above 10–15% of body weight it is a burden**. **Shorten the straps, keep it against your back, use the chest strap**.

## Misunderstanding 3｜A child's randoseru cannot be made lighter

**MEXT allows "oki-ben".** **Talk to the school and reduce what comes home every day**. **Some schools got heavier when tablets were introduced**, so check.

## Misunderstanding 4｜A cross-body bag is one-sided too, so it is the same as a shoulder bag

**A cross-body bag sits against the body and spreads the weight across the trunk**, so it is generally described as **less strain than a shoulder bag**. **Swapping sides** still applies.

## Misunderstanding 5｜To lighten your bag, buy a new one

**Go through the contents first**. **Take out "things I do not use every day"**, **carry a smaller water bottle**, **leave the laptop charger at work** — tips shared among Japanese commuters. Look at **the weight of the bag itself, too (leather can be over 1 kg)**.
