# Q. What are the common misunderstandings about taking breaks at work?｜Japan Questions #364

- Shelf: `health` ／ URL: `/en/health/taking-breaks-at-work` ／ Status: **Draft — English edition, sources unverified**

The feeling that "taking a break is slacking" runs deep in Japanese workplaces, and visitors often ask why Japanese people do not rest. If you work in Japan, it helps to know two things: your legal right to breaks, and what official guidance says about taking them for productivity and health. This article covers both.

## A. The biggest misunderstanding is "breaks are something you take all at once, after the work is done." The Labour Standards Act requires a 45-minute break for work over 6 hours and 60 minutes for work over 8 hours. The guideline for work with display screens (VDT work) calls for a 10–15-minute pause every hour. Short, frequent breaks are generally described as better for concentration and for the body.

## Numbers and rules

| Item | Details | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Statutory breaks | **At least 45 minutes for working time over 6 hours; at least 60 minutes for over 8 hours.** **Taken during the working day, and free for the worker to use as they wish** | Labour Standards Act, Article 34 |
| Display-screen work | **No more than 1 hour of continuous work, with a 10–15-minute pause between blocks**, plus **1–2-minute micro-breaks during the work** | MHLW Guideline on Work with Information Devices (2019) |
| Sitting | **Stand up once every 30 minutes to 1 hour** | Physical Activity Guide 2023 (sedentary behaviour) |
| Eyes | **Every 20 minutes, look 6 m away for 20 seconds** (the 20-20-20 rule, a guide used abroad); **step away from the screen at least once an hour** | General advice from ophthalmologists |
| Lunch break | **If you nap, keep it to 15–30 minutes, early in the afternoon** | Sleep Guide 2023 |
| What a break should contain | **Step away from the screen, stand and move, look at daylight, drink water.** **A break spent staring at your phone is not a break for your eyes or your posture** | As above |

- **The law requires that break time be "free to use as the worker wishes".** **A lunch break spent covering the phones** may not count as a break.
- The idea that **concentration lasts about 90 minutes at most** (the "ultradian rhythm") is well known, but **individual variation is large**, and **the guideline's once-an-hour is the practical yardstick**.
- **Japan's paid-leave take-up rate was about 65% (2023)**, against a government target of 70%. **Reluctance to "rest"** shows up in how breaks are taken too.

## Misunderstanding 1｜Taking breaks lowers productivity

**Short pauses every hour are shown to improve afternoon concentration and reduce errors.** **That is the basis on which the guideline calls for pauses.**

## Misunderstanding 2｜One long lunch break is enough

**Separate from the statutory break, short pauses every hour** are described as necessary for the body (eyes, neck, lower back). **60 minutes of continuous screen work** is the upper guide.

## Misunderstanding 3｜Looking at your phone during a break is a break

**Your eyes and posture get no rest.** **Step away from the screen, stand up and look into the distance.** That is what a break consists of.

## Misunderstanding 4｜A lunch break spent covering the phones is a break

**The legal requirement is that it be "free to use as you wish".** **Waiting time on standby can be judged to be working time.**

## Misunderstanding 5｜Not being able to take breaks is a personal failing

**Workplace systems (timers, standing meetings, no-overtime days, a break room) change it.** **Consulting the health and safety committee or the company's occupational physician (産業医, sangyō-i)** is an established route.
