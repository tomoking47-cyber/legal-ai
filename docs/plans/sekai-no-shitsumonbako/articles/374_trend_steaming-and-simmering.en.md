# Q. What are the common misunderstandings about steaming and simmering (nimono)?｜Japan Questions #374

- Shelf: `trend` ／ URL: `/en/trend/steaming-and-simmering` ／ Status: **Draft — English edition, sources unverified**

Steaming (mushi, 蒸し) and simmering (nimono, 煮物) are the basic cooking methods of Japanese food, yet many people avoid them as "slow" or "difficult". Many households abroad do not own a steamer, and we are often asked how steaming differs from the microwave. Here is how the heat works, and where the food-safety line sits.

## A. The biggest misunderstanding is "steaming needs a dedicated steamer, and simmered dishes taste better the longer they cook." Steaming needs only a pot, a plate and water — a frying pan with a little water and a lid is enough. Simmering works by "cooking through quickly, then letting the flavour soak in as the dish cools"; long cooking breaks the food up and leaches out nutrients. For both, "heated through to the centre (75°C for 1 minute)" is the safety line.

## Steaming

| Item | Detail |
|---|---|
| Equipment | **A steamer, or a pot + a heatproof plate raised on a stand (a small dish or a ring of foil) + a lid.** **A frying pan with 1 cm of water and a lid** also works |
| How it works | **Steam at 100°C (212°F); nothing leaches into the water.** **No oil needed** |
| Suited to | Vegetables (broccoli, kabocha squash, sweet potato), fish, chicken, shūmai dumplings, chawanmushi (savoury egg custard), steamed buns |
| Time guide | Broccoli **3–4 min**, kabocha (2 cm cubes) **8–10 min**, chicken breast (200 g) **12–15 min**, fish fillet **8–10 min** |
| Versus the microwave | **The microwave heats by vibrating the water inside the food (uneven, tends to dry out)**; **steam heats evenly from outside and keeps food moist** |
| Safety | **Steam can scald when you lift the lid.** **Open the lid tilted away from you** |

## Simmering

| Item | Detail |
|---|---|
| Principle | **Simmer briefly in dashi stock and seasonings, turn off the heat, and the flavour goes in as it cools** (**"the flavour soaks in as it cools"**) |
| Otoshibuta (drop lid) | **A lid that sits directly on the food, so even a small amount of liquid reaches everything and the pieces do not break up** (baking parchment or foil works too) |
| Order of seasoning | **"Sa-shi-su-se-so" (sugar → salt → vinegar → soy sauce → miso)** is a rule of thumb: **sugar, with its large molecules, goes in first; strongly aromatic seasonings go in last** |
| Time | **Root vegetables 15–20 min; leafy greens and tofu a few minutes.** **Long cooking breaks food up and leaches out vitamins** |
| Storage | **Simmered dishes hold a lot of liquid and spoil easily.** **Eat the same day or the next, refrigerated.** **Reheat until the whole dish is boiling** (see our Clostridium perfringens article) |

> **[Verify]** Cooking times are guides that vary with the size of the ingredients and the pot. Check the food-poisoning prevention temperature against Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare (MHLW) materials.

## Misunderstanding 1｜You cannot steam without a steamer

**A pot, a plate, water and a lid** are enough. **1 cm of water in a frying pan, a plate on top, lid on** also works. So do **the steam setting on a microwave, or a silicone steamer.**

## Misunderstanding 2｜Warming in the microwave is the same as steaming

**The microwave heats from the inside and tends to give uneven, dry results**; **steam heats evenly from outside and keeps food moist.** **The colour and texture of broccoli** show the difference.

## Misunderstanding 3｜The longer a nimono simmers, the more flavour goes in

**The flavour goes in as the dish cools.** The basic method is **to cook through quickly, turn off the heat and leave it.** **Long cooking breaks the food up and leaches out nutrients.**

## Misunderstanding 4｜Simmered dishes keep well

**They hold a lot of liquid, and leaving them at room temperature causes food poisoning.** **Refrigerate and eat the same day or the next; reheat until the whole dish is boiling.**

## Misunderstanding 5｜Ignore "sa-shi-su-se-so" and the dish fails

It is a **rule of thumb.** There is **a scientific reason to add sugar first (large molecules penetrate slowly)**, but **many cookery writers say strict order makes little difference.** The practical point is **to add soy sauce and miso last so their aroma survives.**
