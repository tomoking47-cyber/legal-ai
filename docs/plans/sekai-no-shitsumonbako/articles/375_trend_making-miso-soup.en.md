# Q. What are the common misunderstandings about making miso soup?｜Japan Questions #375

- Shelf: `trend` ／ URL: `/en/trend/making-miso-soup` ／ Status: **Draft — English edition, sources unverified**

Miso soup is the foundation of the Japanese table, but two common mistakes — boiling it after the miso goes in, and putting all the ingredients in at once — spoil the flavour and push up the salt. This article focuses on the soup itself; see our separate article on making dashi stock.

## A. The biggest misunderstanding is "keep the soup bubbling after the miso is stirred in." Heat drives off miso's aroma; boil it and the flavour flattens, leaving only saltiness. So miso is dissolved in just before the heat goes off, and the soup is never brought to the boil. One bowl holds about 1.3–2 g of salt; make the dashi work harder, add plenty of ingredients and use less liquid, and it fits within the daily target (around 7 g).

## The basics

| Step | What to do | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Make dashi** (bonito, kombu, dried sardines, or granulated dashi) | Umami makes up for salt |
| 2 | **Start with slow-cooking ingredients (root vegetables, potatoes) and simmer.** **Leafy greens, tofu and wakame seaweed go in last** | Prevents breaking up and overcooking |
| 3 | **Lower the heat and dissolve the miso in a little dashi before adding it** (in a misokoshi strainer or a ladle) | Prevents lumps |
| 4 | **Do not let it boil.** **Turn off the heat just before boiling point** | Heat drives off miso's aroma |
| Amount | **One bowl (150–180 mL) with 1 tablespoon of miso (about 18 g) → about 2 g salt equivalent.** **With 2/3 tablespoon (12 g), about 1.3 g** | Salt |
| Ingredients | **Plenty of vegetables, tofu, mushrooms and seaweed** → **doubles as a side dish**, and **less liquid means less salt** | Gudakusan (具だくさん, "packed with ingredients") |
| Storage | **Eat on the day it is made.** **In the fridge until the next day**; **reheat to just below boiling** (tofu and potato spoil quickly) | Food-poisoning prevention |

- **Salt in miso soup**: the National Health and Nutrition Survey ranks **soups among the top sources of salt in the Japanese diet.** **One bowl a day, packed with ingredients, with the dashi doing the work** is the standard way to cut salt (see our salt article).
- **Miso with dashi already added (dashi-iri miso, だし入り味噌)** is convenient, but **refrigerate it once opened** (the dashi component spoils easily). **Many granulated dashi products contain salt**, so **use less miso.**
- **Types of miso**: **white miso (sweet, lower in salt), red miso (higher in salt), and blended (awase).** **Regional differences** are large (see our miso article).

> **[Verify]** The salt figures are estimates based on the salt equivalent of miso in the Standard Tables of Food Composition in Japan (8th revised edition). Check the contribution of soups against National Health and Nutrition Survey materials.

## Misunderstanding 1｜Boil the soup after adding the miso so the flavour blends

**The aroma goes and only saltiness remains.** **Stir the miso in just before turning off the heat, and never let it boil.**

## Misunderstanding 2｜Put all the ingredients in together

**Root vegetables first; leafy greens, tofu and wakame last.** **Wakame can even go in after the heat is off.**

## Misunderstanding 3｜Miso soup is high in salt, so better not to drink it

**About 1.3–2 g a bowl**; **one bowl a day, packed with ingredients,** stays within the target. **The benefit of getting vegetables, seaweed and tofu** outweighs it.

## Misunderstanding 4｜Make a batch and reheat it every day

**Soup with tofu or potato spoils quickly, and leaving it at room temperature causes food poisoning.** **Refrigerate until the next day; reheat to just below boiling.** One household trick: **refrigerate only the simmered stock with the ingredients, and add the miso just before drinking.**

## Misunderstanding 5｜With granulated dashi, use the same amount of miso

**Many granulated dashi products contain salt**, so **use less miso** or **choose a salt-free type.** Check **the salt equivalent on the label.**
