# Q. What are the common misunderstandings about using and storing fresh and dried herbs?｜Japan Questions #377

- Shelf: `trend` ／ URL: `/en/trend/herbs-fresh-and-dried` ／ Status: **Draft — English edition, sources unverified**

Basil, coriander (sold in Japan as pakuchī, パクチー), ōba (大葉, the perilla or shiso leaf), negi (Japanese long onion) — aromatic leaves are used in small amounts and easily left over. Here we sort out fresh versus dried, how to store them, and how Japan's yakumi (薬味, fresh condiment herbs) culture differs from herb use elsewhere.

## A. The biggest misunderstanding is "dried herbs are a substitute for fresh, in the same amount." Dried herbs are concentrated but have lost their volatile aromas, so the basic rule is about one-third the amount of fresh, added early in cooking. Fresh herbs go in at the end. For storage, ōba, coriander and negi keep in the fridge wrapped in damp kitchen paper in a container; basil stands in water at room temperature; leftovers are best chopped and frozen, or kept in oil.

## When to use which, and how to store

| Item | Fresh herbs | Dried herbs |
|---|---|---|
| Aroma | Rich in volatile aromas. **For finishing and eating raw** | Volatiles reduced; **add early in stews and braises** |
| Conversion | — | **About one-third of fresh** (1 tablespoon dried ≈ 3 tablespoons fresh) |
| Storage | **Ōba, coriander, negi, parsley: wrap in damp kitchen paper, keep in a container in the fridge, 1–2 weeks** ／ **Basil: stand in water at room temperature (it blackens in the fridge)** ／ **Rosemary, thyme: fridge, 2 weeks** | **Airtight, dark, room temperature, 6 months to 1 year** (see our spices article) |
| Leftovers | **Chop and freeze (ice-cube tray with water or oil)**, **in oil (fridge, 1–2 weeks)**, **pastes such as pesto (freezer)** | — |
| Japanese yakumi | **Ōba, myōga (ginger bud), negi, ginger, wasabi, mitsuba (Japanese wild parsley), kinome (young sanshō leaf)**: **raw, at the finish, in small amounts.** Dried forms are hardly used | — |

- **Herbs or garlic kept in oil at room temperature carry a risk of botulism**; keep them **in the fridge for 1–2 weeks, or freeze** (see our garlic article).
- **Basil turns black in the fridge** (chilling injury). **Stand the stems in a glass of water at room temperature**, or **process it straight away.**
- **Ōba (green shiso)** is Japan's most typical yakumi, **sold at 10 leaves for a few tens of yen.** A widely shared trick: **stand the stems in water and keep the leaves in a container in the fridge**, and they last **2 weeks.**
- **Food safety**: **wash fresh herbs well under running water** (soil, insects). **Food poisoning from fresh herbs (coriander and others) has been reported abroad**, so **washing comes before eating them raw.**

> **[Verify]** The conversion ratio is a general guide from cookery and herb associations. Check the botulism warning against Food Safety Commission of Japan materials.

## Misunderstanding 1｜Dried herbs give the same aroma in the same amount as fresh

**Dried herbs are concentrated — use one-third — but the volatile aromas are gone**, so **they suit slow cooking, while fresh is for finishing.**

## Misunderstanding 2｜All herbs go in the fridge

**Basil turns black in the fridge.** **Stand it in water at room temperature.** **Ōba and coriander go in the fridge, kept damp.**

## Misunderstanding 3｜Leftover herbs can only be thrown away

**Chop and freeze (ice-cube tray), keep in oil (fridge, short term), or make a paste (freezer).** **Drying** (parsley, rosemary) also works.

## Misunderstanding 4｜In oil, herbs keep for months at room temperature

**Oil with no oxygen is exactly the condition in which botulinum bacteria multiply.** **Fridge for 1–2 weeks, or freeze.**

## Misunderstanding 5｜No need to wash — the aroma is lost anyway

**They carry soil, insects and microbes.** **Wash well under running water and pat dry before use.** Especially **coriander and basil eaten raw.**
