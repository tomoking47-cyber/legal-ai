# Q. What do people get wrong about pollen season as a visitor to Japan?｜Japan Questions #381

- Shelf: `inbound` ／ URL: `/en/questions/pollen-season-for-visitors` ／ Status: **Draft — sources unverified**

Spring is the most popular time to visit Japan and the worst time for the roughly 40% of the population with cedar pollen allergy. Visitors who have never reacted to pollen at home find themselves sneezing in Kyoto and wonder what is happening.

## A. The common misunderstanding is that hay fever is a local problem that does not affect visitors. Japanese cedar (sugi) and cypress (hinoki) release pollen in quantities and over a duration few other countries match — February to May in most of the main island — and people with no prior allergy can react to the sheer load. Masks, glasses, antihistamines from a drugstore, and knowing the daily forecast make the difference.

## The season

| Region | Cedar (sugi) | Cypress (hinoki) | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Tokyo / Kanto** | **early Feb – early Apr** | **late Mar – early May** | Peak mid-March; cherry blossom coincides with the transition |
| Kyoto / Osaka / Kansai | mid-Feb – early Apr | late Mar – early May | Similar |
| Kyushu | early Feb – late Mar | Mar – Apr | Earliest start |
| Tohoku | Mar – Apr | Apr – May | Later |
| **Hokkaido** | **Almost no cedar**; birch (May) instead | — | The pollen-free spring destination |
| **Okinawa** | **No cedar** | — | Same |

- About **40% of Japanese people** have cedar pollen allergy (2019 nationwide survey, up from 16% in 1998). The government's **2023 "Pollen Allergy Countermeasures"** plan aims to cut cedar forest area and improve forecasts.
- Daily **pollen forecasts** appear on every weather app and TV bulletin; the Japan Weather Association and private services publish a **five-level scale** ("very high" is common in March). Pollen counts are highest on **warm, dry, windy days and the day after rain**.
- **Drugstores** sell second-generation antihistamines (fexofenadine, loratadine, cetirizine) **over the counter**, plus eye drops, nasal sprays, and **pollen-blocking glasses**.

> **[Verify]** Prevalence figures with the Japanese Society of Otorhinolaryngology's nationwide survey; season dates with the Ministry of the Environment's pollen information; the 2023 plan with the Cabinet Office.

## Misunderstanding 1｜I do not have hay fever, so this will not affect me

**Japanese cedar pollen loads are extreme**; first-time reactions in visitors are common. Symptoms are runny nose, itchy eyes, sneezing, fatigue — not a cold if there is no fever.

## Misunderstanding 2｜Masks are for infection control

In spring, **masks are for pollen** (see the masks article). A non-woven mask and **glasses** (any glasses help; pollen-blocking frames help more) cut exposure substantially.

## Misunderstanding 3｜It is a few weeks in April

**February to May** in most of Honshu, with a cedar peak in March and cypress running into May. Cherry-blossom week sits in the middle.

## Misunderstanding 4｜You need a prescription for relief

**Second-generation antihistamines are OTC** in Japan (Class 2). Ask the pharmacist for **花粉症の薬 (kafunshō no kusuri)**; bring your usual medication if you have one, within the import limits.

## Misunderstanding 5｜Nowhere in Japan is pollen-free in spring

**Hokkaido and Okinawa have virtually no cedar or cypress.** Hokkaido's birch season starts in May. Sufferers plan spring trips there for exactly this reason.
