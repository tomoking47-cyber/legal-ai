# Q. What do people get wrong about Japanese addresses and finding a place?｜Japan Questions #386

- Shelf: `inbound` ／ URL: `/en/questions/japanese-addresses-and-finding-places` ／ Status: **Draft — sources unverified**

"Turn left at the second convenience store" is not a joke in Japan: most streets have no names, and addresses describe an area, a block and a building number that runs in order of registration, not position. Maps solved most of it; the rest needs one idea.

## A. The common misunderstanding is that a Japanese address tells you where on a street a building is. It does not name a street at all. It names, from large to small, prefecture → city or ward → district (chō) → block (chōme) → sub-block (ban) → building (gō). Blocks are numbered, buildings within a block are numbered in the order they were registered, and the last number is what you look for on the small metal plates on buildings and poles.

## Reading an address

| Element | Example (written large → small) | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Prefecture | **東京都** Tokyo-to | — |
| City / ward | **千代田区** Chiyoda-ku | Ward (in Tokyo's 23), or city (-shi) |
| District | **丸の内** Marunouchi | A named area, not a street |
| **Chōme** | **1丁目** 1-chōme | A numbered block group within the district |
| **Ban** | **9番** 9-ban | A block or sub-block |
| **Gō** | **1号** 1-gō | The building; numbers **follow registration order around the block**, not linear position |
| Building name and room | **〇〇ビル 3F / 302** | Essential for flats and offices |
| Shorthand | **丸の内1-9-1** | Chōme-ban-gō written with hyphens; **the most useful string to paste into a map app** |

- **Postal codes (〒 seven digits, e.g. 100-0005)** identify the district; entering the postcode in a map app narrows the search to a few blocks.
- **Block-number plates (街区表示板)** — small green or blue metal plates on buildings and utility poles — show **chōme-ban** (e.g. 1-9); **building plates (住居番号表示板)** show the gō. Look up at eye level on corners.
- **Street names exist** only for a few major roads (Aoyama-dōri, Omotesandō) and in **Kyoto**, which uses a **grid with intersections** (e.g. "Shijō-Kawaramachi") and its own directional suffixes (agaru/sagaru = north/south of).
- **Landmark navigation** is what residents use: the police box (kōban) with its area map, convenience stores, stations and exits. **Station exit numbers** (e.g. Shinjuku "West Exit", Tokyo Metro "Exit A5") are the most important navigation unit in a city.

> **[Verify]** Address system with the Ministry of Internal Affairs and Communications (住居表示制度); postal codes with Japan Post.

## Misunderstanding 1｜The number tells you how far along the street

**Buildings are numbered around a block in registration order**, so 3 may be next to 10. Find the block (chōme-ban), then walk around it reading plates.

## Misunderstanding 2｜Paste the romanised address into a map

**Paste the Japanese**, or at least the **hyphenated numbers with the district name**; romanisations vary and map apps handle Japanese best. The **postcode** is the fastest key.

## Misunderstanding 3｜Ask a passer-by for the street

There is no street to name. **Ask for a landmark** ("Sebun-Irebun no chikaku desu ka?" — near the 7-Eleven?) or show the map pin. **Kōban police boxes exist to give directions** and have an area map.

## Misunderstanding 4｜The station is one place

**Large stations have dozens of exits**, hundreds of metres apart. The **exit number** in an address or booking (e.g. "Exit B2") saves ten minutes underground.

## Misunderstanding 5｜Kyoto works like Tokyo

**Kyoto uses a grid**: addresses name an intersection and a direction (north/south/east/west of it), plus a historic neighbourhood name. Locals navigate by the street grid; the numbered system is secondary.
