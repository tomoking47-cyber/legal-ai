# Q. What do people get wrong about making onigiri at home?｜Japanese Everyday Wisdom #397

- Shelf: `wisdom` ／ URL: `/en/wisdom/making-onigiri-at-home` ／ Status: **Draft — sources unverified**

Onigiri — rice pressed around a filling, wrapped in seaweed — is the Japanese packed lunch, the convenience-store bestseller and the first thing children learn to make. Abroad it gets treated as sushi's cousin. It is not, and the differences are what make it work.

## A. The common misunderstanding is that onigiri is sushi rice shaped into a triangle. Onigiri uses plain, unseasoned, freshly cooked rice — no vinegar — pressed firmly but not compacted, salted on the outside, with a small filling that keeps, and it is designed to be eaten hours later at room temperature. Shaping through plastic wrap rather than with bare hands is now the hygiene standard, and the seaweed goes on at the last minute if you want it crisp.

## How it is made

| Step | Detail | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Rice** | **Japanese short-grain**, freshly cooked, **slightly firmer** than for eating; **hot but handleable**; **no vinegar or sugar** | Sushi rice is a different dish; onigiri rice must hold and taste plain against the filling |
| **Hands** | **Wet hands, salt on the palms** — or, now standard, **shape through plastic wrap** | **Staphylococcus from hands** is the classic onigiri food-poisoning route; wrap avoids it |
| **Shape** | **Triangle, round or barrel**; press **firmly enough to hold, loosely enough that grains stay distinct** — about **3 presses per side** | Over-compacted rice is hard; under-pressed falls apart |
| **Filling** | **1 teaspoon** of something salty or preserved: **umeboshi, salted salmon, kombu, okaka (bonito with soy), tuna-mayo, tarako** | Salt and preservation keep it safe for hours; wet fillings soak the rice |
| **Salt** | On the surface, **about a pinch per onigiri** | Flavour and mild preservation |
| **Nori** | **Wrap just before eating** for crisp; **wrap when making** for soft (traditional) | Convenience-store film keeps them separate for this reason |
| **Cooling** | **Let steam escape before wrapping** in cling film or foil | Trapped steam = soggy and warm = bacteria |
| **Keeping** | **Room temperature a few hours** (the point); **refrigerated rice hardens** — if you must, microwave briefly before eating; **freeze** without nori, reheat | Onigiri is made to be eaten hours later at room temperature; chilling is what hardens the rice |

- **Food safety**: the Ministry of Health's guidance for bento applies — **clean hands or wrap, cook filling through, cool before closing, keep out of heat** (see the bento article). **Salted and pickled fillings** are the traditional safety margin; **mayonnaise fillings need refrigeration in summer**.
- **Rice for onigiri** is often cooked with **slightly less water**; **"onigiri rice"** is sold as such in some supermarkets.
- **Yaki-onigiri** (grilled, brushed with soy or miso) is the way to use day-old rice.

> **[Verify]** Food-safety points with the MHLW six-point guidance; the rest is conventional technique.

## Misunderstanding 1｜Use sushi rice

**Plain rice, no vinegar.** Onigiri and sushi share a grain and nothing else; vinegared onigiri tastes wrong to any Japanese eater.

## Misunderstanding 2｜Bare hands are traditional and fine

**Traditional, yes; the hygiene standard now is plastic wrap** — staph food poisoning from hand-shaped rice balls is a known pattern. Wet, salted hands if you insist, and eat within hours.

## Misunderstanding 3｜Pack the rice tight so it holds

**Three firm presses per side**, no more. Compacted onigiri is dense and unpleasant; the grains should still be visible.

## Misunderstanding 4｜Refrigerate them for tomorrow

**Cold rice hardens** (starch retrogradation). Onigiri are made to eat **within the day at room temperature**. For tomorrow, **freeze without nori** and microwave.

## Misunderstanding 5｜The filling should be generous

**A teaspoon.** The rice is the food; the filling is seasoning. Wet or large fillings soak through and the onigiri falls apart.
