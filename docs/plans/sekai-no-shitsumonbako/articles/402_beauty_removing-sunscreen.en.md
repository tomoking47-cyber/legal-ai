# Q. What are the common misunderstandings about removing sunscreen?｜Japan Questions #402

- Shelf: `beauty` ／ URL: `/en/beauty/removing-sunscreen` ／ Status: **Draft — English edition, sources unverified**

More people now wear sunscreen every day, and with it come the questions: "Does it come off with soap?", "Do I need a cleansing oil?", "Is leftover sunscreen bad for my skin?" Japanese sunscreens carry labels that answer most of this, but they are in Japanese. Here we sort it out by product type and label wording.

## A. The biggest misunderstanding is "any sunscreen washes off with a face wash." How you remove it depends on how the product is designed. Products labelled "石けんで落ちる" (comes off with soap) do wash off with a face wash or body wash; waterproof and high-SPF products usually need a cleansing oil, balm or gel. Leftover sunscreen is generally described as one cause of blocked pores and dryness, but scrubbing hard to get it off also burdens the skin.

## Label wording and how to remove it

| Label | How to remove | Typical products |
|---|---|---|
| **「石けんで落ちる」「洗顔料で落とせる」 (comes off with soap / face wash)** | **Ordinary face wash or body wash** | Mostly mineral (physical) filters and lower SPF. **Many children's sunscreens** |
| **No such label, or 「クレンジングをお使いください」 (please use a cleanser)** | **Cleanser (oil, balm, gel, etc.) → face wash** | Common for **waterproof, SPF50+, and primer-type products** |
| **「UV耐水性★／★★」「ウォータープルーフ」「汗・水に強い」 (UV water resistance ★/★★ / waterproof / sweat- and water-resistant)** | **Most need a cleanser** | Film-forming ingredients (silicones, etc.) make them water-resistant. **For products shipped from 1 December 2024 onwards, sunscreens claiming water resistance must carry the 「UV耐水性★／★★」 (UV water resistance ★/★★) label under the Japan Cosmetic Industry Association's new standard based on ISO 18861** |
| **Sprays and sticks** | Follow the product label | Anything sprayed on hair comes off with shampoo |
| **Body sunscreen** | **Body wash** if labelled soap-removable; if waterproof, **a body cleanser, or work in a body milk first** | Back and feet are the usual leftover spots |

- **Leftover sunscreen** is generally described as **mixing with sebum, sitting in pores, and contributing to acne and dullness** (the usual dermatological explanation). But **"sunscreen is bad for skin" is not the point — "sleeping with it on" is**.
- **Scrubbing too hard** causes the opposite problems: **dryness, redness and friction-induced pigmentation**. **Use enough cleanser, work it in gently, rinse with lukewarm water** (see our cleansing article).
- **Whether you need a double cleanse (W洗顔: cleanser plus face wash)** depends on the product. If it is labelled **「W洗顔不要」 (no second wash needed)**, one step is enough.

> **[Verify]** Label categories and removal methods should follow each product's own label. Check the dermatological explanations against Japanese Dermatological Association materials.

## Misunderstanding 1｜A face wash removes all of them

**Products without a "comes off with soap" label often need a cleanser**. **Read the label**.

## Misunderstanding 2｜Leftover sunscreen is no big deal

**Residual film plus sebum in the pores is generally described as one cause of acne and dullness**. **Remove it the same day**.

## Misunderstanding 3｜Scrub to get it all off

**Friction causes dryness and pigmentation**. **Use plenty of cleanser, give it time to work in (30 seconds to 1 minute)**, and **lukewarm water**.

## Misunderstanding 4｜Body sunscreen comes off in the bath

**Waterproof formulas can remain after body wash alone**. **Work in a body milk or body cleanser first**. **The back and the tops of the feet** are easily forgotten.

## Misunderstanding 5｜Hard-to-remove sunscreen is worse for your skin

**Being hard to remove is the result of a "sweat- and water-resistant" design**, and **not harmful in itself**. **Match the product to the occasion (sea, sport) and remove it the matching way**. For everyday use, a **"comes off with soap"** type is the practical choice.
