# Q. What are the common misunderstandings about how often and how much to do strength training?｜Japan Questions #429

- Shelf: `health` ／ URL: `/en/health/strength-training-frequency` ／ Status: **Draft — English edition, sources unverified**

In 2023, the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare (MHLW) guide stated "2–3 days a week" of strength training for all adults for the first time, and interest in Japan rose. Here we sort out ideas such as "every day is best", "it is dangerous for older people" and "more reps is better", using the guide and standard exercise physiology.

## A. The biggest misunderstanding is "strength training works better the more days you do it." Muscles grow stronger by resting after a load, and the same muscle group is generally described as needing 48–72 hours to recover. The MHLW's 2023 guide recommends strength training 2–3 days a week for both adults and older people: 10–15 reps per exercise, 2–3 sets, stopping "with a little left in reserve". For older people in particular, strength training matters for preventing falls and the need for long-term care.

## The 2023 guide

| Group | Frequency | Content | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Adults (18–64)** | **2–3 days a week** | Exercises using the major muscle groups (squats, push-ups, core). **10–15 reps make one set; 1–3 sets a day** | 2–3 days a week = Physical Activity and Exercise Guide 2023; reps and sets = e-Health Net, "Resistance exercise" |
| **Older people (65 and over)** | **2–3 days a week** | Strength training plus **balance exercises** (one-leg standing etc.). Do it safely: **use a chair, keep a hand on the wall** | Same |
| Recovery | — | **Leave 48–72 hours between sessions for the same muscle group** (on consecutive days, split body parts) | Exercise physiology |
| Load | — | **Stop with "2–3 more reps" in reserve.** **If your form breaks down, that is too many** | Same |
| Breathing | — | **Do not hold your breath** (raises blood pressure). **Breathe out on the effort** | Same |
| Equipment | — | **Your own body weight is enough to start.** Add dumbbells or resistance bands step by step | — |
| Existing conditions | — | **High blood pressure, heart disease, joint problems**: ask your doctor. **Stop if it hurts** | — |

- The 2023 guide states that **strength training is associated with lower risk of death, lifestyle diseases and needing long-term care**, and sets **2–3 days a week separately from aerobic exercise such as walking**. **Among people in Japan aged 18 and over, only about 15–18% do any strength training even once a year** (Sasakawa Sports Foundation, *Sports Life Data*), and the rate is **higher in their 20s and lower in middle and older age**.
- **Muscle mass is generally described as falling about 1% a year from the 30s**, and **sarcopenia (muscle loss) in old age** is a main cause of falls and needing care (see our article on locomo). **Many studies show that strength increases in older people who train.**
- **Protein**: **spread across three meals, not only after exercise** (see our protein article). **Protein powder is a supplement for when meals do not provide enough.**

> **[Verify]** Check the guide's strength-training text and the participation rate against MHLW materials. The recovery time and load guidelines are standard exercise-physiology statements.

## Misunderstanding 1｜Every day gives the best results

**The same muscle group needs 48–72 hours to recover.** **2–3 days a week, or split body parts.**

## Misunderstanding 2｜More reps is better

**10–15 reps × 2–3 sets, with something in reserve.** **Reps with broken form cause injury.**

## Misunderstanding 3｜Strength training is dangerous for older people

**The guide recommends 2–3 days a week for those 65 and over too.** **Use a chair or wall and combine it with balance exercises.** **Not training is the greater risk of falls and needing care.**

## Misunderstanding 4｜If I walk, I don't need strength training

**Walking is aerobic exercise; strength (especially upper body and core) is separate.** **The guide recommends both.**

## Misunderstanding 5｜You need equipment and a gym

**Squats, push-ups, planks, calf raises**: your own body weight is enough to start. Combining these with **rajio taisō (radio calisthenics) and stairs** (see the articles on each) is what people in Japan actually do.
