# Q. What do people get wrong about accessible travel in Japan?｜Japan Questions #442

- Shelf: `inbound` ／ URL: `/en/questions/accessible-travel` ／ Status: **Draft — sources unverified**

Japan's cities are more accessible than their density suggests — elevators at nearly every station, tactile paving on every pavement, accessible toilets everywhere — and less accessible in their older buildings, restaurants and ryokan. Knowing which is which decides the trip.

## A. The common misunderstanding is that Japan is either fully accessible or hopelessly stair-bound. Public infrastructure built or renovated since the 2006 Barrier-Free Act is strong: stations have elevators and staff assistance, trains have wheelchair spaces, and multipurpose toilets are standard. Private buildings — small restaurants, older hotels, temples — often are not. Planning around stations, department stores and modern hotels works; spontaneity in old districts does not.

## What is reliably accessible

| Setting | Status | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Stations** | **About 95% of stations with 3,000+ daily users are step-free** (elevators or ramps); **tactile paving universal**; **staff bring a ramp for the train gap on request** | Ask at the ticket gate; allow 10 minutes |
| **Trains** | **Wheelchair spaces** in designated cars (marked); **Shinkansen has wheelchair seats and accessible toilets** (book by phone or at a counter; some online) | Reserve **2 days ahead** for Shinkansen wheelchair spaces |
| **Buses** | **Low-floor buses with ramps** in cities; driver assists | Rural buses vary |
| **Toilets** | **Multipurpose toilets (多機能トイレ)** in stations, department stores, convenience stores, parks — with grab bars, ostomate facilities | Among the world's best coverage |
| **Department stores, malls, museums** | Elevators, accessible toilets, wheelchair loan | — |
| **Hotels** | **Universal rooms** required in hotels over 50 rooms (**1% of rooms**, raised from 1 room in 2019); **business hotels often have 1–2** | Book early; specify needs |
| **Taxis** | **Universal-design taxis (JPN Taxi, ramp-equipped)** are now a large share of Tokyo's fleet | Hail or app; drivers set up the ramp |

## What is often not

- **Small restaurants and bars** (steps, narrow doors, no accessible toilet); **older ryokan** (tatami, steps, communal baths); **temples and castles** (gravel, steps, historic structures — though many major sites have paved routes and some elevators); **old shopping streets**.
- **Tactile paving and audio signals** serve visually impaired travellers well; **hearing loops** are rarer, but **written communication is culturally easy** (staff readily write things down).

> **[Verify]** Station accessibility figures with MLIT's barrier-free statistics; hotel room requirements with the 2019 amendment to the Barrier-Free Act enforcement order.

## Misunderstanding 1｜Japan is stair-bound

**Public transport and public buildings are among the most accessible in the world** since the Barrier-Free Act. The gaps are in **private, older and historic** spaces.

## Misunderstanding 2｜You have to manage the train gap yourself

**Station staff bring a portable ramp** and radio ahead to your destination so staff meet you there. Ask at the gate; it is routine.

## Misunderstanding 3｜Wheelchair spaces on the Shinkansen are first come, first served

**They are reserved seats, limited in number, and best booked 2 days ahead** by phone, at a counter, or online where available. Demand is high in peak seasons.

## Misunderstanding 4｜Accessible toilets are rare

**Multipurpose toilets are everywhere** — stations, convenience stores, parks — with a standardised layout. Finding one is rarely a problem in a city.

## Misunderstanding 5｜Ryokan are out of the question

**Some ryokan have universal rooms with private baths and step-free access**; the Japan Ryokan and Hotel Association and JNTO list them. Book directly and describe needs; many will adapt.
