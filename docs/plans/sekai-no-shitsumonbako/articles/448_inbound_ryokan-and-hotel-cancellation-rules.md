# Q. What do people get wrong about hotel and restaurant cancellation rules in Japan?｜Japan Questions #448

- Shelf: `inbound` ／ URL: `/en/questions/ryokan-and-hotel-cancellation-rules` ／ Status: **Draft — sources unverified**

Cancellation policies in Japan are stricter than many visitors expect, and the culture around no-shows is unforgiving. A ryokan that has bought fish for your kaiseki, or a six-seat sushi counter that turned others away, will charge — and the rules are written down.

## A. The common misunderstanding is that cancelling late is a minor discourtesy. Japanese ryokan typically charge 20–50% from 3–7 days out and 100% for same-day or no-show, hotels 100% for same-day, and a growing number of restaurants take deposits or card guarantees and charge the full course price for no-shows — with the law on their side. Read the policy at booking, cancel early by the same channel, and phone if you will be late.

## Typical policies

| Type | Typical cancellation charge | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Ryokan (with meals)** | **7 days before: 20%; 3 days: 30–50%; day before: 50–80%; same day: 80–100%; no-show: 100%** | Meals are prepared per guest; policies are stated in the booking and are enforceable |
| **Business / city hotels** | **Day before: 20–50%; same day: 80–100%; no-show: 100%** | Non-refundable rates are increasingly common |
| **Restaurants (courses, small counters)** | **Deposit or card guarantee at booking**; **no-show: 100% of course price** or a fixed fee | Legally recoverable; Japan's Ministry of Economy published no-show guidance in 2018 |
| **Group bookings** | Stricter | — |
| **Weather** | **Typhoon or transport suspension**: many hotels waive, **ryokan often do too if trains stop**; ask, do not assume | Insurance covers the rest |

- **No-show losses** at Japanese restaurants were estimated at **about ¥200 billion a year** (2018 industry/METI report), which prompted the **no-show guidelines** confirming that restaurants can claim the course price (or a lost-profit share for à la carte) and the rise of **deposit systems** on booking platforms.
- **Ryokan pricing is per person including dinner**; the kitchen buys and preps **the morning of arrival**, which is why late cancellation is charged.
- **Cancelling**: **use the same channel you booked** (platform or direct), and **phone the property** as well if within 24 hours. **Being late**: phone — a ryokan will hold dinner to a point but cannot serve it at 21:00.
- **Consumer protection**: **Consumer Contract Act** caps cancellation charges at the **average actual loss**; typical schedules above are within it.

> **[Verify]** METI no-show guidance (2018) and industry estimates; individual property policies always govern.

## Misunderstanding 1｜Late cancellation is a small discourtesy

**It is a contract with a written charge**, and ryokan and restaurants enforce it. Read the policy; cancel early.

## Misunderstanding 2｜Restaurants cannot really charge for a no-show

**They can, and increasingly do**, via deposits and card guarantees; the 2018 guidance backs the full course price. For a six-seat counter, a no-show is a lost night.

## Misunderstanding 3｜A typhoon automatically cancels everything free

**Often, if transport is suspended — but ask.** Hotels and ryokan decide case by case; **travel insurance** is the backstop (see the typhoon article).

## Misunderstanding 4｜Turning up at 20:00 for a ryokan dinner is fine

**Dinner is at a fixed hour** (see the ryokan article); **phone if late** and expect a shortened or forfeited meal after a point.

## Misunderstanding 5｜Booking several restaurants and picking one on the night is smart

**It is the behaviour the deposit systems were built to stop**, and it burns goodwill in a small-restaurant culture. Book one; cancel by the deadline if plans change.
