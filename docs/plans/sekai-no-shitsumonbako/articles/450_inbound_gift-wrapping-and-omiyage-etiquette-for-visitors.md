# Q. What do people get wrong about buying omiyage and having gifts wrapped in Japan?｜Japan Questions #450

- Shelf: `inbound` ／ URL: `/en/questions/gift-wrapping-and-omiyage-etiquette-for-visitors` ／ Status: **Draft — sources unverified**

Bringing back edible souvenirs — omiyage — for colleagues and family is a Japanese custom that visitors adopt without knowing its rules: individually wrapped, regional, boxed, and wrapped again by the shop. The wrapping counter at a department store is the last unfamiliar step.

## A. The common misunderstanding is that omiyage is a souvenir for yourself. Omiyage is a gift of a place — individually wrapped regional sweets in a box, given to the people you left behind — and the whole retail system is organised around it: boxes of 6, 8, 10 or 12, shelf life of two weeks or more, a paper bag per recipient, and free wrapping. For visitors it is also the least breakable, most accepted gift to take home.

## How omiyage works

| Element | Detail |
|---|---|
| **What** | **Regional sweets or snacks, individually wrapped, boxed** (e.g. Tokyo Banana, Hakata Tōrimon, Shiroi Koibito, Kyoto yatsuhashi); also regional pickles, tea, sake |
| **Where** | **Station and airport omiyage shops**, department-store food floors, the destination's shops; the station near your departure has everything from the region |
| **Box sizes** | **6, 8, 10, 12, 16** pieces — matched to the office headcount; **avoid 4 and 9** (homophones for death and suffering) for formal gifts |
| **Shelf life** | **Boxed omiyage keep 2–4 weeks** (that is the design); **fresh sweets (namagashi) do not travel** (see the wagashi article) |
| **Wrapping** | **Free at department stores and many shops**; say **"purezento de" (as a gift)** or **"omiyage de"**; **noshi paper** for formal occasions on request |
| **Bags** | **One paper bag per recipient** is offered; ask for "kobukuro" (small bags) — Japanese give the gift in the shop's bag |
| **Price norms** | **¥1,000–3,000** per box for colleagues; ¥3,000–5,000 for a host family |
| **Taking home** | **Sealed sweets are fine almost everywhere**; **meat products, fresh fruit, dairy** are restricted (see the food-home article); **tax-free** applies over ¥5,000 (consumables) |

- The omiyage market is estimated at **over ¥1 trillion a year**; station shops in Tokyo alone sell thousands of boxes a day. The custom dates from **Edo-period pilgrims bringing back proof of the shrine visit**.
- **Individual wrapping** is the point: the box is **opened in the office and one piece handed to each person**; a bag of loose cookies does not work.
- **Wrapping etiquette**: the shop wraps **in its own paper** (provenance matters); **do not rewrap in your own paper**; **noshi with a re-tieable bow** for happy repeatable occasions (see the gift article).
- **Do not give**: **knives, combs, sets of four**, and for hospital visits **potted plants**. Sweets are safe.

> **[Verify]** Market size estimates are industry figures; customs rules with your destination.

## Misunderstanding 1｜Omiyage is a souvenir for yourself

**It is a gift for others** — colleagues, family, neighbours — from the place you visited. Buy for yourself too, but that is not what the boxes are for.

## Misunderstanding 2｜A big bag of one kind of cookie is the best value

**Individually wrapped pieces in a box** are what the recipient's office expects; loose or bulk does not distribute.

## Misunderstanding 3｜Wrapping costs extra

**Free at department stores and most shops**; ask for it as a gift and for small bags per recipient.

## Misunderstanding 4｜Any number in a box is fine

For casual colleagues, yes. **For formal gifts avoid 4 and 9**; boxes of 6, 8, 10, 12 exist because of this.

## Misunderstanding 5｜Buy at the first shop you see

**The departure station or airport has the region's full range** at the same prices; buy on the way out so nothing gets crushed for a week in your bag. **Check the shelf life** against your travel date.
