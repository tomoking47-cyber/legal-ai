# Q. What do people get wrong about neighbourhood festivals (matsuri) as a resident?｜Japanese Everyday Wisdom #453

- Shelf: `wisdom` ／ URL: `/en/wisdom/neighbourhood-festivals-matsuri` ／ Status: **Draft — sources unverified**

The big festivals are tourist events; the small ones — the shrine's annual festival in a residential district, the summer bon odori in a school playground — are run by neighbours, funded by donations, and expect residents to take part. Newcomers see a party; the neighbourhood sees a roster.

## A. The common misunderstanding is that a local matsuri is entertainment laid on for you. It is produced by the neighbourhood association and the shrine's parishioner group: residents carry the portable shrine, staff the stalls, sweep up, and donate (the envelope that comes round in summer). Taking part — even one shift on the drinks stall — is how a newcomer becomes a neighbour; the donation is expected but modest.

## How a local festival is run

| Element | Who | What newcomers do |
|---|---|---|
| **The shrine's annual festival (reisai)** | The **shrine's parishioner group (ujiko)** and the **neighbourhood association** | Carry or follow the **mikoshi (portable shrine)**; children pull a small float; **wear the district's happi coat if lent one** |
| **Bon odori (summer dance)** | Neighbourhood association, often in a park or school ground | Join the circle — steps are simple and repeated; **wear yukata if you like** |
| **Donations (goshūgi / kifu)** | Collected by the block leader in an envelope; **¥1,000–5,000 per household** is typical | Give within your means; names are often posted on a board at the festival |
| **Stalls** | Association members; profits fund next year | **Volunteer for a shift** (drinks, shaved ice, cleaning) — the fastest way in |
| **Cleanup** | Everyone present | Stay 30 minutes after |
| **Kids' events** | Association's children's group | Sign children up for the float and the lantern parade |

- There are an estimated **300,000 festivals a year** in Japan, the vast majority **neighbourhood-scale**; the **portable shrine (mikoshi) weighs 300–1,000 kg** and is carried by **20–60 people in shifts**.
- **Donations** fund the festival and are recorded; a **red-and-white envelope with your name** is the format. Not donating is noticed; the amount is not.
- **The mikoshi is sacred**: do not climb on it, and follow the leader's calls; **alcohol is part of the day but not before carrying**.
- **Bon odori** (July–August) is the **ancestral festival**; the dance is open to all, taught on the spot, and one of the easiest ways for a foreign resident to be seen participating.

> **[Verify]** Festival counts are estimates from tourism and cultural bodies; local practice varies enormously — ask your block leader.

## Misunderstanding 1｜The festival is put on for residents

**Residents put it on.** The association's members carry, cook, sweep and fund it; the newcomer's role is to join.

## Misunderstanding 2｜The donation envelope is a scam

**It funds the festival and is recorded publicly**; ¥1,000–5,000 is normal. Ask a neighbour what the block usually gives.

## Misunderstanding 3｜Foreigners are not welcome to carry the mikoshi

**Most neighbourhoods are short of carriers and delighted**; ask the association, wear the happi, follow the calls. Some historic festivals restrict carriers to parishioners — ask, do not assume.

## Misunderstanding 4｜You need to know the dance

**Bon odori is taught by doing**; the steps repeat every 16 beats and children lead. Standing at the edge for one song is enough to get it.

## Misunderstanding 5｜Turning up is participation

**Volunteering for a stall shift or the cleanup** is what registers. It also comes with beer, food and the first real conversations with neighbours.
