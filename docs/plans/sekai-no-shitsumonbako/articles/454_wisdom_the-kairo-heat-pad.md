# Q. What do people get wrong about kairo (disposable heat pads)?｜Japanese Everyday Wisdom #454

- Shelf: `wisdom` ／ URL: `/en/wisdom/the-kairo-heat-pad` ／ Status: **Draft — sources unverified**

Japan uses about 1.5 billion disposable heat pads a winter. They are in every convenience store, every commuter's pocket and every school bag, and they carry a burn warning that most users have never read. What they are, where they go, and the two safety rules cover it.

## A. The common misunderstanding is that a kairo is harmless because it is "warm, not hot". A kairo reaches 50–60°C at peak and holds 40–50°C for 8–20 hours — the low-temperature burn range — so the two rules are: never directly on skin, and never while sleeping. Used correctly (on the outer layer, at the lower back, abdomen or shoulder blades) it warms the whole body and is the cheapest heating there is.

## The product

| Item | Detail |
|---|---|
| Chemistry | **Iron powder oxidising** with air, salt as catalyst, vermiculite and activated carbon to hold water and air; sealed in an oxygen-permeable pouch |
| Temperature | **Average 50–55°C, peak up to 60–65°C**, duration **8–20 hours** depending on type (labels state both) |
| Types | **Stick-on (貼る)** for clothing; **hand-held (貼らない)** for pockets; **shoe inserts (くつ用)**, lower-temperature; **eye and neck steam types** (different chemistry) |
| Where to place (stick-on) | **On the outer layer of the innermost garment** — lower back, abdomen, between the shoulder blades, or over the kidneys; **the "three necks" plus lower back** warm the core |
| Never | **Directly on skin**; **while sleeping**; **on infants, the elderly with reduced sensation, or people with diabetes/circulation problems without care**; **under compression (belt, sitting on it)**; **with other heat sources** (kotatsu, electric blanket) |
| Disposal | **Cooled, in general (burnable or non-burnable per municipality) waste**; contents are iron — some municipalities collect separately |
| Reuse | **Not re-usable**; sealing in a plastic bag pauses but does not reset it |

- **Low-temperature burns** from kairo are reported every winter to the Consumer Affairs Agency; typical cases involve **sleeping with a pad, pads directly on skin under socks, and elderly users**. The time-temperature table (44°C ≈ 3–4 h; 46°C ≈ 30–60 min) applies (see the hot-water-bottle article).
- Kairo were invented in Japan (**1978, Lotte "Hokkairo"**) and remain a Japanese export; the **shoe-insert type** runs cooler (~40°C) because feet have less airflow.
- **Cost**: **¥30–60 per pad**; a winter's daily use is ¥3,000–5,000 — cheaper than any electric heating, and portable.

> **[Verify]** Temperature and duration figures from product labels and the Japan Hygiene Products Industry Association; burn warnings with the Consumer Affairs Agency.

## Misunderstanding 1｜It is only warm, so it cannot burn

**50–60°C for hours is exactly the low-temperature burn range.** The pad feels pleasant while damaging skin.

## Misunderstanding 2｜Stick it on the skin for effect

**On the outer layer of an inner garment**, never skin. Under a sock, over the sock — not against the foot.

## Misunderstanding 3｜Sleep with it for a warm night

**The most common serious burn scenario.** Remove before bed; use bedding and a hot-water bottle (removed) instead.

## Misunderstanding 4｜Hands and feet are the best spots

**Warming the core — lower back, abdomen, shoulder blades — warms hands and feet indirectly** and more safely. Shoe inserts are a low-temperature type for that reason.

## Misunderstanding 5｜It is a gadget

**A billion and a half a year**, in school bags and on construction sites; the cheapest portable heat that exists. Respected, it is one of the best pieces of Japanese winter kit.
