# Q. What do people get wrong about the kyūsu (Japanese teapot) and everyday tea tools?｜Japanese Everyday Wisdom #455

- Shelf: `wisdom` ／ URL: `/en/wisdom/the-kyuusu-teapot-and-tea-tools` ／ Status: **Draft — sources unverified**

The side-handled teapot with a built-in strainer is the workhorse of the Japanese kitchen, and visitors treat it as a ceremonial object. It is a practical design for one specific thing — brewing loose green tea quickly, several times, without a separate strainer — and it comes with a few habits.

## A. The common misunderstanding is that Japanese tea needs the tea ceremony's equipment. Everyday tea needs a kyūsu (a small pot with a side handle and a fine strainer), a way to cool water to 70–80°C, and small cups; the side handle is for pouring with one hand while the other holds the lid, and the small size (200–360 mL) is because green tea is brewed in short, repeated infusions. The cleaning rule matters more than the brewing rule: never leave wet leaves in the pot.

## The tools

| Tool | What it is | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Kyūsu (急須)** | Side-handled pot, **200–360 mL**, with a **built-in mesh or ceramic strainer** at the spout or a removable basket | Small because sencha is brewed in **2–3 short infusions**; the side handle lets you pour and hold the lid with one hand; the strainer means no separate tool |
| **Yuzamashi (湯冷まし)** | A small open pitcher | Pour boiling water in to **drop it about 10°C**; a second pour drops it again — the standard way to reach 70–80°C without a thermometer |
| **Chawan / yunomi** | Small cups, **80–150 mL** | Tea is drunk in small amounts, poured round-robin so every cup has equal strength |
| **Chazutsu (茶筒)** | Airtight tea canister | Leaves oxidise in weeks once open (see the green tea article) |
| **Dobin / hōhin** | Larger pot with a handle over the top; hōhin has no handle (for cooled water) | Family-size or gyokuro |
| **Tetsubin** | Cast-iron kettle | Boils water; **not for brewing** (unlike enamelled "iron teapots" sold abroad) |
| Materials | **Tokoname (red clay)**, **Banko (purple clay)**, porcelain (Arita, Hasami) | Clay is said to soften the tea; porcelain is neutral and easiest to clean |

- **Brewing**: leaves in (**2–3 g per person**), cooled water, **60–90 seconds**, pour **to the last drop** into cups in rotation; second infusion with slightly hotter water and shorter time. Never let leaves sit in water between infusions.
- **Cleaning**: **empty the leaves after the last infusion, rinse with water only, dry upside down with the lid off**. Detergent is avoided (clay absorbs smells); **the strainer is cleaned with a brush** when it clogs. A pot left with wet leaves overnight smells and grows mould.
- **Tokoname** (Aichi) makes most Japanese kyūsu; a hand-made one costs **¥3,000–20,000**, a basic one **¥1,000**.

> **[Verify]** Brewing parameters with the Japan Tea Central Association; production details with the Tokoname ware association.

## Misunderstanding 1｜You need the tea ceremony set

**Matcha needs the whisk and bowl; leaf tea needs a kyūsu and cups.** The ceremony is a different practice.

## Misunderstanding 2｜Bigger pot, more tea

**Small on purpose**: the second and third infusions are part of the drink. A big pot over-steeps.

## Misunderstanding 3｜Wash the pot with detergent

**Water only, rinsed and dried**; clay holds detergent smells. Clean the strainer with a brush.

## Misunderstanding 4｜The iron teapot sold abroad is a Japanese tea tool

**The tetsubin is a kettle**; brewing in unenamelled iron ruins the tea. Enamelled "iron teapots" are an export product, not a Japanese household item.

## Misunderstanding 5｜Leaving leaves in for the next cup saves time

**It stews the tea and grows mould.** Brew, pour out completely, and either re-brew soon or empty the pot.
