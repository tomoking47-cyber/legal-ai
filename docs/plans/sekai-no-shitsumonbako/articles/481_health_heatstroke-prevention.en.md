# Q. Is it true you can get heatstroke indoors? Prevention and first response｜Japan Questions #481

- Shelf: `health` ／ URL: `/en/health/heatstroke-prevention` ／ Status: **Draft — English edition, sources unverified**

Every summer in Japan, around 100,000 people are taken to hospital by ambulance for heatstroke (熱中症, netchūshō). Many newcomers picture young people exercising outdoors. In fact, more than half of those taken to hospital are older people, and the single most common place it happens is the home. If this is your first Japanese summer, the humidity makes the heat harder on the body than the thermometer suggests. Here are the basics of prevention, and how to judge symptoms when they appear.

## A. Around 40% of heatstroke cases happen inside the home. Prevention rests on three pillars: keep the room at 28°C (82°F) or below and humidity at 60% or below (do not hesitate to use the air conditioner); drink small amounts often, before you feel thirsty, and add salt if you sweat heavily; and avoid going out or exercising on days when the heat index (WBGT) is 28 or above. For dizziness, light-headedness or muscle cramps, move somewhere cool, cool the body and drink. For headache, nausea or exhaustion, go to a medical facility. If the person responds slowly when spoken to, or cannot drink unaided, call 119 (Japan's ambulance number) without hesitating.

## Heatstroke in numbers

| Item | Figure (guide) |
|---|---|
| **Ambulance transports** | **About 100,000 a year** (May–September, Fire and Disaster Management Agency; a record 100,510 in 2025 and 97,578 in 2024) |
| **Age** | **About 57% of those transported are 65 or older** (57,433 people, 57.1%, in 2025) |
| **Where it happens** | **The home is the most common place, at about 40%.** Then roads, outdoor workplaces and sports grounds |
| **Deaths** | **Around 1,500 to over 2,000 a year** (Vital Statistics: 1,651 in 2023 and a record 2,160 in 2024). **Over 80% are 65 or older**; in most indoor deaths **the air conditioner was not in use** |
| **Heat index (WBGT)** | **25 and above: caution. 28 and above: severe caution (stop strenuous exercise). 31 and above: danger (avoid going out).** At **33 and above** a "Heatstroke Alert" (熱中症警戒アラート) is issued, and where every observation point in a prefecture reaches **35 and above**, the "Special Heatstroke Alert" (熱中症特別警戒アラート) introduced in April 2024 is issued |
| **Indoor environment** | **Room temperature 28°C or below, humidity 60% or below** as a guide. Older people feel heat less, so place a **thermometer-hygrometer** and judge by the numbers |
| **Fluids** | **1.2 L a day** besides meals, as a guide (Ministry of the Environment). If you sweat heavily, add **0.1–0.2% salt** (sports drinks, oral rehydration solution — 経口補水液, keikō hosui-eki, sold at drugstores) |

## Severity and response (simplified from the Japanese Association for Acute Medicine's Clinical Practice Guideline for Heatstroke 2024)

> The 2024 revision carved a new **Stage IV (most severe)** out of Stage III, for cases with a **core temperature of 40°C or above together with severe impairment of consciousness (GCS 8 or below)**. The first aid a bystander gives is the same as for Stage III: call 119 at once and cool the whole body.

| Grade | Symptoms | Response |
|---|---|---|
| **Grade I (mild)** | Dizziness, light-headedness, muscle pain or cramps, heavy sweating | **Move somewhere cool, loosen clothing, cool the neck, armpits and groin, take fluids and salt.** If no improvement, go to a medical facility |
| **Grade II (moderate)** | **Headache, nausea, vomiting, feeling weak, unable to concentrate** | **Go to a medical facility.** If the person cannot drink unaided, call 119 |
| **Grade III (severe)** | **Confusion, odd replies, convulsions, unable to walk straight, hot body** | **Call 119 at once.** Until the ambulance arrives, cool the whole body (wet towels plus a fan; ice on the neck, armpits and groin) |

- **Getting used to the heat (heat acclimatisation)** takes **from a few days to 2 weeks**. The days right after the rainy season (tsuyu) ends, or any day that turns hot suddenly, are the most dangerous. The recommended approach is to prepare the body **from May–June** with light exercise that makes you sweat, or with baths.
- **Older people** are at higher risk for three reasons: ① they feel heat and thirst less, ② their bodies hold less water, ③ they tend to hold off on using the air conditioner. **Many families phone parents who live apart to ask "have you turned on the air conditioner?"**
- **Children** are closer to the ground and take more reflected heat, and their temperature regulation is still immature. **Never leave them in a car** (at 35°C outside, the inside of a car reaches the **danger zone in 15 minutes**).
- **Clothing**: breathable, moisture-wicking fabrics, **a hat or parasol**, and **choose the shade** when outdoors.
- **Alcohol is a diuretic and worsens dehydration**, so it does not count as fluid intake.

## Misunderstanding 1｜Heatstroke is for people who exercise outdoors

**The most common place is the home, and more than half of those taken to hospital are older people.** Rooms with the windows shut, sleeping hours and the time after a bath all need care.

## Misunderstanding 2｜Drinking when you feel thirsty is enough

**By the time you feel thirsty, mild dehydration has already begun.** Older people feel thirst less, so **drinking at set times** is the reliable way.

## Misunderstanding 3｜With a fan you do not need an air conditioner

**When the room is hot, a fan only moves hot air around.** Both the Ministry of the Environment and the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare urge people "not to hesitate to use the air conditioner". Put life before the electricity bill.

## Misunderstanding 4｜Plenty of plain water and you are safe

**Heavy sweating loses salt too. Drinking large amounts of water alone can dilute the salt in your blood and make you unwell.** On days you sweat a lot, take salt as well.

## Misunderstanding 5｜A short rest will settle it, so no need for a doctor

**Headache, nausea and exhaustion are signs of moderate heatstroke**, the stage at which you should see a doctor. **If the person responds slowly when spoken to, or cannot drink unaided**, call 119. A wrong judgement here can cost a life.
