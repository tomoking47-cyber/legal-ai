# Q. Does curry or stew really taste better the next day? Is leaving it out overnight dangerous?｜Japan Questions #495

- Shelf: `trend` ／ URL: `/en/trend/storing-curry-and-stews` ／ Status: **Draft — English edition, sources unverified**

"Curry tastes better on day two" is common knowledge in Japanese homes, but the habit of "leaving the pot on the hob overnight" draws food-safety warnings almost every year. The culprit is Clostridium perfringens (ウェルシュ菌, Welshu-kin in Japanese). It is not killed by cooking, and it multiplies while a big pot cools slowly. Here we set out a storage routine that gives you both the flavour and the safety.

## A. Once you have made curry, stew or nimono (simmered dishes), do not "leave the pot out to take the heat off". Within 2 hours of cooking, divide it into small portions, chill quickly and refrigerate. The spores of C. perfringens survive 100°C, and the bacteria multiply vigorously while the pot passes through 12–50°C (fastest at 43–47°C, dividing once every 10 minutes). They also dislike oxygen, so the bottom of a large pot is an ideal environment. When you eat it the next day, stir well and bring it to a full boil right through (75°C or above for at least 1 minute). Keep it 2–3 days in the fridge, or 1 month in the freezer. Potatoes and carrots lose their texture when frozen, so remove or mash them.

## C. perfringens and curry

| Item | Details |
|---|---|
| **What it is** | **Clostridium perfringens** (Welshu-kin). Widespread in **soil, water and the gut of humans and animals**; arrives on **meat, fish and vegetables** |
| **Why cooking does not kill it** | It forms **spores** (heat-resistant shells) that **survive several hours at 100°C**. As the pot cools after cooking, the spores germinate and multiply |
| **Growth temperature** | **12–50°C**. **Optimum 43–47°C**, dividing **about once every 10 minutes** (millions-fold overnight) |
| **Oxygen** | **Dislikes oxygen (anaerobic)**. Oxygen does not reach the bottom or centre of a thick, large pot: ideal conditions |
| **Symptoms** | **Stomach pain and diarrhoea** **6–18 hours** after eating. Fever and vomiting are uncommon; most people **recover in 1–2 days**, but watch for dehydration in older people and children |
| **Statistics** | **Around 20–30 outbreaks a year** in Japan, with **1,000–2,000 patients**. **Many patients per outbreak** (large-pot cooking in school meals, catering and events). Nicknamed the "**school-lunch disease**" (kyūshoku-byō) |

## Safe storage, step by step

1. **Within 2 hours of cooking, take the heat off and refrigerate.** "Taking the heat off" is not leaving it out; it is **rapid chilling**:
   - **Divide into small portions** (spread thinly in shallow containers or freezer bags) → **stand the pot in iced water**, or **put ice packs around it** → **stir to let air in as it cools**.
   - Target: bring the centre of the food to **around 20°C within 30 minutes, or around 10°C within 60 minutes** (the cooling standard in the MHLW "Hygiene Management Manual for Large-Scale Cooking Facilities").
2. **Fridge (10°C or below): 2–3 days**. **Freezer (−18°C or below): 1 month**.
3. **Reheat before eating**: **transfer to a pan, stir well and bring to the boil right through (75°C or above for at least 1 minute)**. Microwaves heat unevenly, so **stir partway through and reheat again**.
4. **Heat only what you will eat.** The more times it is reheated, the more chance the bacteria get.

- **Freezing notes**: **potatoes and carrots** lose water on thawing and go spongy. **Remove them, or mash them, before freezing.** **Roux-based curry keeps 1 month; cream-based stews 2–3 weeks.**
- **The science of "better on day two"**: overnight, **the umami and moisture of the ingredients migrate into the sauce, and the starch gelatinises further, so the body and thickness improve**. This **happens just the same in the fridge**. There is no need to leave it at room temperature.
- **Summer (room temperature 25°C or above)** is especially dangerous. **A night in a kitchen without air conditioning is a long stretch at the bacteria's optimum temperature.**
- **Not only curry**: **stews, nimono, soups, mapo tofu, nikujaga, takikomi-gohan (seasoned rice)**: anything made in bulk that cools slowly.

> **[Verify]** Check the division rate of C. perfringens and the food-poisoning statistics against MHLW and Tokyo Metropolitan Government materials, and the cooling targets (below 20°C within 2 hours) against the MHLW "Hygiene Management Manual for Large-Scale Cooking Facilities".

## Misunderstanding 1｜I simmered it thoroughly, so the bacteria are dead

**The spores of C. perfringens survive 100°C.** Other bacteria die with heat; this one multiplies while the pot cools. How you cool it is what counts.

## Misunderstanding 2｜Just leave the pot to cool naturally

**The longer the pot spends between 12 and 50°C, the more the bacteria multiply.** Small portions, iced water and stirring: down to around 20°C within 30 minutes and around 10°C within 60 minutes.

## Misunderstanding 3｜Reheating in the morning makes it safe

**Heat reduces the bacterial count, but bacteria that have multiplied in bulk, and their spores, remain.** Rapid chilling the night before is the precondition; reheating means "stir and bring to the boil".

## Misunderstanding 4｜Day-two flavour comes from resting at room temperature

**The migration of umami and the change in thickness happen in the fridge too.** There is no need at all to rest it at room temperature.

## Misunderstanding 5｜In the fridge it keeps a week

**Even refrigerated, 2–3 days is the guide.** Beyond that, freeze. Bacteria multiply slowly in the fridge too.
