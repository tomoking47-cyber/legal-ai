# Q. Should I wash mushrooms or not? How to store them, and the danger of wild mushrooms｜Japan Questions #498

- Shelf: `trend` ／ URL: `/en/trend/mushroom-storage-and-safety` ／ Status: **Draft — English edition, sources unverified**

Shiitake, shimeji, enoki, maitake: mushrooms are essential to the Japanese table, and they raise a lot of small questions. Should they be washed? Does freezing really bring out more umami? Can they be eaten raw? And every autumn there are poisonings from wild mushrooms.

## A. Cultivated mushrooms from the supermarket are grown in a clean environment, and washing makes them absorb water and lose flavour and texture. So as a rule do not wash them; trim the base (ishizuki) and brush off any dirt by hand or with kitchen paper. Store them in their pack in the fridge for 3–4 days; if you want them to last longer or have bought in bulk, trim, separate and freeze (1 month). Freezing breaks down the cells, which is generally described as releasing more umami. Cook mushrooms of every kind before eating (raw enoki has been reported to contain a toxic component, so avoid eating it raw). As for wild mushrooms, folk rules such as "if it splits lengthways it is edible" or "if insects have eaten it, it is safe" are all wrong. Not eating wild mushrooms on your own judgement is the only safe policy.

## Storage guide

| Mushroom | Fridge (in the pack, or wrapped in kitchen paper) | Freezer (base trimmed, separated) | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Shiitake** | **4–5 days**. Stems upward | **1 month** | A few hours in the sun increases vitamin D |
| **Shimeji, maitake** | **3–4 days** | **1 month** | Spoil quickly once droplets form |
| **Enoki** | **3–4 days** | **1 month** (cut first) | **Not for raw eating** (see below) |
| **Eringi (king oyster)** | **1 week** | 1 month | Tolerates drying |
| **Nameko** | **2–3 days** (once opened, same or next day) | 1 month | Discard if the slime smells sour |
| **Button mushrooms** | 3–4 days | 1 month | Generally described as the only mushroom that can be eaten raw, and only when very fresh |
| **Dried shiitake** | Airtight at room temperature, **1 year** | — | Use the soaking liquid as stock. **Soaking overnight in the fridge** brings out the umami |

- **Why not wash**: **cultivated mushrooms are grown on sterile substrate, close to pesticide-free and soil-free, so they are clean**. Washing makes them **absorb water, weakening the flavour and making them soggy**. Wipe dirt off **with damp kitchen paper**, or at most **pass quickly under running water and wipe at once**. **Nameko should be rinsed briefly** (to remove debris from the slime).
- **The benefit of freezing**: freezing **breaks the cell walls, so umami compounds (such as guanylic acid) are released more readily on cooking**, according to the usual explanation. **Cook straight from frozen without thawing.** The texture becomes slightly softer, so frozen mushrooms suit soups, stir-fries and takikomi-gohan (seasoned rice).
- **Do not eat raw**: **mushrooms in general are meant to be cooked**. **Raw enoki contains "flammutoxin", a protein with a haemolytic (blood-cell-damaging) action**, which is inactivated by heat. **"Shiitake dermatitis"** (whip-like welts over the whole body) has been reported after **eating shiitake raw or undercooked**. **Everything except button mushrooms must be cooked.**
- **Signs of spoilage**: **a sour smell, slime (other than on nameko), droplets that have turned dark, limpness**.
- **Ishizuki (the base)**: the hard part at the root of shimeji and maitake. **On enoki, cut off the bottom 1–2 cm**; **on shiitake, only the tip of the stem** (the stem itself is edible).

## Wild mushrooms: never rely on your own judgement

- According to Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare (MHLW) statistics, **poisonous mushrooms cause around 20–30 food-poisoning incidents a year** (24 incidents with 60 patients in 2023, and 19 incidents with 48 patients in 2024), **concentrated in autumn (September–October)**. The top culprits are **tsukiyotake (mistaken for shiitake or hiratake), kusaura-benitake (mistaken for shimeji types) and the Amanita group (tengutake)**. **There have been deaths.**
- **Folk rules that are all wrong**: "**If it splits lengthways it is edible**"; "**if insects have eaten it, it is safe**"; "**bright colours mean poison, dull ones mean safe**"; "**salting or drying removes the poison**"; "**it is fine if you cook it with aubergine**".
- **The principle**: **if you cannot be certain a mushroom is edible, do not pick it, do not eat it, do not give it to anyone**. **The same applies to mushrooms you are given.**
- **If you feel ill after eating**: **nausea, vomiting, stomach pain, diarrhoea, hallucinations, numbness**. **Take the leftover mushrooms and any vomit with you** and see a doctor at once. Identifying the species decides the medical response.

## Misunderstanding 1｜Mushrooms are like vegetables, so wash them before use

**Cultivated mushrooms keep their flavour and texture better unwashed.** Brush off dirt with kitchen paper.

## Misunderstanding 2｜Freezing spoils the flavour

**Umami compounds are released more readily, making them well suited to soups and stir-fries.** The texture changes, but for bulk buying, freezing wins.

## Misunderstanding 3｜If they are fresh they can be eaten raw

**Everything except button mushrooms is meant to be cooked.** Avoid raw enoki in particular, and cook shiitake right through.

## Misunderstanding 4｜A mushroom that splits lengthways is edible

**This folk rule has no basis; poisonous mushrooms split lengthways too.** Do not eat wild mushrooms without identification by an expert.

## Misunderstanding 5｜I can tell them apart with a field guide or an app

**Tsukiyotake and shiitake, or kusaura-benitake and shimeji, are so alike that even experts misidentify them without the specimen in hand.** Judging by photo is dangerous.
