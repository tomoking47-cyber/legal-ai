# Q. How do I store wakame, kombu, hijiki and nori? Is there such a thing as eating too much seaweed?｜Japan Questions #499

- Shelf: `trend` ／ URL: `/en/trend/seaweed-storage-and-iodine` ／ Status: **Draft — English edition, sources unverified**

Seaweed is an everyday part of eating in Japan, with a strong healthy image. But "eat lots of kombu every day and you will be healthier" is not true: too much iodine is a real issue, and the inorganic arsenic in hijiki has become widely known. Here are the basics of storage, and a guide to sensible amounts.

## A. For dried seaweed (wakame, kombu, hijiki, nori) moisture is the great enemy: once opened, keep it in an airtight container with a desiccant in a dark place at room temperature; nori does best in the freezer. Fresh and salted wakame go in the fridge and should be used quickly. On quantity: kombu is extremely high in iodine, and the adult tolerable upper limit (3,000 μg a day) is easily exceeded if you eat a lot of kombu tsukudani (simmered, sweet-salty kombu) or kombu itself every day, so keep it to "stock, plus a small side dish". Hijiki contains inorganic arsenic, but soaking or boiling it removes most of it, and the Japanese government's assessment is that at normal Japanese intakes the health concern is low. If you have a thyroid condition, ask your doctor how much seaweed is appropriate.

## Storage guide

| Seaweed | Unopened | Opened | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Dried wakame (cut wakame)** | Room temperature, dark, **about 1 year** | **Airtight container with desiccant**, dark, room temperature. **3–6 months** | Damp wakame loses flavour and can grow mould. **Swells 8–12 times** when rehydrated (one serving is 2–3 g dried) |
| **Salted wakame (enzō wakame)** | Refrigerated, **several months** | Refrigerate; rinse off the salt before use | Desalt in **water for about 5 minutes**; too long and it goes limp |
| **Fresh wakame** | Refrigerated, **2–3 days** | Blanch (it turns bright green), then refrigerate **3–4 days** or freeze **1 month** | In season in spring (February–May) |
| **Kombu (dried, for stock)** | Room temperature, dark, **1 year or more** (the flavour matures) | Airtight, away from moisture | **The white powder on the surface is an umami compound (mannitol)**; do not wash it off |
| **Hijiki (dried)** | Room temperature, dark, **1 year** | Airtight, away from moisture | Swells **8–10 times** when rehydrated |
| **Toasted nori (yaki-nori)** | Room temperature, dark; **goes soft within days of opening** | **Seal and freeze** (let it return to room temperature before opening each time), or seal with a desiccant | Softened nori recovers if you pass it over a flame or warm it briefly in a frying pan |

## Iodine and arsenic: a guide to amounts

| Item | Details |
|---|---|
| **Recommended iodine intake** | Adults **140 μg a day**, **tolerable upper limit 3,000 μg a day** (Dietary Reference Intakes for Japanese, 2025 edition). For pregnant and breastfeeding women the upper limit is **2,000 μg** |
| **Iodine in kombu** | Dried kombu: **about 200,000 μg per 100 g**. **One tablespoon of kombu tsukudani (about 15 g) can contain several thousand μg.** **Even one cup (200 ml) of kombu stock: several hundred to around 1,000 μg** |
| **Wakame, nori, hijiki** | **One to two orders of magnitude less** than kombu. Dried wakame: about 10,000 μg per 100 g; one sheet of toasted nori (3 g): about 60 μg |
| **Effects of too much** | Prolonged excess iodine can cause **underactive thyroid or goitre**. **Japanese intakes are high to begin with** (estimated average 1,000–3,000 μg a day), though populations used to seaweed are also reported to be less affected |
| **Inorganic arsenic in hijiki** | Hijiki contains more inorganic arsenic than other seaweeds. **The UK Food Standards Agency (2004) advised against eating it.** Japan's Food Safety Commission and MHLW assess that "**at normal intakes (a few times a week, one small bowl at a time) the health concern is low**". **Soaking or boiling reduces inorganic arsenic by about 50–90%** (MAFF survey); discard the soaking water |

- **Living with kombu**: **using it for stock, plus a small amount as a side dish**, is within the safe range. **Eating kombu itself in quantity every day (lots of tsukudani, several cups of kombu tea a day, "ne-kombu water", etc.)** easily exceeds the upper limit. **People with thyroid conditions or undergoing thyroid care, and pregnant women**, should ask their doctor about amounts.
- **How to rehydrate hijiki**: **soak in plenty of water for 30 minutes → discard the water → boil briefly**, and inorganic arsenic drops. **For infants**, keep the frequency modest.
- **Allergies**: seaweed itself is not one of Japan's designated allergens, but **processed products (seasoned nori, tsukudani, seaweed salad) may contain wheat, soy, shrimp or crab**; check the ingredients.

> **[Verify]** Check the recommended and upper-limit iodine figures against the "Dietary Reference Intakes for Japanese (2025 edition)", the iodine content of kombu and wakame against the Standard Tables of Food Composition, and the reduction rate of inorganic arsenic in hijiki against the MAFF survey material.

## Misunderstanding 1｜Seaweed is good for you, so you can eat as much as you like

**Kombu is very high in iodine; eaten in quantity every day it exceeds the upper limit.** Keep to stock and a small side dish.

## Misunderstanding 2｜Hijiki is dangerous and should not be eaten

**Soaking or boiling greatly reduces inorganic arsenic, and at normal Japanese intakes the concern is assessed as low.** Discarding the soaking water and not eating it excessively often is enough.

## Misunderstanding 3｜The white powder on kombu is mould

**It is an umami compound (mannitol).** Washing it off weakens the stock. Mould shows as green or black spots and smells different.

## Misunderstanding 4｜Nori stays crisp in the fridge

**Condensation each time it goes in and out of the fridge makes it soft.** Seal it, freeze it, and let it return to room temperature before opening: that works.

## Misunderstanding 5｜Drinking kombu water or eating ne-kombu every day makes you healthier

**This is a classic habit that leads to excess iodine**, and effects on the thyroid have been pointed out. People with thyroid conditions and pregnant women in particular should avoid it.
