# Q. What do people get wrong about uchimizu, sprinkling water on the ground?｜Japanese Everyday Wisdom #512

- Shelf: `wisdom` ／ URL: `/en/wisdom/uchimizu-water-sprinkling` ／ Status: **Draft — sources unverified**

On summer evenings in Japan you still see shopkeepers and grandmothers throwing a ladle of water across the pavement in front of the house. It looks like a ritual — and it partly is — but uchimizu is evaporative cooling with 400 years of practice behind it, and Tokyo's government has run city-wide uchimizu campaigns since 2003. Done at the wrong time, though, it makes the evening more humid, not cooler.

## A. The common misunderstanding is that uchimizu cools the air by wetting it. It cools by evaporation: water on a hot surface takes heat with it as it turns to vapour, dropping the surface temperature by 10°C or more and the air just above it by 1–2°C. That only works if the water actually evaporates fast — early morning or after 17:00, on shaded ground or ground that has cooled, using recycled water (bath, rain, air-conditioner drip), a ladleful per square metre. Splash it on sun-baked asphalt at 14:00 and it flashes off in seconds, raising humidity without lasting cooling. Never use it near a neighbour's laundry or doorway, and never drinking water in a shortage.

## How it works and how to do it

| Item | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Principle** | Evaporating **1 litre of water absorbs about 2,400 kJ** — enough to cool a large patch of pavement by several degrees. The cooled surface then stops radiating heat into the room and the street |
| **Measured effect** | Tokyo trials: **ground surface −10 to −15°C**, **air at 1.5 m −1 to −2°C** for 30–60 minutes after wetting in the evening |
| **Best time** | **Early morning (6–8 am)** and **evening (after 17:00, when the sun is off the surface)**. Water on ground still in full sun evaporates too fast for a lasting effect |
| **Where** | **Shaded or cooling ground**: the entrance path, the balcony floor, the north side, under the eaves. **Balconies**: the concrete floor and the outdoor unit's surroundings (not the unit itself) |
| **How much** | **1–2 litres per square metre** — a ladle or a watering can. Puddles don't help; a film does |
| **Which water** | **Leftover bath water, rainwater from a barrel, air-conditioner drain water, rinse water** — the campaign rule is **"secondary water only"**. Never tap water during a drought advisory |
| **Don'ts** | Not on a **neighbour's wall, doorstep or laundry**; not on **a road where cars pass** (splash); not on **wooden decks daily** (rot); not with **soapy water** on plants |

- **The etiquette origin**: uchimizu began as **purification and welcome** — wetting the entrance path before guests arrived — and as dust control on unpaved streets. The cooling was understood too; **Edo-period manuals** recommend it for evening comfort.
- **Around the air conditioner**: cooling the **area around the outdoor unit** (shading it, wetting the concrete nearby) can improve its efficiency slightly; **spraying water onto the unit itself** is discouraged by manufacturers.
- **Balcony and veranda routine**: many households combine **sudare (shade) + uchimizu (evaporation) + window opened for cross-breeze** in the evening before turning on the air conditioner, and report the room needs less cooling.
- **Community campaigns**: "**Mission Uchimizu**" (Tokyo, since 2003) and municipal uchimizu days in August; **schools and shopping streets** do it together at 17:00 on a set day.
- **Humidity caveat**: in already humid, still evenings (**above 75% RH**), the cooling is small and the wet ground adds to the mugginess — **skip it and ventilate instead**.

> **[Verify]** Temperature-reduction figures from Tokyo uchimizu trials; latent heat value (2,400 kJ/L) is standard physics.
