# Q. What do people get wrong about shōji, the paper sliding screens, and re-papering them?｜Japanese Everyday Wisdom #513

- Shelf: `wisdom` ／ URL: `/en/wisdom/re-papering-shoji-screens` ／ Status: **Draft — sources unverified**

Shōji — the wooden lattice screens covered in translucent paper — still divide rooms and windows in millions of Japanese homes, and the paper tears, yellows and sags with humidity and cats. Households traditionally re-paper them at year-end, and the job costs a few hundred yen and an afternoon. Visitors and new residents assume torn shōji means a carpenter.

## A. The common misunderstanding is that shōji paper is fragile and permanent. It is a consumable, designed to be replaced every few years: soak off the old paper with a wet sponge, dry the frame, brush on shōji glue or use pre-glued paper, roll the new sheet on, cut with a blade along the frame, and mist it so it tightens as it dries. A standard sheet costs ¥300–1,000, glue ¥200, and a screen takes 30–60 minutes once you've done one. Small tears can be patched with cherry-blossom-shaped stickers. What actually ruins shōji is leaving wet paper on the frame, or using plastic "paper" that traps humidity in the wood.

## Re-papering, step by step

| Step | How | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Remove the screen** | Lift the screen up into the top groove, swing the bottom out | Note which way it faces (paper side faces **out of the room toward the light**) |
| **2. Soak off the old paper** | Wet the lattice side with a sponge or brush; wait **3–5 minutes**; peel from a corner | Water loosens the starch glue. Don't scrape the wood |
| **3. Clean and dry** | Wipe glue residue; **dry fully (1–2 h)** | Wet wood warps and stains the new paper |
| **4. Glue** | **Shōji glue (nori)** brushed on every lattice bar, or a **glue tube with a lattice nozzle**; or use **pre-glued (iron-on or water-activated) paper** | Thin, even lines; too much glue shows as spots |
| **5. Roll on the paper** | Lay the roll at one edge, unroll across, smooth gently. Standard **roll width 94 cm**, length **1.8–2.15 m** — one roll per screen | Work in one pass; wrinkles are hard to fix |
| **6. Cut** | Run a **cutter** along the frame with a **cutting ruler/guide** | Cut before the glue dries |
| **7. Tighten** | **Mist lightly with water**; as it dries the paper pulls taut | Do not over-wet |

- **Paper choices**: **washi (mulberry)**: most breathable and beautiful, tears easily, **¥300–600**. **Reinforced (with rayon/polyester fibres)**: **2–4× stronger**, still breathable, **¥600–1,000**. **Plastic laminated (Warlon-type)**: **pet- and child-proof, wipeable**, but **reduces the wood's breathing and can trap humidity**; used in kitchens and where cats live. **UV-cut and thermal** papers exist.
- **Why paper at all**: shōji **diffuse light** softly and **let the room breathe** — humidity passes through the paper, which is part of how traditional houses managed damp. Plastic sheets stop that.
- **Small tears**: **patch stickers** shaped like blossoms or leaves (¥100 for a sheet) are the accepted fix and part of the aesthetic.
- **Frequency**: **every 2–5 years**, traditionally in December (**osōji** season) so the new year has clean, bright screens. Yellowing comes from UV; sagging from humidity cycles.
- **Fusuma** (opaque sliding doors) are a different job — thicker paper, often professionally done (**¥3,000–8,000 per side**) because the surface must be perfectly flat.
- **Renters**: **replacing shōji paper is normally the tenant's routine maintenance**, and torn paper at move-out may be deducted; re-papering before inspection is cheap insurance.
- **Cats**: reinforced or laminated paper on the bottom half, washi above, is the household compromise.
