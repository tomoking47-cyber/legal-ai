# Q. What do people get wrong about drying wet shoes? The newspaper trick and what not to do｜Japanese Everyday Wisdom #514

- Shelf: `wisdom` ／ URL: `/en/wisdom/drying-wet-shoes` ／ Status: **Draft — sources unverified**

Japan's rainy season, typhoons and snowy winters mean wet shoes for months, and every household has a method. The one that has survived everything — stuffing shoes with crumpled newspaper — works for a reason, and the modern alternatives (dryers, hair-dryers, radiators) tend to shorten a shoe's life.

## A. The common misunderstanding is that heat dries shoes best. Direct heat — a heater, hair-dryer, or a tumble dryer — warps soles, cracks leather and weakens glue. Newspaper works because it wicks water from the inside and its large surface area lets it evaporate; change it after an hour, again after a few hours, and the shoe is dry by morning without damage. Take out the insoles, loosen the laces, stand the shoes on their sides or upside down in moving air, and never in direct sun for leather. A ¥1,000 shoe dryer with a fan (not a heater) is the one gadget worth having in a rainy country.

## Methods compared

| Method | Speed | Damage risk | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Newspaper stuffing** | **Overnight** (change paper 2–3 times) | **Low** | Newsprint is highly absorbent; **coloured pages can transfer ink** to light linings — use black-and-white pages or a paper towel layer |
| **Fan / circulator** | 4–8 hours | Low | Point at the opening; combine with newspaper |
| **Shoe dryer (fan type)** | 3–6 hours | Low | ¥1,000–3,000; some have a **low-heat (40°C) mode** — fine for trainers, avoid for leather |
| **Shoe dryer (heated, 60°C+)** | 1–3 hours | **Medium** | Glue and leather don't like it repeatedly |
| **Hair-dryer** | 30–60 min | **High** | Localised heat **warps soles and cracks leather**; hot air on wet leather = stiff leather |
| **Radiator / heater / kotatsu** | Hours | **High** | Same; also a fire risk with paper inside |
| **Tumble dryer** | 30–60 min | **High** | Banging damages the drum and the shoe; glue softens |
| **Sun** | Hours | **Medium** | OK for canvas and trainers; **leather fades and dries out**; rubber degrades |
| **Rice / silica gel** | Overnight | Low | Silica packs from packaging work; rice is messy |

## The routine

1. **Rinse mud off** first (dried mud pulls moisture and stains).
2. **Remove insoles and laces**; dry them separately.
3. **Stuff with newspaper** loosely — enough to touch the lining, not to stretch the toe.
4. **Stand on the side or heel-up** so the opening faces moving air; put them in the **ventilated entrance (genkan)** or under a **fan**, not in a closed cupboard.
5. **Change the paper after 1 hour, then after 3–4 hours**. Most trainers are dry by morning.
6. **Leather**: once damp rather than wet, insert **shoe trees (cedar)** and apply **conditioner** when fully dry — water strips oils; salt stains from snow come off with a **1:1 water–vinegar wipe**.
7. **Smell**: **baking soda** in a sock overnight once dry; **do not spray deodoriser into a wet shoe** — it seals moisture and smell.

- **Rain days**: many households keep **two pairs in rotation** and a **second pair at the office**. **Waterproof spray** (fluorine-free options now) applied monthly keeps water out in the first place.
- **Snow**: **grit and salt** — rinse the outside before drying.
- **Suede**: **newspaper and air only**; brush when dry with a suede brush to lift the nap.
- **Children's shoes**: a **wall-mounted shoe-drying rack** in the bathroom's drying mode (see the bathroom-fan article) is the Japanese apartment solution.

> **[Verify]** Drying times and temperature thresholds are practical estimates; NITE incident data on heaters and hair-dryers used for drying should be cited specifically.
