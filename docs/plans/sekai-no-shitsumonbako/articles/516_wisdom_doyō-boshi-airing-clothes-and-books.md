# Q. What do people get wrong about doyō-boshi, the midsummer airing of clothes and books?｜Japanese Everyday Wisdom #516

- Shelf: `wisdom` ／ URL: `/en/wisdom/doyō-boshi-airing-clothes-and-books` ／ Status: **Draft — sources unverified**

In the hottest, driest spell after the rainy season — the doyō days in late July — Japanese households traditionally take everything out of the cupboards: kimono, winter bedding, books, scrolls — and air them in the shade for a day. It looks quaint; it is a mould- and insect-control routine timed to the one week of the year when the air is dry enough to work.

## A. The common misunderstanding is that airing means sunshine. Doyō-boshi is done in shade with a breeze — sun fades dyes and yellows paper — on a day with humidity below 60% after two or three dry days, when the fabric and paper can release the moisture they absorbed during the rainy season. Kimono go on hangers inside out, books stand open and fanned, bedding lies flat on a rail, all for a few hours; you inspect for mould spots and insect damage as you go, then everything goes back with fresh desiccant and mothballs. Modern apartments do the same job with a dehumidifier and an afternoon of open drawers, but the calendar reminder still matters.

## The tradition and the reason

| Item | Detail |
|---|---|
| **When** | **Doyō** of summer: the roughly **18 days before risshū (early August)**, so about **20 July – 6 August**; the airing day is chosen for **low humidity (below 60%) and a breeze**, after **2–3 consecutive dry days**. Also done in **autumn (mid-October)** and **midwinter (late January–February)** — the "three airings" |
| **Why then** | After the rainy season (**June–mid-July**, indoor humidity 70–85%), stored textiles and paper hold moisture; **mould germinates above 60–65% RH** and **clothes moths and carpet beetles lay eggs in early summer**. The first dry heat is the window to dry things out and catch damage early |
| **Shade, not sun** | **UV fades silk and dyes, yellows paper and lignin, dries out leather and lacquer**. Hang under the eaves, indoors by an open window, or in a shaded room with a fan |
| **How long** | **3–5 hours** on the day; **10:00–15:00** when humidity is lowest |
| **Kimono** | On a **kimono hanger (ikō)**, sleeves spread, **inside out**; check for **spots (mould), yellowing, insect holes**; refold along the original creases; new **tissue paper (tatōshi)** if stained |
| **Bedding, futon** | Flat on a rail in the shade or sun (cotton futon tolerate sun; **down and wool in shade**); beat gently or vacuum |
| **Books, scrolls, photos** | **Stand books open and fan the pages**; **unroll scrolls partially** in a dry room; replace desiccant in boxes |
| **Afterwards** | Return only when **fully cool and dry**; add **fresh desiccant** and **one type of mothball** (see the mothproofing article); keep a **humidity indicator card** in the box |

- **What you're looking for**: **white or green spots** (mould — brush outdoors, wipe with diluted alcohol on cottons, professional cleaning for silk), **small holes** and **casings** (moths and beetles — freeze wool items 48 h or dry-clean, clean the drawer), **foxing** on paper (brown spots — stabilise with dry storage; don't wipe).
- **Modern equivalents**: a **dehumidifier running in the closed room with drawers and closet open** for an afternoon (target **50% RH**), or the **bathroom drying mode** for bedding. **Vacuum-sealing isn't a substitute** — it traps whatever moisture is already there (see the compression-bag article).
- **Apartments without balconies**: open the wardrobe, run a fan through it, lay things on the bed.
- **Records**: households with heirloom kimono keep a **notebook of airing dates and condition** — the earliest "collection management" most people ever do.
- **Doyō and the eel**: the same calendar term is why Japan eats **eel on Doyō no Ushi no Hi** — the tradition of getting through the heat, not just protecting the wardrobe.
