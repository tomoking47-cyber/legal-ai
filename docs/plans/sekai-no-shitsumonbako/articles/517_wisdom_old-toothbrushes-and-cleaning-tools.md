# Q. What do people get wrong about the Japanese habit of cleaning with old toothbrushes and chopsticks?｜Japanese Everyday Wisdom #517

- Shelf: `wisdom` ／ URL: `/en/wisdom/old-toothbrushes-and-cleaning-tools` ／ Status: **Draft — sources unverified**

Ask a Japanese household how they clean window tracks, the gap around the tap, or the grooves of a gas hob, and the answer is usually an old toothbrush, a disposable chopstick wrapped in cloth, or a cotton bud. It looks like thrift — and it is — but the tools are chosen because they fit the millimetre-scale gaps that Japanese fittings have and that commercial brushes do not.

## A. The common misunderstanding is that these are second-best tools. For narrow grooves they are the right tools: a toothbrush head fits a sliding-door track and a tap base, a chopstick wrapped in a damp cloth or a sock cleans the window sash channel and the rubber seal of a fridge, and a cotton bud reaches the drain-lid rim. Keep a box of retired toothbrushes (marked with a tape colour so they never go back to the bathroom), a bundle of disposable chopsticks and a roll of old cotton T-shirt cut into squares, and most of the "detailing" jobs in a Japanese apartment need nothing bought. What people get wrong is using them on scratchable surfaces, or on the toilet and then anywhere else.

## The kit and its jobs

| Tool | Best for | Don't use on |
|---|---|---|
| **Old toothbrush** | **Tap bases, sink rim, grout, shower-head holes, window-lock hardware, gas-hob burner caps, the rubber seal of the washing-machine door, shoe welts, trainers' soles** | Non-stick pans, acrylic bathtubs (scratches), screens |
| **Disposable chopstick + damp cloth (or an old sock over it)** | **Window sash tracks, sliding-door rails, the gap between hob and counter, fridge door seals, air-conditioner louvres, keyboard gaps** | Anything you might poke through (screens, speaker fabric) |
| **Chopstick + cotton bud / toothpick** | **Drain-lid rim, phone ports (dry), tap aerator, remote-control buttons** | — |
| **Old T-shirt squares (zōkin cloth)** | Wet wiping, polishing, wrapping tools | — |
| **Old stockings/tights** | **Polishing stainless and mirrors** (lint-free), **stuffed with soap scraps** for a scrubber | — |
| **Bread bag clips / cards** | Scraping soap scum from tiles | Coated surfaces |
| **Disposable chopstick sharpened** | **Hair from drain covers**, pushing cloth into corners | — |

## The rules that make it hygienic

- **Colour-code**: **tape or a marker on the handle** — one colour for kitchen, one for bathroom, one for toilet. **A toilet toothbrush never travels.** Retire toothbrushes to the kitchen only after a run through the dishwasher or a 1-minute boil.
- **Sanitise between jobs**: rinse, then a **dip in diluted oxygen bleach** or a wipe with alcohol; **dry standing up**.
- **Throw away**: when bristles splay or after a mould job. They cost nothing — don't keep a manky one.
- **Chemicals**: a toothbrush **carries cleaner into gaps** — use **neutral detergent** or **baking soda paste** in kitchens; **citric acid** for limescale around taps (**never with chlorine**); a **melamine sponge** for marks on hard, uncoated surfaces only.
- **Where the habit comes from**: Japanese homes have **many narrow tracks and rails** (sliding doors, shōji, window screens), **hobs with removable parts**, and **tight bathrooms**; commercial "detail brushes" exist (**¥100 shops sell sets**) but a toothbrush is the same tool.
- **Year-end cleaning (ōsōji)** is when the whole box comes out: tracks, sash channels, hob grooves and drain rims are the day's list (see the year-end clean article).
- **Kids**: an old toothbrush and a bowl of soapy water is how many Japanese children first "help" — cleaning the bath toys.
