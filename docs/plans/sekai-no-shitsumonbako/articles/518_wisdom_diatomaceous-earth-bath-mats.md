# Q. What do people get wrong about the diatomaceous-earth bath mat and its recall?｜Japanese Everyday Wisdom #518

- Shelf: `wisdom` ／ URL: `/en/wisdom/diatomaceous-earth-bath-mats` ／ Status: **Draft — sources unverified**

Around 2015 a hard, chalky slab replaced the towelling bath mat in many Japanese bathrooms: the keisōdo (diatomaceous earth) mat, which drinks a wet footprint in seconds and dries itself. In 2020 several brands were recalled for asbestos contamination, and the product has never fully recovered its reputation. What it does well, what went wrong, and how to use one safely deserve separating.

## A. The common misunderstanding is either that all diatomaceous-earth mats are dangerous, or that the recall was nothing. The 2020 recall covered specific products — imported mats and coasters in which the earth had been mixed with asbestos-containing material — sold by major retailers, who published lot lists and took them back. Mats from those lists should not be sanded or broken; return or dispose of them as instructed. Products made after the recall under Japanese labelling are tested for asbestos, and a mat that is intact and used as a mat poses no exposure. The everyday problems are different: the mat stops absorbing when soap scum clogs its pores (sand lightly with the supplied paper outdoors, or wash with a brush), it cracks if dropped or stepped on unevenly, and it grows mould if left on a wet floor.

## What it is and what happened

| Item | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Material** | **Diatomaceous earth (keisōdo)** — fossilised diatom shells, porous silica — pressed with binders into a board. **Absorbs water instantly by capillary action, then evaporates it** |
| **Why people liked it** | No wet towelling mat, **no washing**, fast drying, no smell, lasts **2–3 years** |
| **The 2020–2021 recall** | **Asbestos was found in some products** (mats, coasters, spoons) sold by **Nitori, Cainz, Nafco, Canon-related brands and others**; the earth had been mixed with asbestos-containing material at overseas factories. Retailers published **lot numbers and product lists**, offered **refunds and collection**. Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare guidance: **do not sand, cut or break** a suspect product; **wrap and return** |
| **Risk in practice** | Asbestos is hazardous when **fibres become airborne** — from **sanding, cutting or breaking**. **An intact mat used as a mat** does not release fibres; the concern was people **sanding recalled mats to restore absorbency** |
| **Now** | Products sold in Japan carry **"asbestos not detected" test claims**; the **Industrial Safety and Health Act** bans asbestos-containing products above 0.1% |

## Using one well

- **Restore absorbency**: when footprints stay wet, **soap and skin oils have clogged the pores**. **Wash with water and a brush**, dry in shade; if still poor, **sand lightly with the supplied fine paper, outdoors, with a mask, wetting the surface** — only on a product you know is post-recall and tested. **Never sand a mat from the recall lists.**
- **Mould**: keep it **standing on its edge after use** (most come with a stand); **wipe with diluted alcohol** if spots appear; a mat that stays wet underneath grows black mould in the grooves.
- **Cracking**: place on a **flat floor**, not over a tile edge; **don't drop it**. Some mats have a **soft version (diatomaceous earth mixed into a foam mat)** that avoids the cracking and the sanding.
- **Kitchen uses**: **drainer boards, cup coasters, spoons for salt and sugar** — same material, same rules.
- **Disposal**: **non-burnable / ceramic waste** in most municipalities (check); **recalled items go back to the retailer**, not the bin.
- **Alternatives**: quick-dry microfibre mats (washable), cedar duckboards (traditional, need drying), and the towelling mat washed twice a week — which is what most households went back to.

> **[Verify]** Retailer names and the exact scope of the 2020–2021 recall with the MHLW/METI lists; asbestos threshold (0.1%) with the Industrial Safety and Health Act.
