# Q. What do people get wrong about kakeibo, the Japanese household account book?｜Japanese Everyday Wisdom #520

- Shelf: `wisdom` ／ URL: `/en/wisdom/kakeibo-household-budget-book` ／ Status: **Draft — sources unverified**

Every January, Japanese bookshops stack the year's kakeibo — printed household ledgers with envelopes, monthly summary pages and columns for food, utilities and "special expenses". The practice dates from 1904 and a magazine for housewives; it went global in the 2010s as a mindfulness-flavoured budgeting method. In Japan it remains a plain tool, and most households that use it use it in a simpler way than the books abroad describe.

## A. The common misunderstanding is that kakeibo is a strict, item-by-item accounting exercise. Households that keep one long-term do the opposite: they decide the month's fixed costs and savings first, put the remainder into four or five broad categories (food, daily goods, leisure, clothing/medical, other), record totals daily or weekly from receipts, and look at one number at month-end — how much was left. The value is the weekly glance, not the precision. Whether on paper (¥500–1,500 a year), a free app, or a spreadsheet, the routine is: fixed costs and savings out first, a weekly food budget in cash or on one card, and a monthly review of three or four lines.

## The method as households actually run it

| Step | What | Japanese practice |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Fixed costs first** | Rent/mortgage, utilities, insurance, phone, school fees, loan repayments | Written once at the top of the month; **auto-debited**; not tracked daily |
| **2. Savings second** | A fixed amount moved on payday — "**sakidori chokin**" (take savings first) | Typical target **10–20% of take-home**; many use an **automatic transfer** or **company savings (zaikei)** |
| **3. Remainder = living budget** | Split into **4–5 categories**: **food**, **daily goods (nichiyōhin)**, **leisure/eating out**, **clothing & medical**, **special (gifts, travel)** | The **weekly food budget** is the one most kept in cash — the envelope method (**fūtō-wake**) — or on a single card |
| **4. Record** | Totals from receipts **daily or weekly** — by category, not item | 5 minutes; receipts go in a clip; **rounding to ¥100 is normal** |
| **5. Month-end** | Compare category totals to budget; **note the one thing to change**; carry surplus to savings or next month | The printed books give **one page a month** for this |
| **6. Special expenses** | A separate **annual list**: car tax (May), property tax, insurance, New Year gifts, school fees, appliances | Divided by 12 and set aside monthly — the step that separates households that "always have a surprise cost" from those that don't |

- **Why it works**: it makes the **living budget explicit** and the **food budget weekly** — the only two numbers most households can actually change. The rest is fixed.
- **Paper vs app**: paper kakeibo (**Fujin no Tomo, Takahashi, Kodansha; ¥500–1,500**) suit people who buy mostly in cash; **apps (Zaim, MoneyForward ME, Moneytree)** link to bank and card accounts and auto-categorise — **most under-40s use an app**, most over-60s paper. **Receipt-scanning** covers cash.
- **The Fujin no Tomo model** (Hani Motoko, 1904): budget by category **at the start of the year**, and record **only against those categories**; the founder's line — "**the point is not to record, but to plan**" — is still on the first page.
- **Typical Japanese household figures** (2-person-plus, Statistics Bureau, 2023): monthly consumption **about ¥290,000**; **food ~27%**, **utilities ~8%**, **transport/communications ~14%**, **leisure ~10%**. Households compare their own share to these.
- **Common failure**: **too many categories** and **tracking every item** — people quit by March. Four categories and a weekly total survive.
- **Couples**: a **shared kakeibo for household costs** and **separate personal allowances (okozukai)** is the standard Japanese arrangement; the allowance survey (Shinsei Bank, annual) is a national talking point.
- **Kids**: children's kakeibo (**okozukai-chō**) are sold for pocket money; schools use them in home-economics classes.
