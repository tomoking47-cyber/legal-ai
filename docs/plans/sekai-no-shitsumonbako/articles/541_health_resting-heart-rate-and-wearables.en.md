# Q. How do I read the heart rate on my smartwatch? What is a normal resting heart rate?｜Japan Questions #541

- Shelf: `health` ／ URL: `/en/health/resting-heart-rate-and-wearables` ／ Status: **Draft — English edition, sources unverified**

Smartwatches and fitness trackers are everywhere in Japan too, and many people now check their "resting heart rate", "heart rate variability" and "sleep score" every morning. A number on a screen is hard to ignore. But many people worry without knowing what is normal and what is a reason to see a doctor. Here are the guide figures, and the limits of the devices.

## A. For adults, a resting heart rate of 60–100 beats per minute is generally described as the "normal range", and people who exercise regularly are often fine in the 50s. What matters more than the absolute number is the gap from your own usual figure: several days running at 10 or more beats above your normal can signal the start of a cold, lack of sleep, dehydration, alcohol or stress. On the other hand, wearables measure heart rate with an optical sensor on the wrist, so errors appear during exercise and with irregular rhythms. The signs to see a doctor are "always above 100 at rest", "below 50 with dizziness or breathlessness" and "repeated alerts about skipped or irregular beats" — in that case take the device's records to a cardiology clinic (循環器内科, junkanki-naika). If looking at the numbers every day makes you anxious, checking once a week is enough.

## Heart rate guide figures

| Item | Guide | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Resting heart rate (adults)** | **60–100 beats/min** is the normal range. **50–70** is the measured range for many healthy adults | **People who exercise regularly are often fine in the 40s–50s** ("athlete's heart") |
| **Caution at the low end** | See a doctor if **below 50** with **dizziness, breathlessness or fainting** | Without symptoms, watchful waiting is usually fine |
| **Caution at the high end** | **Consistently above 100 at rest** (tachycardia). **Thyroid problems, anaemia, dehydration, fever, caffeine or medicines** may be behind it | See a doctor if it lasts several days |
| **"Gap from your usual"** | **10 or more beats above your normal for several days**: an indicator of a cold starting, lack of sleep, alcohol, dehydration or stress | The most practical use of a wearable |
| **Heart rate variability (HRV)** | Fluctuation in the interval between beats. **Higher means recovery and relaxation**; lower tends to mean fatigue or stress | **Varies hugely between people; comparing with others is meaningless.** Watch your own trend |
| **During exercise** | **Maximum heart rate ≈ 220 − age**. Moderate intensity is **60–70% of that**, high intensity **70–85%** | At 40: maximum 180, moderate 108–126 |
| **During sleep** | Normally **10–20% lower** than at rest | If it stays high: alcohol, fever or possible sleep apnoea |

- **Accuracy of wearables**: many validations find **heart rate at rest and when walking is close to medical-device accuracy** (errors of a few beats). Errors increase with **intense exercise or sports with big arm movements (tennis, weight training)**, **a cold wrist**, and **tattoos or darker skin tones**. **Wear it one finger-width above the wrist bone, neither too tight nor too loose.**
- **Irregular-rhythm alerts**: the **atrial fibrillation (AF) notification feature on the Apple Watch and others is approved as a medical device in Japan too**; **if alerts keep coming, see a cardiology clinic**. But **one alert is not a diagnosis**. **The ECG function records 30 seconds**, and showing it at your appointment helps.
- **Sleep score, stress score**: **each maker's own index, with no medical definition**; use them only for day-to-day comparison. Some people **chase the score until they cannot sleep (orthosomnia)** — if it bothers you too much, take the device off at night.
- **How to use your records**: **at an appointment, show "the trend of your resting heart rate" and "the heart rate at the time your symptoms appeared"** — it gives the doctor something to work with. **Use a screenshot or the app's sharing function.**
- **Symptoms to act on (whatever the device says)**: **palpitations with chest pain, cold sweat or breathlessness**, **fainting**, **a feeling of skipped beats that goes on for a long time**. **Chest pain with cold sweat means an ambulance (call 119).**

> **[Verify]** The normal range for resting heart rate and the formula for estimating maximum heart rate are general cardiology guide figures. Check the statements on wearable accuracy against each device's validation papers and approval information.

## Misunderstanding 1｜A resting heart rate in the 50s means a weak heart

**In people who exercise regularly it is normal, and if anything a sign of good cardiorespiratory fitness.** Without symptoms (dizziness, breathlessness, fainting) there is no cause for concern.

## Misunderstanding 2｜Wearable numbers are accurate, so they can be used directly for diagnosis

**Accuracy at rest is high, but errors appear during exercise and with irregular rhythms.** The right use is to show them to a doctor as reference data.

## Misunderstanding 3｜Low HRV means illness

**HRV varies enormously between people; comparing with others or with an "average" is meaningless.** It is an index for spotting fatigue and recovery in your own trend.

## Misunderstanding 4｜One irregular-rhythm alert means something terrible has happened

**A single alert can be a measurement error.** If they repeat, or come with symptoms, get an ECG at a cardiology clinic.

## Misunderstanding 5｜Checking every number every day makes you healthier

**The more you chase the numbers, the more anxious some people get, and sleep can suffer.** A weekly look at the trend, plus a record when you have symptoms, is enough.
