# Q. When should I eat the fruit I bought? Judging ripeness and storing fruit well｜Japan Questions #553

- Shelf: `trend` ／ URL: `/en/trend/ripening-fruit-and-ethylene` ／ Status: **Draft — English edition, sources unverified**

A rock-hard kiwi, a green banana, a persimmon that is still sour. Some fruit "needs a few more days"; other fruit should be eaten the day you buy it. The difference is whether the fruit ripens after picking. If you do not know which is which, you end up with fruit that stays hard in the fridge for ever. Japanese supermarkets sell plenty of both kinds, so here is the sorting.

## A. Fruit divides into "types that ripen after harvest (climacteric fruit)" and "types that ripen only on the tree". Bananas, kiwis, avocados, European pears, peaches, mangoes and persimmons keep ripening, so if they are hard, wait at room temperature and refrigerate once they are ready. To speed ripening, put them in a bag with an apple or a banana (which give off ethylene gas). Strawberries, grapes, mikan (mandarins), watermelon, pineapple and cherries do not ripen further, so they are ready when you buy them: refrigerate at once and eat soon. Apples give off a lot of ethylene, so storing them with other fruit and vegetables spoils those faster (keep them away from leafy greens and kiwis).

## Fruit that ripens after picking, and fruit that does not

| Ripens after picking (at room temperature) | How to tell it is ready | Does not ripen further (ready when bought) |
|---|---|---|
| **Banana** | **Brown flecks on the skin (sugar spots)** | **Strawberry** |
| **Kiwi** | **Gives slightly when pressed gently** | **Grapes** |
| **Avocado** | **Skin darkens; slightly soft around the stem** | **Mikan, orange, grapefruit** |
| **European pear (La France)** | **Soft when pressed around the stalk**; fragrant | **Watermelon** (many melon varieties, such as netted melons, do ripen after harvest) |
| **Peach, plum, Japanese plum (sumomo)** | **Stronger scent, slightly soft all over** | **Pineapple** |
| **Mango, papaya** | Scent, glossy skin, slight give | **Cherries** |
| **Persimmon (kaki; some astringent types sweeten as they ripen)** | Orange to red all over, slightly soft | **Blueberries, raspberries** |
| **Loquat (biwa), fig** | Scent and softness | **Apple** (complete at harvest; the taste declines in storage) |

## Using ethylene, and avoiding it

| Aim | Method |
|---|---|
| **Speed up ripening** | **Put the fruit in a paper bag with one apple (or a ripe banana) at room temperature**. It softens in **1–3 days**. **A sealed plastic bag can lead to mould** |
| **Slow down ripening** | **Fridge (low temperature weakens the action of ethylene)**. But **bananas blacken below 13°C** and **avocados stop ripening and stay hard** |
| **Protect other foods** | **Apples, ripe bananas, melons and tomatoes** give off a lot of ethylene. Do not put them next to **leafy vegetables (they wilt), cucumbers (they yellow), or kiwis and avocados (they over-ripen)**. **Potatoes are the exception: ethylene suppresses sprouting** (see our separate article) |

- **What ethylene is**: a **plant-hormone gas** that fruit produces itself, driving ripening. **Damaged fruit gives off large amounts of ethylene**, so **one rotten piece spoils its neighbours fast**. Remove damaged fruit at once.
- **Bananas**: **separate them from the bunch**, **wrap the stems in cling film**, or **hang them on a banana stand** to delay blackening. **Once ripe, you can freeze them, skin and all** (for smoothies).
- **Avocados**: **buy them hard and ripen at room temperature (2–5 days)**, then refrigerate when ready (they keep 2–3 days). **Once cut, leave the stone in, brush the flesh with lemon juice and wrap**.
- **Kiwis**: **put hard kiwis in a bag with an apple for 2–3 days**. **Ripe kiwis keep a week in the fridge**.
- **Peaches**: **ripen at room temperature for 1–2 days**, then **refrigerate 2–3 hours before eating** (over-chilling dulls the sweetness). **Wrap in paper and keep out of sunlight**.
- **Persimmons**: **for astringent persimmons (shibugaki), dab shōchū spirit on the calyx and seal in a bag for a week** to remove the astringency. Sweet persimmons (amagaki) can be eaten as soon as you buy them.
- **Strawberries and grapes**: **wash only just before eating** (moisture leads to mould); **refrigerate in the pack and eat within 2–3 days**.
- **Mikan**: **a boxful spoils from the bottom**, so **open the box, let air in and remove any damaged fruit**. **Better in a well-ventilated cool, dark place at around 5–10°C than in the fridge**.

> **[Verify]** The classification of which fruit ripens after picking and how much ethylene each produces follows the general consensus in agricultural and food science. Check against material from MAFF and fruit-tree research institutes.

## Misunderstanding 1｜Fruit goes straight into the fridge

**Fruit that ripens after picking stops ripening in the fridge and stays hard.** Room temperature until ready, then the fridge.

## Misunderstanding 2｜Strawberries and grapes get sweeter if you leave them

**Fruit that does not ripen further is at its best when bought**; leaving it only lets it spoil. Refrigerate at once and eat soon.

## Misunderstanding 3｜Apples can be stored with anything

**Apples give off a lot of ethylene and spoil leafy greens, cucumbers and kiwis fast.** Keep them separate or bagged. Storing them with potatoes, on the other hand, is a plus.

## Misunderstanding 4｜Bananas keep longer in the fridge

**Below 13°C the skin blackens (chilling injury) and they look unappetising.** Hang them at room temperature and freeze them, skin on, once ripe.

## Misunderstanding 5｜One damaged piece of fruit can stay in the bowl

**Damaged fruit gives off large amounts of ethylene and spoils everything around it quickly.** Remove it as soon as you spot it.
