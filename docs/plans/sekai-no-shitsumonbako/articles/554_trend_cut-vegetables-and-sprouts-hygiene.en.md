# Q. Bagged cut vegetables and bean sprouts: can I eat them as they are? Should I wash them?｜Japan Questions #554

- Shelf: `trend` ／ URL: `/en/trend/cut-vegetables-and-sprouts-hygiene` ／ Status: **Draft — English edition, sources unverified**

Bagged cut vegetables (カット野菜, katto yasai), salad mixes and bean sprouts (もやし, moyashi) are staples of busy households in Japan. But opinions differ on whether they can be eaten unwashed, and whether moyashi can be eaten raw. Here is how they are made, and how to read the labels on the bag.

## A. Cut vegetables and salad mixes labelled "ready to eat as is" (そのままお召し上がりいただけます) or "no need to wash" (洗わずに食べられます) have been sanitised and washed in the factory with sodium hypochlorite or similar, and can be eaten straight from the bag (a light rinse is fine if you prefer). Bean sprouts are sold on the assumption that they will be cooked, and most packs say "please heat before eating" (加熱してお召し上がりください); avoid eating them raw. Once opened, both should be finished the same day, and even unopened the use-by date is a short 2–4 days. Cut vegetables lose a little nutrition from the cut surfaces, but the difference is small compared with not eating vegetables at all, and they are a reasonable way to make up a shortfall.

## How to handle each product

| Product | Wash? | Raw? | After opening | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Salad cut vegetables (labelled "ready to eat")** | **Not needed** (you may) | **Yes** | **Same day** | **Sanitised with sodium hypochlorite etc. → rinsed → spun dry → nitrogen-flushed**. Chlorine is controlled so none remains |
| **Stir-fry cut vegetables (cabbage, carrot, onion mixes etc.)** | Follow the label | Most are labelled **for cooking** | Same day to next day | No need to wash if you are cooking them |
| **Bean sprouts (moyashi)** | Follow the label (**a quick rinse** is usual) | **Cooking recommended**. Packs say "please heat before eating" | **Same day to next day**. **2–3 days even unopened** | **Sprouted and grown in the dark, in conditions that also suit bacteria**. Abroad, raw sprouts (including alfalfa) have repeatedly caused food poisoning |
| **Cut fruit** | Not needed | Yes | Same day | Bacteria grow readily on cut surfaces. Must be refrigerated |
| **Shredded cabbage** | Not needed (per label) | Yes | Same day | Many cut surfaces; loses freshness fastest |

- **How cut vegetables are sanitised**: **the "Standards for foods and additives" under the Food Sanitation Act (Japan's food hygiene law) set no sanitising standard specific to cut vegetables; each company manages its sanitising step under HACCP-based hygiene control, fully in force since June 2021. In practice, following the MHLW's "Sanitation management manual for mass catering facilities", sodium hypochlorite is used (about 200 ppm for 5 minutes, or 100 ppm for 10 minutes) and then rinsed thoroughly with water** (the concentration is an operational hygiene figure, not a product standard). **Residual chlorine is kept at or below tap-water level**, and you will hardly ever notice a smell. **They are not sterile, which is why the use-by date is short**.
- **Use-by date (消費期限, shōhi kigen)**: cut vegetables last **2–4 days from manufacture**. **Even unopened, eat within the date**; **once opened, the same day**. Discard if there are **droplets inside the bag, wilting or a change in smell**.
- **Temperature**: store at **10°C or below (refrigerated)**. **Pick them up last when shopping and carry them home in a cool bag**. Left in a car in summer, quality drops within tens of minutes.
- **On eating moyashi raw**: **factory moyashi are grown in water alone and need no pesticides**, but **bacteria from the seed plus the high-humidity environment** mean counts can be high. **Japan's industry association also recommends cooking**. **Blanch them even for namul or dressed salads** (**30 seconds to 1 minute** is enough; they stay crunchy).
- **Storing moyashi**: **refrigerate in the original bag and use within 2–3 days**. **To stretch this, pierce the bag or keep them in water in the fridge (change the water daily), which adds 1–2 days**, at some cost to flavour. **Freezing** makes them limp, but they are fine for stir-fries and soups.
- **Nutrition**: most measurements find **cut vegetables have around 10–30% less vitamin C than whole vegetables**, but **fibre and potassium are almost unchanged**. **"Washing washes out the nutrients"** applies only to some water-soluble vitamins, and the difference is small.
- **Cut vegetables as a top-up towards "350 g of vegetables a day"** (the Japanese government's target): a salad bag is **100–150 g**, a stir-fry bag **200–300 g**. A reasonable way to **fill the gap on days you fall short**.
- **Leftover salad**: **once dressed, it cannot be kept**. Set some aside before adding dressing.

> **[Verify]** Check the sodium hypochlorite concentration and residue controls against the Food Sanitation Act standards and industry guidelines, and the nutrient-loss figures for cut vegetables against measurements by public bodies.

## Misunderstanding 1｜Cut vegetables are washed in chemicals, so they are bad for you

**The sodium hypochlorite used for sanitising is rinsed off thoroughly, and residue is kept at or below tap-water level.** Rinsing them again at home is fine if you prefer.

## Misunderstanding 2｜Moyashi can go raw into a salad

**Moyashi are sold for cooking and should not be eaten raw.** Blanching for 30 seconds to 1 minute keeps the crunch.

## Misunderstanding 3｜Cut vegetables have almost no nutrition

**Vitamin C is down by around 10–30%; fibre and minerals are about the same.** They are a perfectly good way to make up a vegetable shortfall.

## Misunderstanding 4｜Unopened, they are fine past the use-by date

**Deterioration and bacterial growth start from the cut surfaces, which is why the date is set short.** Eat within the date, and the same day once opened.

## Misunderstanding 5｜Washing extends the use-by date

**Washing does not stop the cut surfaces deteriorating; the extra moisture in fact encourages mould and slime.** Wash only just before eating.
