# Q. How long do home-made jam and preserves keep? Is a sealed jar fine at room temperature?｜Japan Questions #555

- Shelf: `trend` ／ URL: `/en/trend/home-jam-and-preserves` ／ Status: **Draft — English edition, sources unverified**

Strawberry jam in season, marmalade from the yuzu in the garden, ume (Japanese plum) syrup. Home preserving is deeply rooted in Japanese households, but the assumptions "it is sealed, so it keeps a year at room temperature" and "it has sugar in it, so it cannot go off" are dangerous. Here is what can be made safely at home, looked at through sugar content, acidity and sterilisation.

## A. Home-made jam can be stored at room temperature only if it has a sugar content of 60% or more, is properly acidic, was poured hot into boiled jars, sealed and vacuum-sealed; even then, once opened it keeps about 2–3 weeks in the fridge. The now-popular "low-sugar jam" (40–50% sugar) keeps poorly: even sealed, 1–2 weeks in the fridge, and the freezer for anything longer. Far more dangerous are jarred low-acid vegetables, meat, fish and mushrooms, and oil preserves such as garlic in oil. Home heating (100°C) does not kill Clostridium botulinum spores, which can produce toxin inside a sealed container. Do not make room-temperature jarred preserves of low-acid foods at home; keep such foods refrigerated for a short time or freeze them.

## What can be made at home, and how long it keeps

| Type | Room temperature | Fridge | Freezer | Conditions |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **High-sugar jam (60–65% sugar or more)** | **Yes** (unopened, boiled jars, hot-filled, vacuum-sealed). **6 months to 1 year** as a guide | **2–3 weeks** once opened | Yes | **Acidic fruit** (strawberry, citrus, apple, apricot, blueberry). Add lemon juice to low-acid fruit |
| **Low-sugar jam (40–50% sugar)** | **No** | **1–2 weeks** | **1–2 months** (in portions) | Commercial low-sugar jam relies on additives and factory sterilisation |
| **Ume syrup, fruit vinegar** | Relatively stable thanks to high sugar and acid. **If it ferments (bubbles, alcohol smell), heat it** | Refrigeration recommended | — | Freezing the ume first speeds the process |
| **Umeboshi (salted plums, 18% salt or more)** | **Yes** (traditional method) | — | — | **Low-salt umeboshi (10% or less) mould easily. Refrigerate** |
| **Pickles (in vinegar)** | **Relatively stable if the vinegar ratio is high**, but **refrigeration still recommended** | **1–3 months** | — | Note that water from the vegetables dilutes the acidity |
| **Garlic or herbs in oil** | **No (dangerous)** | **Within 1 week** | Yes | **Botulism risk**. Many incidents abroad |
| **Jarred vegetables, mushrooms, meat, fish (low-acid)** | **Do not make at home** | Short term | Yes | **Needs pressure sterilisation (120°C)**, impossible in a domestic pan |

## Steps for making jam safely

1. **Boil the jars and lids** (**5–10 minutes in boiling water**) and dry them on a clean cloth.
2. **Cook with sugar equal to at least 50–60% of the fruit's weight** (60% or more if you are aiming for 60% sugar content). **Add lemon juice** (acid improves both keeping and setting).
3. **Reduce**: to **around 105°C** (a drop does not spread when dripped into water), or check the set with a chilled spoon.
4. **While hot (85°C / 185°F or above), fill the jars to the top and close the lids immediately**.
5. **Vacuum-seal**: **turn the closed jars upside down to cool**, or **boil the filled jars in water for 10 minutes (lids loosely on) and let them cool**. **If the centre of the lid is dimpled inwards once cool, the seal has worked**.
6. **Label with the date and sugar content** and store in a dark place. **Refrigerate once opened**.

- **Why sugar preserves**: **a high sugar content lowers the water activity, so bacteria and mould struggle to grow**. **60% is the benchmark**; below that, mould grows. **Low-sugar jam is popular for taste and health reasons, but regard it as a fresh food, not a preserve**.
- **About Clostridium botulinum**: spores germinate and produce toxin under the conditions **low acidity (pH 4.6 or higher), no oxygen and room temperature**. **Home heating at 100°C does not kill the spores**. **Garlic in oil, izushi (fermented fish) and home vacuum-packing** are the incident cases in Japan and abroad. **Jam made from acidic fruit has a low pH, so this concern basically does not apply**.
- **If mould appears**: **do not scrape off the top and eat the rest** (see our separate article on mouldy food). **Discard the whole jar**.
- **When giving jam as a gift**: write **the date, "refrigerate after opening" and, if the sugar is low, say so**. Tell people to keep low-sugar jam **in the fridge and eat it soon**.
- **Signs of fermentation**: **a "psst" when you open the lid, bubbles, an alcohol smell, a sour smell** — bacteria or yeast are growing. **Heat it before eating, or discard it**.

> **[Verify]** The sugar-content guide and the germination conditions for Clostridium botulinum (pH 4.6) are general food-hygiene figures. Check against MHLW and MAFF material.

## Misunderstanding 1｜Anything sealed keeps at room temperature

**A sealed container at room temperature is exactly what Clostridium botulinum favours.** Do not make room-temperature jarred preserves of low-acid foods at home.

## Misunderstanding 2｜It has sugar in it, so it cannot go off

**Keeping quality comes at 60% sugar or more.** Handle low-sugar jam like fresh food: 1–2 weeks in the fridge, or freeze.

## Misunderstanding 3｜I boiled it thoroughly, so all the bacteria are dead

**Clostridium botulinum spores are not killed at 100°C.** With acidic fruit the risk is low, but vegetables, meat and garlic are another matter.

## Misunderstanding 4｜Scrape off the mould and the rest is fine

**In a soft food like jam, the mould's threads spread beyond what you can see.** Discard the whole jar.

## Misunderstanding 5｜Home-made is additive-free, so it is safer than shop-bought

**Commercial jam relies on sterilisation steps and controlled sugar and acidity.** Understand home-made jam as "additive-free but poor keeping", and respect the times and temperatures.
