# Q. Eating wild plants or garden greens in Japan: which poisonous plants are easy to confuse?｜Japan Questions #557

- Shelf: `trend` ／ URL: `/en/trend/wild-plants-and-poisonous-lookalikes` ／ Status: **Draft — English edition, sources unverified**

Foraging for spring mountain vegetables (sansai), or picking the nira (garlic chives) and fuki (butterbur) growing in the garden: eating familiar wild plants is part of Japanese food culture. But every year there are poisonings from "mistaking daffodil for nira" or "mistaking autumn crocus for wild garlic", and some are fatal. MHLW statistics record several dozen cases of plant-toxin food poisoning a year. Here are the pairs most often confused, and how to prevent an accident for certain.

## A. Many edible and poisonous plants have very similar leaves, and even experts sometimes "cannot judge without the actual plant, roots included". Most household accidents fall into six patterns: (1) daffodil leaves mistaken for nira (garlic chives); (2) autumn crocus (inu-safuran) bulbs or leaves mistaken for gyōja-ninniku (Alpine leek), potato or onion; (3) false hellebore (baikeisō) mistaken for urui (hosta shoots); (4) aconite (torikabuto) mistaken for nirinsō (anemone) or momijigasa; (5) potato sprouts and green skin; (6) angel's trumpet (chōsen-asagao) root mistaken for burdock (gobō). Prevention: "if you cannot identify a plant with certainty, do not pick it, eat it or give it away"; "do not plant ornamental bulbs (daffodil, autumn crocus) next to vegetables in the garden"; and "the same applies to wild vegetables you are given". If nausea, diarrhoea or numbness appears within tens of minutes to a few hours of eating, take the remaining plant and see a doctor at once.

## Pairs that are easy to confuse

| Poisonous plant | Edible plant it is mistaken for | How to tell them apart (but check the actual plant) | Symptoms |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Daffodil (suisen)** (leaves, bulb) | **Nira (garlic chives), nobiru (wild rocambole), onion (bulb)** | **No nira smell** (nira smells strongly). Daffodil leaves are thicker and broader; the bulb is smaller than an onion | Nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea and headache **within 30 minutes** of eating. **The most common plant poisoning in Japan** |
| **Autumn crocus (inu-safuran, Colchicum)** (leaves, bulb) | **Gyōja-ninniku (Alpine leek), myōga (Japanese ginger), potato and onion (bulb)** | Gyōja-ninniku smells of garlic. Autumn crocus has no smell | Vomiting and diarrhoea from **colchicine**, leading to multiple organ failure. **Several deaths** |
| **False hellebore (baikeisō, kobaikeisō)** (young shoots) | **Urui (hosta, ōba-gibōshi), gyōja-ninniku** | False hellebore has deeply pleated leaves with parallel veins, wrapping the stem | Nausea, vomiting, low blood pressure, convulsions |
| **Aconite (torikabuto)** (young leaves) | **Nirinsō (anemone), momijigasa, yomogi (mugwort)** | Deeply divided leaves. **Extremely similar before flowering**. Grows mixed in the same spots | Numbness, arrhythmia and breathing difficulty from **aconitine**. **Highly lethal** |
| **Angel's trumpet (chōsen-asagao, Datura)** (root, seeds, leaves) | **Burdock (gobō) root, sesame (seeds), okra (buds), moroheiya (jute mallow)** | Thicker and shorter than burdock root; different smell | Hallucinations, agitation, dilated pupils, rapid pulse |
| **Potato** (sprouts, green skin, immature tubers) | — | See our separate article on storing potatoes | Nausea, stomach pain, diarrhoea |
| **Pokeweed (yōshu-yamagobō)** (root, berries) | **Yamagobō (edible, used for pickles), grapes (berries)** | Reddish-purple stems; clusters of blackish-purple berries | Vomiting, diarrhoea, convulsions |
| **Kuwazu-imo (Alocasia)** (tuber) | **Taro (satoimo)** | Long leaf stalks, no proper tuber | Severe pain and swelling in the mouth |
| **Hydrangea (ajisai)** (leaves) | Eaten by mistake as a garnish on a dish | — | Vomiting, dizziness |
| **Ginkgo nuts (ginnan)** (in quantity) | Edible, but **children should have no more than 5–6, and adults should avoid 10–20 or more** | — | Convulsions (a compound that causes vitamin B6 deficiency) |

## Statistics and prevention

- **Plant-toxin food poisoning**: **20–50 incidents a year, around 50–100 patients**, **0 to a few deaths a year** (MHLW food-poisoning statistics). **The number one plant is daffodil**, followed by potato, false hellebore and autumn crocus. **Autumn crocus causes few incidents but has a high death rate**.
- **How accidents happen**: **ornamental and edible plants side by side in the garden or vegetable plot** (daffodil and nira, autumn crocus and onion); **species growing mixed together on a foraging trip** (aconite and nirinsō); **eating wild vegetables you were given without question**; **children putting berries or leaves in their mouths**.
- **Principles of prevention**: (1) **If you cannot identify a plant with certainty, do not pick, eat, sell or give it away**. (2) **Do not plant ornamental bulbs near vegetables**, and do not store bulbs with vegetables. (3) **Go foraging with someone experienced and check every single stem**. (4) Make **checks with your senses, such as "does it smell of nira?"**, a habit. (5) **Do not trust the result of a photo-identification app**.
- **If you have eaten some**: **nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea, numbness, dizziness, hallucinations**. **Take the remaining plant, the cooked dish and any vomit** and see a doctor at once (**the emergency department is fine**). Identifying the plant guides the medical care. **Japan Poison Information Center hotline (中毒110番)**: Osaka 072-727-2499 (24 hours), Tsukuba 029-852-9999 (9:00–21:00).
- **Children and pets**: **teach children not to put garden berries or leaves in their mouths**, and **know which poisonous plants (daffodil, angel's trumpet, oleander, red spider lily) you have planted around the house**.

## Misunderstanding 1｜The nira growing in my garden is safe

**Daffodil leaves mixed in with nira is the most common accident of all.** Smell every stem as you pick, and do not eat nira growing near daffodils.

## Misunderstanding 2｜Compare with a field guide or an app and you can tell them apart

**Before flowering, aconite and nirinsō, or false hellebore and urui, are so alike that even experts need the actual plant.** Photo identification is dangerous.

## Misunderstanding 3｜Cooking destroys the poison

**The toxins in daffodil, autumn crocus and aconite are not broken down by heat.** Not eating them is the only countermeasure.

## Misunderstanding 4｜I have eaten these wild plants for years, so I will not get it wrong

**Experienced foragers have accidents every year.** Toxic species grow mixed with edible ones, and the young-shoot stage is when they are hardest to tell apart.

## Misunderstanding 5｜I only ate a little, so I will wait and see

**Autumn crocus and aconite can be lethal even in small amounts.** Nausea or numbness after eating a plant means: take the rest of the plant and see a doctor at once.
