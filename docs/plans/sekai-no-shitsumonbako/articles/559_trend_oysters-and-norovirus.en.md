# Q. Are raw oysters safe in winter? What is the difference between "for raw consumption" and "for cooking"?｜Japan Questions #559

- Shelf: `trend` ／ URL: `/en/trend/oysters-and-norovirus` ／ Status: **Draft — English edition, sources unverified**

Raw oysters in winter, deep-fried kaki furai, oyster hot-pot. Oysters are a taste of the Japanese winter, but they are also a well-known cause of norovirus food poisoning. In Japanese shops, oysters are sold either as "for raw consumption" (生食用, seishoku-yō) or "for cooking" (加熱用, kanetsu-yō). Many people assume "raw-grade is fresh, cooking-grade is old", but the difference is not freshness; it is the sea area they were harvested from and how they were processed.

## A. The difference between "for raw consumption" and "for cooking" is not freshness but the harvest area and whether the oysters were purified. Raw-grade oysters come from prefecture-designated "raw-consumption oyster harvesting areas" (waters with little sewage influence that meet standards for E. coli and other bacteria), or have been purified in UV-sterilised seawater, and meet the compositional standards of the Food Sanitation Act. Cooking-grade oysters come from other waters; their freshness is often equal or better, and cooked they are delicious and safe. But even raw-grade oysters carry a norovirus risk that is never zero (purification does not remove all the virus), and winter (November to March) coincides with the norovirus season. Cook to a core temperature of 85–90°C (185–194°F) for at least 90 seconds; fry until the centre is done inside the coating. Older people, pregnant women, children and people with weakened immunity are safest avoiding raw oysters.

## Raw-grade vs cooking-grade

| Item | For raw consumption (生食用) | For cooking (加熱用) |
|---|---|---|
| **Sea area** | **Prefecture-designated "raw-consumption oyster harvesting areas"** (little influence from river mouths or sewage; meet bacterial-count standards) | Non-designated waters (often nutrient-rich, near river mouths; **the meat is often bigger and richer**) |
| **Processing** | **Purified for around 18–20 hours in UV-sterilised seawater** (to reduce bacteria). Meets the **compositional standards** (bacterial count, E. coli, Vibrio parahaemolyticus) | No purification. **No compositional standard** |
| **Freshness** | Equal | **Equal** (they are not "old") |
| **Norovirus** | **Purification reduces bacteria but does not remove all the virus**. The risk is lower but not zero | Sold for cooking, so no problem once cooked |
| **Labelling** | "生食用" (raw), "消費期限" (use-by date), "採取海域" (harvest area), "殻を外した日" (date shucked) | "加熱用" or "加熱調理用" (for cooking) |
| **Price** | Higher | Lower |

## Norovirus and cooking

| Item | Details |
|---|---|
| **Why oysters** | Bivalves **filter more than 200 L of seawater a day** to feed, and **concentrate viruses from the water in the digestive gland**. Norovirus **multiplies only in the human gut and reaches the sea via sewage** |
| **Season** | **November to March** is the peak. **The virus persists longer in cold seawater**, and this overlaps with the human outbreak season |
| **Cooking conditions** | **Core temperature 85–90°C for at least 90 seconds** (MHLW). **The surface can be browned while the centre has not reached temperature** (large fried oysters, oysters dipped briefly in hot-pot) |
| **Symptoms** | **24–48 hours** after eating: **vomiting, diarrhoea, stomach pain, slight fever**, **recovering in 1–2 days**. **Secondary infection from vomit and stools** (it spreads within the household) |
| **Infectiousness** | **10–100 virus particles are enough to infect**. **Alcohol sanitiser works poorly; sodium hypochlorite (chlorine bleach) is effective** |
| **Statistics** | Norovirus food poisoning: **100–300 incidents and 5,000–10,000 patients a year**, **the number one causative agent by patient numbers**. Bivalves including oysters account for part of this; **most cases come from contamination via food handlers' hands** |

- **At home**: **eat raw-grade oysters within the use-by date, and straight away once the pack is opened**. **Cook cooking-grade oysters through to the centre** — **fry at 170–180°C for 3–4 minutes and break a large one open to check**; **in hot-pot, another 1–2 minutes after the flesh plumps and shrinks**; **the microwave heats unevenly and is unsuitable**.
- **Cross-contamination while cooking**: **do not touch other foods with hands, boards or knives that have touched raw oysters**. **Wash the sink area with detergent where oyster-washing water has splashed**. **Wash hands with soap after cooking**.
- **Oysters in the shell**: eat raw **only shell-on oysters labelled "生食用"**. **Shell-on cooking-grade oysters must be cooked**. **For grilled oysters, cook a further 2–3 minutes after the shell opens**.
- **Lemon, vinegar, wasabi**: **have no effect on norovirus**.
- **If you feel ill afterwards**: **keep up fluids** (oral rehydration solution), **clean up vomit wearing gloves and a mask, with diluted chlorine bleach (0.1%)**, and **keep washing hands, because the virus is shed in stools for about a week after symptoms stop**. **People who cook for a living should stay off food handling for several days after recovering**.
- **Who should avoid raw oysters**: **older people, pregnant women, infants, people with weakened immunity and people taking acid-suppressing medicines**. There is also a risk of **Vibrio parahaemolyticus (summer) and hepatitis A virus**.

> **[Verify]** Check the compositional standards and purification conditions for raw-grade oysters against the Food Sanitation Act standards, and the norovirus statistics against the latest MHLW year.

## Misunderstanding 1｜Raw-grade is fresher, cooking-grade is old

**The difference is harvest area and purification; freshness is the same.** Cooking-grade oysters often grow near river mouths and are bigger and richer.

## Misunderstanding 2｜Raw-grade means no norovirus worries

**Purification reduces bacteria but does not remove all the virus, so the risk never reaches zero.** Take particular care in the winter season.

## Misunderstanding 3｜Lemon or vinegar sterilises them

**Norovirus resists acid and alcohol; only heat or chlorine deals with it.**

## Misunderstanding 4｜A quick dip in the hot-pot counts as cooked

**A core temperature of 85–90°C for at least 90 seconds is needed**, and a quick dip does not get there. Another 1–2 minutes after the flesh plumps and shrinks.

## Misunderstanding 5｜Only the person who ate the oysters gets ill

**Norovirus spreads to the family via vomit, stools and hands.** Clean up with gloves and chlorine bleach, and wash hands thoroughly.
