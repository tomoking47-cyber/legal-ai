# Q. What do people get wrong about drying persimmons and daikon at home?｜Japanese Everyday Wisdom #592

- Shelf: `wisdom` ／ URL: `/en/wisdom/home-drying-persimmons-and-daikon` ／ Status: **Draft — sources unverified**

Every November, strings of peeled orange persimmons appear under the eaves of Japanese country houses, and rows of shredded white radish dry on nets over the veranda. Hoshigaki (dried persimmon) and kiriboshi daikon (dried radish) are the two home-drying traditions that survived supermarkets, and both fail for city dwellers who try them without the two conditions they need: cold, dry air and moving wind.

## A. The common misunderstanding is that drying food is about sun. It is about low humidity, low temperature and airflow: hoshigaki are started only after the first cold spell (daytime under 15°C, nights near 5°C, humidity under 60%), because in warm humid air the peeled fruit moulds within two days. Peel astringent persimmons, dip in boiling water for 5–10 seconds, hang them without touching each other in a shaded, windy spot for 2–3 weeks, and massage them gently from day 7 to draw the sugar to the surface. Kiriboshi daikon is easier — shred, spread on a net, 3–5 days of dry weather — and both can be done on an apartment balcony with a fan and a mesh drying basket. Bring everything in when rain or fog is forecast.

## Hoshigaki (dried persimmon)

| Step | How | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **When** | **Mid-November to December** in most of Japan: **daytime below 15°C, dry air, no rain forecast for a week** | Above ~20°C or in humidity the fruit moulds |
| **Fruit** | **Astringent varieties (shibugaki: Hachiya, Ichida, Hiratanenashi)** with the **T-shaped stem intact**. Sweet persimmons dry poorly | Drying removes the astringency and concentrates sugar |
| **Peel** | Peel thinly, leave the calyx and stem; **tie string around the stem** (or hook the T) | — |
| **Blanch** | **Dip 5–10 seconds in boiling water** (or spray with shōchū) | Kills surface mould spores — the step most beginners skip |
| **Hang** | **Under eaves or a covered balcony, in shade with wind**, fruits **not touching**, **on a rod or hanger**; **a mesh drying basket or net keeps birds and insects off** | Sun darkens the skin and hardens it; shade + wind dries evenly |
| **Massage** | From **day 7**, **gently knead each fruit every 2–3 days** | Breaks internal fibres, evens moisture, draws sugar to the surface (the white bloom is crystallised glucose, not mould) |
| **Done** | **2–3 weeks**: firm outside, soft inside (anpo-gaki at ~50% moisture, 1–2 weeks) or fully dried (**korogaki**, 3–4 weeks, white bloom) | — |
| **Store** | **Refrigerate 1–2 weeks, freeze 6 months** in bags; the white bloom develops in the cold | Home-dried has no preservatives |
| **Mould vs bloom** | **Bloom is white, dry, powdery, all over; mould is fuzzy, green/black/blue, in spots**. Mould → discard that fruit (and check neighbours) | — |

## Kiriboshi daikon (dried shredded radish)

- **Shred** daikon into **5 mm × 5 cm** strips (or 1 cm batons for "wari-boshi"); **spread in a single layer on a mesh drying net (¥500–1,500 at 100-yen and home-centre stores)**.
- **Dry 3–5 days** in **cold, sunny, windy weather** (**sun is fine here** — the daikon browns slightly and sweetens); **bring in at night and in rain**; **turn once a day**. **1 kg daikon → ~100 g dried**.
- **Done** when brittle and pale amber. **Store in a jar with a desiccant, 1–2 months; refrigerate in summer**.
- **Use**: **soak 15–20 minutes** (the soaking water is sweet dashi), then **simmer with carrot, aburaage, soy and mirin** — the classic side dish.

## City-balcony adaptations

- **A small circulating fan on low** replaces the wind; **a north or east balcony** replaces the eaves; **a hanging mesh basket (三段干し網, 3-tier)** replaces the string across the veranda.
- **Check the humidity**: **under 60% RH** for persimmons; Tokyo's November average is ~60%, so **pick the dry spells after a north wind**.
- **Other things to dry in the same weeks**: **sliced shiitake, sweet potato (hoshi-imo, steamed first), yuzu peel, chillies, mikan peel (for baths)**.
