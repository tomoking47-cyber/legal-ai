# Q. What do people get wrong about the yuzu bath on the winter solstice?｜Japanese Everyday Wisdom #596

- Shelf: `wisdom` ／ URL: `/en/wisdom/yuzu-bath-winter-solstice` ／ Status: **Draft — sources unverified**

On the shortest day of the year — around 21 December — Japanese bathhouses and homes float yellow yuzu citrus in the hot bath. It is one of the most-kept seasonal customs, backed by a proverb (bathe with yuzu at the solstice and you won't catch cold all winter), and it comes with a real chemistry lesson about citrus oils and skin.

## A. The common misunderstanding is that yuzu-yu is a folk practice that either does nothing or keeps colds away. It does neither: the fragrance and the warmth are the point — a warm bath before bed raises core temperature and helps sleep, and the citrus scent is relaxing — while yuzu peel oils (limonene, citral) can sting broken or sensitive skin and, in children and atopic skin, cause redness. Float 3–5 whole fruit in a 40°C bath for a family soak of 10–15 minutes; do not cut or squeeze them into the water unless you are prepared for the oil, and skip it for babies under one and anyone with an eczema flare. Rinse the tub afterwards — the oils stain and film — and never reheat or reuse yuzu bathwater for laundry.

## The custom and the chemistry

| Item | Detail |
|---|---|
| **When** | **Tōji (winter solstice), 21 or 22 December**. Public baths (sentō) and many onsen offer yuzu-yu that day, often free for children |
| **The saying** | "**Tōji ni yuzu-yu ni hairu to kaze o hikanai**" — bathe with yuzu at the solstice and you won't catch a cold. Also a pun: **yuzu ↔ yūzū (融通, flexibility)**, tōji ↔ **tōji (湯治, a hot-spring stay for health)** |
| **The yuzu** | **Citrus junos**, ripe and yellow in December; **¥100–200 each**, sold in nets "for the bath" at supermarkets in the week before |
| **How many** | **3–5 whole fruit** in a 200 L family bath; sentō use dozens in a net |
| **Whole or cut?** | **Whole** for gentle fragrance and no sting; **cut/squeezed** releases peel oil and juice → strong scent, **more skin irritation**, more mess. Compromise: **whole fruit in a mesh bag**, or **a few shallow slits** |
| **Skin** | Peel oils (**limonene, citral, and furanocoumarins in some citrus**) can cause **stinging, redness, itching**, especially on **dry winter skin, eczema, shaved skin, babies**. **Rinse with plain shower water after** if sensitive. **Photosensitivity is a theoretical risk with cut citrus + sunlight, minor for an evening bath** |
| **Health effect** | **Warm bath (40°C, 10–15 min) 1–2 h before bed improves sleep onset** (evidence exists for warm baths generally); **citrus scent is relaxing**; **cold prevention: no specific evidence** — the winter bath habit and the vitamin-C association explain the proverb |
| **Water** | **Don't reuse for laundry or the next day** (oils, and the fruit softens and disintegrates); **don't reheat with the fruit in** (oils concentrate; the bath heater filter clogs) |
| **The tub** | **Rinse and wipe after** — yuzu oil leaves a film; **acrylic tubs can stain**; use the bath's cleaner |

- **Other seasonal baths**: **shōbu-yu (sweet-flag iris leaves) on 5 May (Children's Day)**, **momiji or mikan-peel baths** in autumn, **hinoki (cypress) blocks**. Sentō calendars list them.
- **Kabocha at the solstice**: the day's other custom — **eating pumpkin (kabocha) and foods with "n" sounds (nankin, ninjin, renkon, ginnan, kinkan, kanten, udon — the "seven n's")** for luck and vitamins through winter.
- **Children**: **fine for kids over about 1 with whole fruit**, supervised; **under 1, skip**; **extra care for toddlers — short soak and a plain-water rinse after**; **stop if skin reddens**.
- **The sentō visit**: **21 December is the busiest night of the year at neighbourhood bathhouses**; many give **yuzu to take home**. Bathe first, then soak; keep the fruit in the water, not on the floor (slip hazard).
- **Leftover yuzu**: the bath fruit is **not for eating** afterwards (soap, heat); buy eating yuzu separately for **yuzu-pon, marmalade, yuzu-kosho**.
