# Q. What do people get wrong about the gō, Japan's rice measure, and the rice-cooker cup?｜Japanese Everyday Wisdom #597

- Shelf: `wisdom` ／ URL: `/en/wisdom/the-go-rice-measure-and-cooker` ／ Status: **Draft — sources unverified**

Japanese rice recipes, rice cookers and rice bags all speak in gō (合) — 1 gō is 180 ml of raw rice, about 150 g — and the plastic cup in the rice-cooker box is exactly that, not a Western 240 ml cup. Newcomers who measure with a kitchen cup get mush or gravel; residents who never learned what a gō yields waste rice or run short.

## A. The common misunderstanding is that the rice-cooker cup is "a cup". It is one gō: 180 ml of dry rice, roughly 150 g, which cooks into about 330–350 g of rice — two small bowls, or a large one, or two onigiri. The cooker's inner-pot water lines are calibrated to that cup, so use it (or 180 ml, or weigh 150 g) and fill water to the matching line, adjusting slightly: a little less for new-crop rice in autumn, a little more for old rice or for softer results. The old Japanese units run 10 gō = 1 shō (1.8 L — the size of a large sake bottle) and 100 gō = 1 to (18 L), which is why rice bags are 5 kg or 10 kg but cooking is still counted in gō.

## The gō and what it yields

| Measure | Raw rice | Cooked rice | Serves |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1 gō (合)** | **180 ml ≈ 150 g** | **≈ 330–350 g** | 2 small bowls / 1 large / **2 onigiri** |
| **2 gō** | 360 ml ≈ 300 g | ≈ 660–700 g | 3–4 bowls |
| **3 gō** | 540 ml ≈ 450 g | ≈ 1 kg | 5–6 bowls (family dinner + tomorrow's bento) |
| **1 shō (升) = 10 gō** | 1.8 L ≈ 1.5 kg | — | The sake "isshōbin" bottle |
| **1 to (斗) = 100 gō** | 18 L | — | Historic |
| **5 kg bag** | ≈ **33 gō** | — | About a month for two adults at 1 gō/day |

## Using the cooker cup correctly

| Step | How |
|---|---|
| **Measure** | **Fill the plastic cup level** (scrape flat, don't pack). Or **180 ml** in a measuring jug, or **150 g** on a scale — weight is the most reliable |
| **Rinse** | **Quick swirl and pour off 3–4 times until water is mostly clear**; modern rice needs little "polishing" (see the rice-washing article) |
| **Water line** | The **inner pot's lines (1, 2, 3…) are for that cup**: **white rice line = about 1.2× the rice volume (180 ml rice → ~215 ml water)**. Separate lines for **sushi rice (less), brown rice (more), okayu (much more)** |
| **Adjust** | **New-crop rice (shinmai, Sept–Nov): a little below the line**; **old rice or very dry storage: a little above**; **firmer for sushi/onigiri, softer for kids/elderly** |
| **Soak** | **30 min (summer) to 60 min (winter)** in the water before pressing start improves texture; many cookers include soaking in the programme |
| **Rest and fluff** | **10–15 min after the beep**, then **fluff from the bottom with a wet shamoji** to release steam |
| **Cooker size** | **3-gō cooker** (1–2 people, up to 3 cups), **5.5-gō** (families — the Japanese standard), **1-shō (10-gō)** for big households. **Cook at 50–80% of capacity for the best result** |

- **Why 180 ml**: the **Edo-period gō was standardised as 1/10 of the shō (1.8039 L)**; the **rice ration and rice tax were measured in koku (石) = 10 to = 1,000 gō ≈ 180 L**, historically "one person's rice for a year". **Metric rules legally since 1959**, but rice, sake and cooker makers kept the gō for daily life.
- **Sake and the gō**: **a "one gō" (ichigō) is the standard sake serving (180 ml)**; the **tokkuri** carafe holds one or two gō; **"ni-gō" is a generous evening**.
- **Rice bags**: **2 kg, 5 kg, 10 kg**; **"musenmai" (pre-washed rice) needs slightly more water** — cookers have a musenmai line or you add 1–2 tablespoons per gō.
- **Leftover**: cooked rice per gō freezes as **two 170 g portions** in wrap — the household routine (see the cooked-rice article).
- **Buying a cooker**: **IH (induction) with pressure** for the best texture; **micom (microcomputer) heater types** are cheaper; **a 5.5-gō IH is the mainstream buy at ¥20,000–40,000**. Check the cup is included; replacements are ¥200–500 from the maker.
