# Q. What do people get wrong about the 5 pm chime and the neighbourhood loudspeakers?｜Japanese Everyday Wisdom #598

- Shelf: `wisdom` ／ URL: `/en/wisdom/the-five-oclock-chime-and-disaster-radio` ／ Status: **Draft — sources unverified**

Every day at 17:00 (16:30 in winter in some towns, 18:00 in summer in others), a melody — "Yūyake Koyake", "Greensleeves", the local school song — plays from poles across Japanese towns. Visitors think it is a curfew or a charming quirk. It is a daily test of the municipal disaster broadcasting system, and the same speakers will one day say something you need to understand.

## A. The common misunderstanding is that the chime is a "go home" signal for children. It is that too, informally, but its purpose is to test the bōsai gyōsei musen — the municipal disaster-administration radio system of outdoor loudspeakers and indoor receivers that carries tsunami warnings, evacuation orders, heat-stroke alerts, missing-person notices and, in rural areas, announcements about bear sightings and vaccination clinics. Learn what the chime sounds like in your town, and what the speakers sound like when it is not the chime: a siren followed by a spoken announcement means check your phone's area mail and the municipal website; a tsunami warning is a specific siren pattern; and the daily "children, go home" and "please look out for the elderly in the heat" messages are the system working as designed.

## The system

| Item | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Name** | **Bōsai gyōsei musen (防災行政無線)** — municipal disaster administration radio: **outdoor loudspeaker poles (屋外拡声子局)** and **indoor receivers (戸別受信機)** in many rural homes |
| **Coverage** | Nearly **all municipalities**; **~90% have the synchronous outdoor system**; dense cities rely more on phones |
| **The daily chime** | **A test broadcast** ("時報"), usually **17:00**, some towns **noon** and/or **morning**; **seasonal shifts (16:30 in winter, 18:00 in summer)**; **melodies chosen locally** — "Yūyake Koyake", "Furusato", Westminster chimes, the town song |
| **Informal function** | **"Children, go home"** — schools and parents use it as the outdoor play cut-off; **the elderly use it to close shutters** |
| **What else it carries** | **Tsunami/earthquake warnings**, **evacuation information (levels 3–5)**, **heavy-rain and typhoon notices**, **heat-stroke alerts** ("please drink water and use air conditioning"), **missing persons (esp. dementia wandering)**, **bear/wild boar sightings**, **planned power/water outages**, **vaccination and health-check reminders**, **the noon "lunch" chime**, **fire-drill announcements**, **New Year and Obon greetings** in small towns |
| **Sirens** | **Tsunami warning: a specific siren pattern** (e.g. 3-second blasts) followed by "**tsunami keihō**"; **evacuation: chime + spoken order**; **J-Alert (missile, large quake)**: the national alarm tone. The **patterns are printed in the municipal disaster guide** |
| **Can't hear it** | **Rain and wind swallow the sound**, and many complaints are that it's **too loud** at night — the reason municipalities also run **phone apps, area mail, a telephone playback number (テレホンサービス) and cable TV**. **Register for your town's email/LINE alerts** |

- **Why the melody**: to test the speakers **without alarming anyone**; the 17:00 timing dates from the **1960s–70s** and coincided with children's home time. Some towns **vote on the melody**.
- **Complaints and adjustments**: **noise complaints** (especially from new residents near a pole) versus **"we can't hear it" from the elderly**; many towns have **lowered volume, moved poles, or shifted the winter chime earlier**. **It is not switched off** — the test is the point.
- **The dialect and speed problem**: **spoken announcements are slow and formal** and often in the local accent; **foreign residents should register for multilingual alerts** (many municipalities offer **English/Chinese/Vietnamese** via app or email) rather than rely on the loudspeaker.
- **What to do when it isn't the chime**: **stop and listen for keywords — tsunami, hinan (evacuate), keihō (warning), kōzui (flood)**; **check the area-mail on your phone and the municipal site**; **look at what neighbours do**. **If in doubt on the coast after a strong quake: move to high ground without waiting for the announcement.**
- **The indoor receiver**: in rural houses a small box on the wall that **cannot be turned off** for emergency broadcasts; renters inherit it with the house — **don't unplug it**.
- **For visitors**: the chime is a **useful clock** and a signal that **parks and playgrounds empty**; if you hear a siren and spoken announcement in a coastal town, **ask someone "nani ga arimashita ka?" (what happened?)** and follow.
