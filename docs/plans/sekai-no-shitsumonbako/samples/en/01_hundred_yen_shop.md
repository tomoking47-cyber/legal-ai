Travellers to Japan often ask this before they arrive. The shops look like ordinary discount stores from the outside, so it is easy to walk past them — and easy to be overwhelmed once you are inside.

## A. Look for small tools that solve one specific problem. That is what these shops are actually good at.

Not souvenirs. Not bulk basics. **The things worth carrying home are the narrow, single-purpose items that quietly solve a small annoyance** — and that often do not exist in shops in other countries.

## Why these shops are different

### They are built around small problems, not low prices

Low prices are the obvious feature, but they are not the interesting one. What sets these shops apart is **how narrow their products are allowed to be.**

In most retail markets, a product has to appeal to a wide audience to earn shelf space. In Japan's 100-yen shops and home centres, an item that solves one small, specific irritation can still be worth stocking. So you find things like:

- A clip designed only to keep a half-used bag of snacks closed
- A drainer shaped for one particular corner of a kitchen sink
- A brush made for the gap between a window and its frame
- A case sized for exactly one pair of travel shoes

None of these are essential. Each of them removes one small daily friction.

### Why Japanese retail produces so many of them

Two conditions overlap.

**Homes are often compact.** When space is limited, storage and organisation become everyday problems rather than occasional ones. That creates steady demand for small solutions.

**Product cycles are fast.** These shops introduce new items frequently, which means a narrow idea can be tried without a large commitment. Ideas that work stay; ideas that do not, quietly disappear.

The result is a category of goods that is unusually **specific**.

### The same logic applies to Japanese home centres

Larger home-improvement stores in Japan follow the same pattern at a bigger scale. Alongside standard hardware, they carry a wide range of original items aimed at particular household situations. **Many visitors find these more interesting than souvenir shops** — because the items are things they will actually use after they get home.

## How to shop there without wasting time

| Instead of | Try |
|---|---|
| Browsing everything | Deciding on one or two categories first (kitchen, storage, stationery) |
| Buying because it is cheap | Asking "does this solve something that annoys me at home?" |
| Buying liquids and gels | Choosing solid, light, non-breakable items (easier to fly with) |
| Assuming everything is ¥100 | Checking the price tag — many shops now carry ¥300 and ¥500 lines |

### Three practical notes

1. **Prices are usually shown before consumption tax**, so the amount at the register may be slightly higher than the shelf label.
2. **Check your airline's rules** before buying anything liquid, aerosol or sharp. Scissors, knives and blades cannot go in hand luggage.
3. **Check your own country's import rules** for food items. Rules on meat, dairy, fresh produce and seeds vary widely, and we cannot advise on the regulations of your destination — **please confirm with the customs authority of the country you are returning to.**

## How we think about it at VV

We are less interested in "cheap Japan" than in something quieter: **the habit of treating a small daily annoyance as a problem worth solving properly.** That habit is not limited to one type of shop. It shows up across Japanese household goods, and it is one of the things we find most worth sharing.
